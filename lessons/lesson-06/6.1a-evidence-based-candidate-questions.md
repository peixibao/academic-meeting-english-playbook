# Lesson 06 · Topic-Specific Discussion

## 6.1a Evidence-Based Candidate Questions: Strengths, Weaknesses, Challenges, and Readiness

**Placement:** Immediately after 6.1 *Presenting Your Research Background and Academic Interests*, before 6.2.  
**Status:** Curriculum-audit supplement for review; archived on GitHub at the learner's request.  
**Goal:** Move beyond a neat self-introduction to credible, evidence-backed answers and follow-ups about readiness, experience, challenges, and behavior. This develops the shorter expressions introduced in 6.1 F2–F4 and 6.5 G6.

### A. What these questions are assessing

#### A1. Five question families

| Question family | Possible question | What your answer should establish |
|---|---|---|
| Strengths | “What would you say your strengths are?” | A relevant ability, evidence and limits |
| Weaknesses | “Where do you still need to improve?” | Accurate self-assessment and concrete progress |
| Setbacks | “Tell me about a time a project didn't go as planned.” | Judgment, accountability and learning |
| Collaboration | “How do you work with other researchers?” | Your contribution and communication choices |
| Readiness | “How has your background prepared you?” | Verifiable preparation for the specific next step |

These questions are not invitations to invent the ideal personality. Different selection panels may weigh evidence differently.

#### A2. A reliable evidence structure

Use **Claim → Evidence → Your action → Learning / relevance**, abbreviated **CEAL** here for study purposes:

1. **Claim:** Name one relevant strength or challenge.
2. **Evidence:** Describe a real situation, not a generic self-description.
3. **Your action:** Identify what **you** did.
4. **Learning/relevance:** Show why the example matters for research work now.

> “One strength I'd highlight is being careful with data quality. In a project I worked on, [ACTUAL PROBLEM] came up. My responsibility was [ACTUAL ACTION], which helped me understand why [REAL LESSON] matters for interpretation.”

A claim without evidence (*“I'm extremely hardworking”*) is easy to say and hard to assess.

#### A3. Multiple variants of the same question

- “What are your greatest strengths?”
- “What can you bring to this group?”
- “Why do you think you'd be a good fit for this project?”
- “What has prepared you for independent research?”

These overlap, but **strengths** asks about you, **contribution** asks about what you might bring, and **fit** adds the actual demands of the group or project.

### B. Answering questions about strengths

#### B1. Choose strengths you can demonstrate

Possible research-related qualities, **only if true**:

- Careful data handling, documentation or quality control.
- Curiosity and willingness to ask specific scientific questions.
- Explaining a result clearly to another researcher.
- Reliability in participant coordination or project administration.
- Learning a new analysis workflow or software with supervision.
- Listening well, clarifying uncertainty, and recording decisions.

Avoid choosing “leadership” simply because it sounds impressive. A concrete, modest example can carry more weight than an unsupported grand claim.

#### B2. Three natural answer frames

**Frame 1 — Direct and concise**

> “I'd say one of my strengths is [SKILL]. For example, when [REAL SITUATION], I [PERSONAL ACTION].”

**Frame 2 — Evidence first**

> “In one project, I had to [TASK]. That experience showed me that I'm good at [ACTUAL CAPABILITY], especially when [CONDITION].”

**Frame 3 — Match the group**

> “If your team needs someone who can [CONFIRMED PROJECT NEED], I could contribute [DEMONSTRATED ABILITY]. My direct experience is in [TRUE CONTEXT], so I'd still need to learn [NEW DOMAIN].”

#### B3. “I'm good at,” “I'm comfortable with,” “I'm proficient in”

| Phrase | Strength of claim | Example |
|---|---|---|
| “I've had some exposure to...” | Limited contact | “I've had some exposure to survival analysis.” |
| “I've used...” | Actual hands-on use | “I've used R for regression analyses.” |
| “I'm comfortable with...” | Practical confidence | “I'm comfortable with basic data cleaning tasks.” |
| “I'm proficient in...” | High competence | “I'm proficient in [TOOL]” only if the claim stands up to probing. |

“I'm familiar with” can describe knowledge without extensive independent use. Be precise about **what** you can do.

#### B4. After the professor asks for proof

**Professor:** “Can you give me a specific example?”  
**Student:** “Yes. In [TRUE PROJECT], the team needed to [TASK]. I personally handled [PART]. I checked [SPECIFIC CHECK] because [REASON].”  
**Professor:** “Was that your decision?”  
**Student:** “The overall analysis plan was agreed with the supervisor; I was responsible for implementing and checking that part.”

Accurate credit can increase trust.

### C. Discussing weaknesses and development needs

#### C1. Good weakness answers have three components

**Area → Current limitation → Concrete development plan**:

> “One area I'm still developing is [REAL SKILL]. I've had [ACTUAL EXPOSURE], but I don't yet have much experience with [SPECIFIC TASK]. I'm working on that by [REAL ACTION OR PLAUSIBLE NEXT STEP].”

Do not claim you have **already** begun a course or project if you have only considered it.

#### C2. What “weakness” can mean

- A technical skill you lack.
- A dimension of research independence you have not practiced much.
- A communication habit you are improving.
- A domain outside your current background.

It need not be a deeply personal confession. The interviewer is usually looking for awareness, trainability and appropriate judgment.

#### C3. Distinguish true weakness from a clichéd humblebrag

**Unconvincing:** “My weakness is that I'm too hardworking.”  
**More informative:** “I sometimes spend too long checking details before asking for feedback. I've been learning to clarify what level of checking is needed and to raise problems earlier.”

Use this only if true. A credible answer makes room for context and improvement rather than claiming the weakness is secretly a superpower.

#### C4. “I haven't yet” vs. “I can't”

- “I **haven't used** that method yet.” — No experience claimed.
- “I **haven't had the opportunity to use** it yet.” — Explains access, if true.
- “I **can't** use it.” — Current inability; can sound more categorical.
- “I'd need additional training before using it independently.” — Specific boundary.

Add *yet* only when development is realistically contemplated, not as empty optimism.

#### C5. When the weakness is central to the proposed project

**Professor:** “Our work requires experience with longitudinal models. Have you used them?”  
**Student:** “My hands-on experience has mainly involved [REAL METHODS]. I haven't independently fitted longitudinal models yet. I'd be interested in knowing which analyses a student would be expected to conduct and whether there is training or supervision for that part.”

This respects both your limits and the project's actual requirements.

### D. Research failures, setbacks, and constructive criticism

#### D1. “Failure” need not mean a disaster

In academic conversations, *failure*, *setback*, and *challenge* can describe different degrees of difficulty:

- A planned analysis became infeasible.
- A data-quality problem required rechecking earlier work.
- Recruitment was slower than expected.
- An initial interpretation turned out to be too strong.
- A manuscript received critical reviews or was rejected.

Avoid treating every unfinished task as a failure, but do not hide a real setback behind vague wording.

#### D2. A four-step narrative

**Situation → Complication → Response → Lesson**.

> “We initially planned to [TRUE PLAN]. However, [ACTUAL CONSTRAINT] meant that wasn't feasible. I [MY ROLE], and the team decided to [REAL RESPONSE]. The experience taught me to assess [SPECIFIC ISSUE] earlier.”

If the work is ongoing: “We haven't resolved the issue completely; the next step is [ACTUAL PLAN].”

#### D3. Responsibility language

- “I overlooked...” — Explicit personal mistake; use when accurate.
- “I realized that I'd misunderstood...” — Self-correction.
- “The team identified an issue with...” — Collective detection.
- “I raised the concern with my supervisor.” — Action and communication.
- “We revised the analysis plan.” — Collective change.
- “I wasn't involved in that decision.” — Clarify ownership.

Do not use passive voice to obscure your own error, but do not claim responsibility for decisions that were not yours.

#### D4. Criticism and disagreement

**Professor:** “What happened when someone disagreed with your approach?”  
**Student:** “We discussed what assumption the alternative approach would require. I checked [ACTUAL EVIDENCE], and we agreed on [REAL DECISION]. If the disagreement remained, I'd describe the alternatives rather than claim the issue had been settled.”

The point is to explain how you reasoned and communicated, not to portray the other person as unreasonable.

#### D5. Rejection is not the same as scientific invalidity

- “The manuscript was **rejected by the journal**.” — Editorial outcome.
- “The reviewers **raised concerns about** the analysis.” — Feedback.
- “We are **revising** the manuscript.” — Work in progress.

Never say a paper was accepted, published or resubmitted unless that actually happened.

### E. Independent work, teamwork, and conflict

#### E1. What “independent” really asks

- “How independently did you work?”
- “Could you take ownership of a task?”
- “Tell us about a situation where you sought help.”
- “How do you handle feedback?”

**Independent work** can mean carrying responsibility for a defined task while receiving guidance. It does not imply that you single-handedly designed and completed every study stage.

#### E2. Describe the division of labor

> “I was responsible for [TASK]. The supervisor advised on [DECISION], and the rest of the team handled [OTHER TASKS]. I worked independently on [MY SPECIFIC PART], but major interpretations were discussed collaboratively.”

Verbs that distinguish roles: **led, coordinated, drafted, implemented, checked, reviewed, assisted with, contributed to, consulted on**.

#### E3. “Led” vs. “coordinated” vs. “contributed to”

- **Led:** Had principal responsibility or direction for an activity.
- **Coordinated:** Organized components and communication; may not mean intellectual lead.
- **Contributed to:** Participated in a component without implying primary ownership.

If you prepared statistical code with oversight, “I conducted the analyses under supervision” may be accurate; “I led the entire project” might not be.

#### E4. Talk about conflict without gossip

**Question:** “Tell me about a disagreement within a team.”

A safe, substantive structure:

1. State the **work issue**, not personality judgments.
2. Explain what evidence or responsibility was unclear.
3. Describe your action and communication.
4. Say what was resolved and what remained unresolved.

> “We had different views about [WORK-RELATED ISSUE]. I suggested that we clarify [CRITERION / ROLE / EVIDENCE]. We then [ACTUAL STEP]. That experience reinforced the value of documenting decisions.”

Do not use a public portfolio or interview to share identifiable private disputes, confidential messages or accusations.

#### E5. Seeking help as good judgment

- “I tried [SPECIFIC STEPS] before bringing the issue to my supervisor.”
- “I realized the decision involved an assumption I hadn't verified.”
- “I sought feedback because the interpretation had implications beyond my role.”

Needing advice and having ownership are compatible.

### F. Transcript, grades, and academic readiness

#### F1. What the interviewer might ask

- “Your grades vary quite a bit across modules. Why is that?”
- “How have you prepared for the quantitative demands of the degree?”
- “Are there areas of your undergraduate training you'd want to strengthen?”
- “What evidence shows you're ready for research-based graduate study?”
- “How would you manage a demanding research workload?”

The correct response depends on your **actual academic record** and the actual program requirements. No explanation for a grade should be invented.

#### F2. A practical response sequence

**Acknowledge → Context if relevant and truthful → Evidence → Current plan**.

> “Yes, my transcript shows [ACCURATE PATTERN]. [TRUTHFUL RELEVANT CONTEXT, IF APPROPRIATE]. Since then, I've gained experience in [VERIFIABLE TASK / COURSE / PROJECT]. I also know I'll need to strengthen [SPECIFIC AREA] for this program.”

You do not need to disclose private personal history when it isn't necessary to answer the academic question.

#### F3. Answer what is asked

**Asked about a specific course:** Explain that course, not an unrelated award.  
**Asked about the overall GPA:** State it accurately using the official scale and available transcript; don't silently convert it into a different system.  
**Asked about quantitative readiness:** Discuss actual training and ability demonstrated in relevant work.

- “My university uses a five-point GPA scale.”
- “I'd be happy to clarify how the grading scale works.”
- “The official transcript reports [EXACT FACT].”

#### F4. Evidence of readiness

- Actual coursework in epidemiology, statistics or clinical sciences.
- Tasks you can show you performed, such as cleaning, checking or analyzing data.
- A reproducible coding sample **if your work really is reproducible and shareable**.
- A completed independent assignment, report, poster or conference presentation.
- A specific training plan for a genuine skill gap.

A published paper isn't the only form of research evidence; don't overstate an unfinished manuscript as one.

#### F5. Delicate follow-up

**Interviewer:** “Do you think you would struggle with advanced quantitative work?”  
**Student:** “I expect some parts to be challenging, particularly [ACTUAL AREA]. I don't want to claim a level of preparation I haven't reached. What I can point to is [ACTUAL EXPERIENCE], and I'm prepared to develop the additional skills required.”

This is more credible than reflexive “No problem at all.”

### G. “Why should we choose you?” and contribution evidence

#### G1. A four-part structure

**Need → Evidence → Contribution → Growth**.

> “From our conversation, it sounds as though the group values [ACTUAL PRIORITY]. I've worked on [RELEVANT REAL TASK], where I was responsible for [ROLE]. I could contribute experience with [SKILL], while developing [OTHER SKILL] through the project.”

You must know something about the group's needs before claiming you meet them.

#### G2. Distinguish the questions

| Question | Main focus |
|---|---|
| “Why this degree?” | Your training motivation; see 6.5 |
| “Why our program?” | Program-specific fit; see 6.5 |
| “Why should we choose you?” | Evidence you can contribute and succeed |
| “How would you contribute to my group?” | Group-relevant role and future work |
| “What are your strengths?” | A small number of demonstrated abilities |

One generic motivation speech cannot answer all five well.

#### G3. Avoid unsupported comparative claims

**Weak:** “I'm the best applicant because nobody works harder than I do.”  
**More defensible:** “I can give a concrete example of [CAPABILITY], and I think it could be useful for [PROJECT FEATURE].”

#### G4. Handle the professor's skepticism

**Professor:** “But we have candidates with more experience in this specialty.”  
**Student:** “That's understandable. My existing work has been in [TRUE AREA], so there would be a learning curve. What I could bring is [VERIFIABLE TRANSFERABLE SKILL], and I'd be keen to understand the training requirements for the specialty-specific work.”

No promise of acceptance or an invented advantage is implied.

### H. Ten branching interview situations

#### Scenario 1 — A vague strength is challenged

**Professor:** “What are your strengths?”  
**Student:** “I'd highlight careful data handling.”  
**Professor:** “Everyone says that. What did you actually do?”  
**Student:** “A concrete example would be [REAL QUALITY-CONTROL TASK]. I checked [WHAT], identified [IF TRUE], and documented [ACTUAL ACTION].”

#### Scenario 2 — You don't have an obvious weakness prepared

**Professor:** “Where would you need the most development?”  
**Student:** “I would need more hands-on experience with [METHOD / CONTEXT]. I've studied [TRUE FOUNDATION], but I haven't yet been responsible for [ADVANCED TASK].”

#### Scenario 3 — An analysis didn't work

**Professor:** “Tell me about something that went wrong.”  
**Student:** “We found that [CONSTRAINT] made our initial analysis plan unsuitable. I [MY ACTION], and we [ACTUAL CHANGE]. That taught me [LESSON].”

#### Scenario 4 — You are asked what you did personally

**Professor:** “When you say 'we', which part was yours?”  
**Student:** “I personally handled [SPECIFIC PART]. [OTHER DECISION] was made jointly with the supervisor.”

#### Scenario 5 — You've never used an advertised technique

**Professor:** “Can you work with [TOOL] independently?”  
**Student:** “I haven't used that tool independently, so I wouldn't want to suggest otherwise. My relevant experience is [REAL SKILL].”

#### Scenario 6 — Collaboration is questioned

**Professor:** “Do you prefer working alone?”  
**Student:** “I enjoy taking responsibility for defined analytical tasks, and I've also found supervisor feedback valuable when interpreting results. The balance depends on the type of work.”

#### Scenario 7 — A grade concern is raised

**Professor:** “Why is [COURSE / GRADE] lower than the rest?”  
**Student:** “That's a fair question. [TRUTHFUL EXPLANATION, IF KNOWN AND RELEVANT]. What I've done since then is [VERIFIABLE ACTION].”

#### Scenario 8 — The interviewer asks for a teamwork story

**Professor:** “Describe a time you worked with others under pressure.”  
**Student:** “During [TRUE PROJECT], we needed to [TASK]. My role was [RESPONSIBILITY]. I coordinated with [TEAM ROLES] by [ACTUAL METHOD], and the outcome was [TRUE OUTCOME].”

#### Scenario 9 — A rejection is mentioned

**Professor:** “Has any of your research been rejected?”  
**Student:** “The [MANUSCRIPT / ABSTRACT], if applicable, received [ACTUAL DECISION]. We reviewed the feedback, and [TRUE CURRENT STATUS]. I learned [REAL LESSON].”

#### Scenario 10 — The applicant is asked to compare themselves to others

**Professor:** “Why you rather than another applicant?”  
**Student:** “I can't evaluate other applicants' backgrounds. What I can speak to is [MY DEMONSTRATED EXPERIENCE], and how it relates to [GROUP PRIORITY].”

### I. Extended dialogue: evidence, challenge, and academic preparation

**Professor:** “You've mentioned observational research several times. What would you say your main strengths are?”  
**Student:** “One strength is working carefully through data preparation and analysis. In one project, I was responsible for [ACTUAL TASK], which required [CONCRETE PROCEDURE].”  
**Professor:** “Were you making the analysis decisions yourself?”  
**Student:** “Some implementation decisions were mine. The overarching model specification was discussed with my supervisor, and I wouldn't want to overstate my independence there.”  
**Professor:** “What did you find challenging?”  
**Student:** “One challenge was [TRUE DIFFICULTY]. Initially, we expected [REAL EXPECTATION], but [REAL CONSTRAINT] meant the approach needed to change.”  
**Professor:** “What did you do?”  
**Student:** “I [PERSONAL ACTION] and brought [SPECIFIC FINDING OR QUESTION] to the team. We then [TRUE DECISION].”  
**Professor:** “How has that changed the way you work?”  
**Student:** “I now pay more attention to [VERIFIABLE PRACTICE OR LESSON] before interpreting results.”  
**Professor:** “Some parts of your undergraduate preparation seem less strong. How would you handle advanced training?”  
**Student:** “I recognize that [ACTUAL AREA] would need more work. My existing experience with [REAL FOUNDATION] gives me a starting point. I would also want to understand the methods requirements so I could prepare in a focused way.”  
**Professor:** “What would you contribute to the group?”  
**Student:** “I could contribute the experience I've gained from [ACTUAL RESEARCH WORK]. I'd also like to learn how your group approaches [REAL PROJECT NEED] rather than assume my previous methods transfer without adjustment.”  
**Professor:** “That's useful. Any questions for me?”  
**Student:** “Yes. What kinds of research tasks do incoming students usually take responsibility for in their first term?”

**Conversation analysis:** The student offers a specific strength, survives evidence and ownership follow-ups, acknowledges a meaningful development need, and finishes with a question that helps assess the group's expectations.

### J. Phrase bank, grammar, and answer-quality checklist

#### J1. High-value language

| Function | Expression |
|---|---|
| Introduce a strength | “One strength I'd highlight is...” |
| Give proof | “A concrete example would be...” |
| Specify role | “I was personally responsible for...” |
| Clarify team decision | “That decision was made jointly with...” |
| Acknowledge limit | “I haven't used that method independently yet.” |
| Identify growth area | “One area I'm still developing is...” |
| Describe setback | “The original plan wasn't feasible because...” |
| Describe response | “I raised the issue with my supervisor and...” |
| Discuss disagreement | “We had different views about...” |
| Discuss criticism | “The feedback made me reconsider...” |
| Discuss grades | “My transcript shows [ACCURATE FACT].” |
| Link evidence to fit | “That experience would be relevant to...” |

#### J2. Grammar and usage

- **Responsible for + noun / -ing:** “I was responsible for cleaning the dataset.”
- **Involved in + noun / -ing:** “I was involved in preparing the report.”
- **Contributed to + noun / -ing:** “I contributed to developing the analysis plan.”
- **Experience in/with + noun / -ing:** “I have experience with data cleaning.”
- **Need to work on + noun / -ing:** “I need to work on presenting findings concisely.”
- **Comfortable with + noun / -ing:** “I'm comfortable with interpreting regression output.”
- **Learned from + noun:** “I learned from that setback.”

“**I am responsible for**” can describe a **current** role; “**I was responsible for**” describes a past responsibility. Avoid switching tenses without reason.

#### J3. Credibility checklist

- [ ] Every strength has at least one true example.
- [ ] The example distinguishes personal contribution from team decisions.
- [ ] The weakness is real and the improvement claim is accurate.
- [ ] The setback narrative states what happened without fabricating a happy ending.
- [ ] Grade and transcript statements use the official facts and grading scale.
- [ ] Unpublished work is described by its actual status.
- [ ] The answer can survive “What exactly did you do?” and “How do you know?”
- [ ] Personal or confidential project details have been removed where necessary.
