# Lesson 06 · Topic-Specific Discussion

## 6.5 Explaining Your Motivation: Why This Degree, Country, University, Program, and Supervisor?

**Learning objectives:** Answer motivation and academic-fit questions in a prospective-supervisor meeting; explain why you are considering an MPhil, research master's, or PhD; discuss country, university, program, and supervisor choices without generic flattery; address alternative routes and uncertainties; and keep the discussion natural, factual, and two-way.

**Scope note:** This is a targeted addition to Lesson 06, which already covers research background (6.1), explaining projects (6.2), discussing papers (6.3), and exploring research directions (6.4). Section 6.5 develops the previously missing **motivation questions**. It does not replace or reorder the fixed eight-unit course. Detailed bargaining over funding, roles, or commitments belongs to Lesson 07.

**Accuracy note:** MPhil, MSc by Research, MRes, and PhD structures vary by country and institution. None of the hypothetical examples below establishes a particular school's admission requirements, duration, funding, or progression policy. Verify the actual program before making claims in a real meeting.

### A. What professors are actually asking

The word **why** appears in many different questions, but these questions test different parts of your reasoning.

| Question | Main conversational purpose | What your answer should establish |
|---|---|---|
| “Why do you want to pursue graduate study?” | Broad motivation | What you want to develop or investigate |
| “Why an MPhil?” | Degree format and stage | Why substantial research training suits your current stage |
| “Why a PhD?” | Long-term research commitment | Why you may want sustained independent research |
| “Why not a taught master's?” | Comparison of training formats | Why research involvement matters to your goals |
| “Why not apply directly for a PhD?” | Sequencing and readiness | Why an intermediate route may be appropriate—or why direct entry might be worth considering |
| “Why this country?” | Academic environment | What genuinely attracts you to that location's training/research context |
| “Why this university?” | Institutional fit | Specific verified reasons for that institution |
| “Why this program?” | Curriculum and degree design | Program-specific features aligned with your aims |
| “Why my research group?” | Scientific fit | Overlap with a project, paper, research question, approach, or expertise |
| “Why should we consider you?” | Contribution and readiness | Your evidence-backed experience, skills, and learning potential |

#### A1. Different levels of “fit”

Imagine five concentric levels of specificity:

1. **Your purpose:** Why further study at all?
2. **Degree route:** Why an MPhil or PhD now?
3. **Country or region:** Why that academic environment?
4. **University and program:** Why that institution and degree structure?
5. **Research group and supervisor:** Why that work and academic relationship?

A professor may ask any of these independently. An answer to *Why this university?* consisting only of *“I want to do a PhD someday”* leaves the institutional-fit question unanswered.

#### A2. Don't mistake a motivation question for a demand for a life story

Professor: “Why are you interested in an MPhil?”

Overly broad answer: “I've always loved science, and I really want to become a better person.”

Focused answer: “I'm looking for a degree where research forms a substantial part of the training. I've worked on observational studies as an undergraduate, and I'd like to build on that experience by taking more ownership of a research project.”

A concrete reason, a link to your experience, and a forward-looking goal will usually serve the conversation better.

### B. Building an answer: reason, evidence, fit, direction

A flexible four-part framework:

**Reason → Evidence → Fit → Direction**

- **Reason:** What attracts you to the option?
- **Evidence:** What have you experienced or learned that supports that reason?
- **Fit:** Which verified aspect of the degree or environment connects with your aim?
- **Direction:** What would you hope to do or learn next?

You don't need all four in every short answer. For a casual interview, two or three meaningful sentences are often enough; expand if asked.

#### B1. Short answer (around 15–25 seconds at a normal speaking pace)

> “I'm interested in an MPhil because I'd like to gain more experience conducting research rather than primarily taking courses. My undergraduate projects gave me some exposure to the research process, and I want to develop greater independence.”

#### B2. Expanded answer (roughly 35–60 seconds, depending on pace)

> “Most of my research experience so far has been in observational studies. I've enjoyed working on the questions behind the analyses as well as interpreting the findings, and I've realized I'd like more sustained involvement in the research process. That's why a research-focused master's appeals to me. Ideally, I'd like to take more responsibility for a defined project and gain a better understanding of how research questions are developed and refined.”

#### B3. Don't deliver an entire script when the question is simple

Professor: “Why this program?”

A good opening could be:

> “The research component is what initially caught my attention.”

You can then explain the particular, verified feature. The professor may follow up, and the conversation can unfold naturally.

### C. Why MPhil? Why a research master's? Why not a taught MSc?

#### C1. Core language

- “I'm looking for **more sustained involvement in research**.”
- “What appeals to me is **the opportunity to work on a substantial research project**.”
- “I'd like to **build on my undergraduate research experience**.”
- “I want to develop **more independent research skills**.”
- “I'm particularly interested in **the research-focused structure of the degree**.”
- “I think this could be a good next step **at my current stage of training**.”

#### C2. A complete MPhil answer

Professor: “Why are you considering an MPhil?”

Student:

> “I'd like my next degree to involve meaningful research experience. I've worked on research projects as an undergraduate, but I'd like to become more independent in developing questions, working through analytical decisions, and interpreting results. A research-focused master's seems like a useful step toward that goal.”

This answers the **training-format** question without claiming a PhD decision has already been made.

#### C3. A question about the alternative

Professor: “Why not a taught master's?”

Student:

> “I can see the value of structured coursework, especially when it builds skills I need. At this stage, I'm particularly interested in applying those skills through sustained work on a research project. That's why the balance of research and coursework matters to me.”

This is respectful to coursework while communicating a genuine priority.

#### C4. Important degree labels

| Label | General meaning | What to verify |
|---|---|---|
| **MPhil** | Often a research-based master's; details vary | Research requirements; supervision; whether it is standalone or embedded in a doctoral route |
| **MSc by Research** | Research-oriented Master of Science | Thesis/research share, training, duration, structure |
| **MRes** | Master of Research; often research training | Balance of methods teaching, placements and thesis |
| **Taught MSc** | Predominantly course-based degree, sometimes with a dissertation | Actual research component, thesis supervision and placements |
| **PhD** | Doctoral program involving original research | Entry requirements, milestones, supervision, duration and funding |

Do not imply all MPhil programs have the same design or guaranteed PhD progression. In some institutions, *MPhil* has a different administrative meaning.

#### C5. Useful grammar: “appeal to,” “be drawn to,” “be interested in”

- “The research component **appeals to me**.” — It is attractive to me.
- “I'm **drawn to** the opportunity to work on a defined project.” — It strongly attracts my interest.
- “I'm **interested in** gaining more independent research experience.” — Neutral expression of interest.

**Grammar patterns:**

- **appeal to + person:** “The structure appeals to me,” not *“appeals me.”*
- **be drawn to + noun / -ing:** “I'm drawn to interdisciplinary research,” or “I'm drawn to working with real-world data.”
- **be interested in + noun / -ing:** “I'm interested in developing my skills,” not *“interested to developing.”*

### D. Why PhD? And why not a PhD immediately?

The long-term doctoral motivation question needs an answer about **sustained research**, **original contribution**, and **readiness for uncertainty**. A professor might ask about a future PhD even if you're applying for a master's.

#### D1. Interest in a future doctorate

> “I'm seriously considering a PhD because I find the process of investigating research questions rewarding, and I'd like to develop the ability to carry out more independent work. At the moment, I also want to gain enough experience to make an informed decision about that next step.”

**Seriously considering** expresses real interest without implying a final commitment.

#### D2. If you have firmly decided on doctoral research

> “I want to pursue a PhD because I'd like to investigate questions in greater depth and contribute original research. My previous projects have shown me how much I enjoy working through research design, analysis, and interpretation, and I want to develop the skills needed to lead an independent research project.”

Use this only if it genuinely reflects your decision. Avoid claiming you have always wanted this if that is untrue.

#### D3. Why an MPhil before a PhD?

Professor: “If you're considering a PhD, why not apply directly?”

Possible answer:

> “I'm open to direct PhD opportunities if the fit and entry requirements make sense. I'm also considering an MPhil because I'd value additional supervised research experience before committing to a longer doctoral project. I'd like to strengthen my skills and get a clearer idea of the research direction I want to pursue.”

A shorter version:

> “I see the MPhil as a possible way to develop greater independence and refine my research interests before making a longer-term commitment.”

These examples do not assert that every MPhil offers a formal transition to a PhD.

#### D4. Why a PhD rather than just research employment?

> “I'd like the opportunity to investigate a question in depth and develop the ability to make an original scholarly contribution. The structured supervision and sustained focus of a doctoral project are appealing to me.”

Avoid implying that all research jobs exclude original contributions. The difference between roles depends on the actual position.

#### D5. Degrees of certainty

| Expression | Commitment signaled |
|---|---|
| “I'm **curious about** pursuing a PhD.” | Exploratory |
| “It's **something I'm considering**.” | Undecided |
| “I'm **leaning toward** doctoral research.” | Provisional preference |
| “I'm **seriously considering** a PhD.” | Strong interest |
| “I'm **planning to apply** for PhD programs.” | Concrete intention to apply |
| “I'm **committed to pursuing** a PhD.” | Strong declared commitment |

These phrases must match your actual position. You can be strongly interested in research without declaring a lifelong academic career.

### E. Why this country or region?

A country-level question asks for an explanation of the **environment**, not an account of a university's ranking.

#### E1. Useful areas of comparison

- Research approaches and academic culture that are relevant to your interests.
- Opportunities for collaboration across disciplines or institutions.
- Program structures or research training that you have verified.
- Availability of relevant programs, groups, datasets, or clinical partnerships.
- Practical conditions that genuinely influence your decision, if appropriate.

Choose reasons you can substantiate. Avoid claiming that every university in a country offers the same conditions.

#### E2. Broad answer template

> “I've been looking at programs in [COUNTRY/REGION] because [GENUINE, VERIFIABLE REASON ABOUT THE ACADEMIC ENVIRONMENT]. I'm particularly interested in opportunities to work on [TYPE OF RESEARCH], and I'd like to understand how research students are trained and involved in projects there.”

#### E3. A hypothetical UK-oriented answer

> “I've been exploring research programs in the UK because I'm interested in the groups working in my area and the specific research-training opportunities I've found while looking at different universities. For me, the key consideration is the match between the project, the supervisor, and the kind of experience I'd gain.”

This does not pretend the country is uniformly superior. Before a real interview, replace it with a specific fact you've verified.

#### E4. “Why here instead of your home country?”

> “I'm interested in experiencing a different research environment and learning from groups working on questions that interest me. I'm looking at different countries and institutions, and the specific research fit is an important part of my decision.”

This is a professional, truthful **academic framing** when it reflects your motivation. If personal or other factors are important, you may decide how much to disclose; you don't have to invent an academic reason.

#### E5. Country vs. university

Weak answer to “Why the UK?”:

> “Because your university is highly ranked.”

This gives an institution-centered reason that barely explains the country choice.

Better structure:

> “The reason I began looking in the UK was [COUNTRY-LEVEL REASON]. The reason this particular university caught my attention was [UNIVERSITY/RESEARCH-SPECIFIC REASON].”

The two rationales connect without becoming identical.

### F. Why this university? Why this program? Why this supervisor?

These are three separate fit questions.

#### F1. University-level fit

> “What initially drew me to [UNIVERSITY] was [SPECIFIC, VERIFIED INSTITUTIONAL FEATURE]. As I looked more closely, I became interested in [RELEVANT RESEARCH ENVIRONMENT OR GROUP], which seems connected to the kind of work I'd like to explore.”

Relevant verified features might include an interdisciplinary research center, a particular research network, shared facilities, or a documented student-research opportunity. **Do not manufacture them.**

#### F2. Program-level fit

> “The research structure of [PROGRAM] particularly interests me. I'm looking for opportunities to develop greater independence through [VERIFIED THESIS/PROJECT/TRAINING COMPONENT], and that seems relevant to my current goals.”

Program fit concerns **the actual training design**. Check whether there is a thesis, placement, methods sequence, compulsory coursework, cohort or individual supervision, and how projects are allocated.

#### F3. Supervisor-level fit

> “I came across your work on [SPECIFIC TOPIC]. What interested me was [SPECIFIC QUESTION, DESIGN OR FINDING]. It connects with an issue I encountered in my own work, and I'd be interested in learning more about how your group is approaching it.”

This can lead naturally into the paper discussion taught in 6.3.

#### F4. Rank and reputation

“The university has a strong reputation” can be part of your motivation. On its own, it is often a generic answer that could apply to many institutions.

Compare:

- “I applied because the university is famous.” — Generic.
- “The university's reputation initially led me to explore it, but the particular research group and the structure of the program are what made me interested in applying.” — More informative *if true*.

You do not need to hide personal interest in institutional reputation; explain the actual professional fit as well.

#### F5. Contrast expressions and grammar

- “What **stood out to me** was the project's focus on [TOPIC].”
- “I was particularly **drawn to** [FEATURE].”
- “That **aligns with** my interest in [AREA].”
- “My experience **connects with** the group's work on [TOPIC].”
- “One **appealing aspect** of the program is [FEATURE].”
- “I've been **looking into** the work your group is doing.”

**Stand out to someone** = be especially noticeable or memorable.

**Align with** = fit or correspond with. It's a common professional expression, but too much *alignment* language can sound mechanical. Alternate with *connect with*, *relate to*, *interest me*, and specific descriptions.

**Look into** = investigate or examine: “I've been looking into several research programs.”

### G. Answering follow-up questions and avoiding weak claims

#### G1. “Why us rather than another university?”

> “I've been considering several programs, and each has different strengths. What interests me about this one in particular is [VERIFIED FEATURE]. That seems especially relevant to [GOAL OR PREVIOUS EXPERIENCE].”

There is no need to disparage competing institutions.

#### G2. “Have you applied anywhere else?”

> “Yes, I'm exploring a few programs that would offer relevant research training. I'm comparing them mainly in terms of research fit, training opportunities, and supervision.”

Or, if true:

> “I'm still researching my options and haven't finalized my applications yet.”

Be accurate; don't invent other applications.

#### G3. “Do you know what our program involves?”

If you've verified features:

> “From the information I've read, [VERIFIED FEATURE]. I'd be interested in how that works in practice, particularly for students working on [TYPE OF PROJECT].”

If you're uncertain:

> “I've read the program description, but I want to make sure I've understood the research component correctly. Could I ask how projects are usually selected?”

Never assert program rules you haven't confirmed.

#### G4. “Would you be interested if the project involved a different topic?”

> “I'd certainly be open to learning more. I'd want to understand the main research question, the day-to-day work, and what skills the project would involve before deciding whether it would be a good fit.”

This demonstrates curiosity while preserving your genuine preferences.

#### G5. “What if you don't get a PhD place afterward?”

> “I'd still value the research skills and experience gained through the degree. A PhD is an option I'm considering, and I'd use the experience to make a more informed decision about what to pursue next.”

Adapt to what you genuinely believe.

#### G6. “Why should we choose you?”

This question asks for a contribution-oriented answer, not a second generic motivation speech.

> “I can bring practical experience from observational research, including [ONLY TASKS YOU ACTUALLY DID]. Those projects have taught me to be careful about data quality and interpretation. I'm also aware that there are areas where I need further training, and I'd be motivated to develop those skills while contributing to a defined project.”

Tie abilities to evidence, distinguish prior experience from future potential, and avoid implying that a manuscript is published if it is under review or in preparation.

#### G7. Words to avoid using mechanically

- “Prestigious” repeated without any personal connection.
- “World-class” without evidence.
- “Perfect fit” when you still need to explore the project.
- “I've always dreamed of...” when your interest developed recently.
- “Guaranteed pathway to a PhD” without official confirmation.
- “I'm passionate about everything your group does.”

A convincing answer doesn't need dramatic adjectives.

### H. Natural questions and branching responses

#### H1. Why MPhil?

**Professor:** “Why are you interested in an MPhil?”  
**Student:** “I want my next degree to involve substantial research experience. My undergraduate projects helped me realize that I'd like more opportunities to work through the research process independently.”  
**Professor:** “And what would you most like to learn?”  
**Student:** “I'd like to become more confident in developing research questions and making analytical decisions, while still having guidance from an experienced research team.”

#### H2. Why not a taught master's?

**Professor:** “Have you considered a taught MSc?”  
**Student:** “Yes, I have. I think coursework can be valuable, and I'm looking at what each program actually offers. My main priority is meaningful research experience, so I'm particularly interested in programs with a substantial research component.”  
**Professor:** “That makes sense.”

#### H3. Why not direct PhD?

**Professor:** “If a PhD is your long-term goal, why take an MPhil first?”  
**Student:** “I'm open to different routes. I think additional supervised research experience could help me refine my interests and prepare for a longer doctoral project. I'd also be interested in understanding whether direct entry would be realistic in my situation.”  
**Professor:** “That would depend on the program and your preparation.”  
**Student:** “Of course. That's part of what I'm hoping to learn more about.”

#### H4. Why this country?

**Professor:** “Why are you looking at universities in our country?”  
**Student:** “I've been exploring several research environments internationally, and I'm interested in [REAL COUNTRY/REGION-SPECIFIC REASON]. What matters most to me is whether I can get the kind of research training I'm looking for.”  
**Professor:** “Are you considering programs elsewhere too?”  
**Student:** “Yes. I'm comparing opportunities across a few places, particularly in terms of research fit and program structure.”

#### H5. Why this university?

**Professor:** “What interested you in our university?”  
**Student:** “I initially looked into it because of [VERIFIED INSTITUTIONAL FEATURE]. Then I came across [SPECIFIC RESEARCH GROUP OR PROGRAM ELEMENT], and I thought it might connect well with the sort of work I'd like to explore.”  
**Professor:** “Which part in particular?”  
**Student:** “The work on [SPECIFIC RESEARCH QUESTION OR AREA]. I'd be interested in how students might get involved in those projects.”

#### H6. Why my group?

**Professor:** “What made you reach out to me?”  
**Student:** “I came across your work on [TOPIC]. I was particularly interested in [SPECIFIC DESIGN CHOICE / QUESTION]. It reminded me of an issue I encountered while working on [YOUR VERIFIED PROJECT], and I wanted to learn more about how your team approaches it.”  
**Professor:** “That's interesting. What issue did you encounter?”  
**Student:** “In that project, [BRIEF ACCURATE EXAMPLE]. It made me curious about [GROUNDED QUESTION].”

#### H7. You don't have a final research direction

**Professor:** “What's your exact topic for a PhD?”  
**Student:** “I haven't settled on a specific doctoral question yet. My current interests are in [GENUINE FIELD OR TYPE OF PROBLEM], and I'd like to develop a clearer direction through further research experience.”  
**Professor:** “So you're keeping your options open?”  
**Student:** “Yes, within a broad set of research areas. I'd like to understand the actual projects before narrowing that down.”

#### H8. A program feature turns out to be different from your assumption

**Student:** “I was interested in the opportunity to complete an independent thesis.”  
**Professor:** “Actually, in this program, most students work within a predefined project.”  
**Student:** “Thanks for clarifying. That could still be interesting. Would students have opportunities to shape a question or contribute to the analysis plan?”  
**Professor:** “It depends on the project.”  
**Student:** “I see. I'd be interested in understanding what that might look like in practice.”

### I. Extended realistic conversation: From motivation to research fit

**Professor:** “I can see from your CV that you've worked on a few research projects. Why are you interested in graduate study now?”  
**Student:** “Those projects gave me valuable experience, particularly with observational data. I'd like more sustained opportunities to develop research questions, work through analytical decisions, and understand how a project develops from the initial idea to its final interpretation.”  
**Professor:** “Why are you considering an MPhil specifically?”  
**Student:** “The research-focused structure is what appeals to me. At this stage, I'd particularly value a defined project where I could gradually take more responsibility and develop greater independence.”  
**Professor:** “And do you want to pursue a PhD afterward?”  
**Student:** “It's an option I'm seriously considering. I enjoy the process of investigating questions in depth, but I'd also like to use further research experience to refine the direction I want to pursue before making a longer-term commitment.”  
**Professor:** “Why are you looking at institutions outside your home country?”  
**Student:** “I'm interested in experiencing a different research environment and learning from groups working on questions that interest me. I'm comparing programs in several places, and research fit is a major factor.”  
**Professor:** “Why did this university catch your attention?”  
**Student:** “I started looking into [UNIVERSITY] because of [VERIFIED FEATURE]. I then became interested in [ACTUAL PROGRAM OR GROUP FEATURE], particularly the work on [TOPIC].”  
**Professor:** “Is there anything in my recent work that stood out to you?”  
**Student:** “Yes. In your paper on [TOPIC], I was interested in [SPECIFIC QUESTION, DESIGN DECISION OR RESULT]. I encountered a related issue in one of my projects and wondered how your group thinks about it.”  
**Professor:** “Could you tell me a little more about that issue?”  
**Student:** “Of course. [TWO OR THREE SENTENCES ABOUT AN ACTUAL PROJECT AND YOUR CONTRIBUTION.] What I found challenging was [REAL SCIENTIFIC OR ANALYTICAL ISSUE].”  
**Professor:** “That connects with some of our current work. Would you consider a project involving [OTHER TOPIC]?”  
**Student:** “I'd be open to hearing more. I'd want to understand the research question and what the work would involve, but it sounds worth exploring.”  
**Professor:** “Fair enough. Let me give you an overview.”  
**Student:** “That would be great. Thank you.”

#### I1. Why this dialogue works

- The student connects **motivation to actual undergraduate experience**.
- “I'm seriously considering” expresses doctoral interest without inventing certainty.
- The country, university, program, and supervisor reasons remain **different levels of specificity**.
- University-specific placeholders must be filled with **verified facts**.
- A question about another project receives an **exploratory answer**, not a promise.
- The conversation moves toward research questions, creating a bridge back to Sections 6.2–6.4.

### J. Quick reference, grammar notes, and reusable answer bank

#### J1. Fifteen high-utility expressions

| Function | Expression | Nuance |
|---|---|---|
| Explain training preference | “I'm looking for a more research-focused degree.” | Degree structure |
| Name next learning goal | “I'd like to develop greater research independence.” | Growth |
| Link to experience | “That experience made me interested in...” | Evidence-based motivation |
| Explain attraction | “What appeals to me is...” | Natural spoken opening |
| Emphasize feature | “What stood out to me was...” | Specificity |
| State current-stage fit | “At this stage, I'd particularly value...” | Timing |
| Express ambition tentatively | “A PhD is something I'm seriously considering.” | Honest uncertainty |
| Keep routes open | “I'm open to different routes.” | Flexibility |
| Avoid early commitment | “I'd like to learn more before deciding.” | Noncommitment |
| Explain research match | “That connects with work I've done on...” | Scientific link |
| Explain degree fit | “The research component is what initially caught my attention.” | Program-specific |
| Explain supervisor fit | “I came across your work on...” | Specific academic connection |
| Request project details | “What would that look like in practice?” | Ground abstraction |
| Describe independent work | “I'd like to take more ownership of a project.” | Responsibility |
| Invite correction | “Have I understood the program structure correctly?” | Check assumptions |

#### J2. Grammar and collocations to remember

1. **pursue a degree / pursue a PhD** — formal-neutral and appropriate in academic interviews.
2. **apply for** a PhD / **apply to** a university or program. Both *apply for a program* and *apply to a program* are used, with different emphasis.
3. **be interested in + noun / -ing:** “interested in pursuing a PhD.”
4. **be drawn to + noun / -ing:** “drawn to working on clinical questions.”
5. **appeal to someone:** “The program appeals to me.”
6. **align with / connect with + noun:** “The research aligns with my current interests.”
7. **build on + noun:** “build on my undergraduate experience.”
8. **be involved in + noun / -ing:** “involved in analyzing data.”
9. **take ownership of + noun:** “take ownership of a defined project.”
10. **contribute to + noun / -ing:** “contribute to study design.”
11. **look into + noun:** “I've been looking into the program.”
12. **consider + noun / -ing:** “I'm considering applying directly,” not *“consider to apply.”*

#### J3. Five answer patterns worth keeping

**Why MPhil?**

> “I'm looking for a more research-focused degree, and I'd like to build on my undergraduate experience by taking greater responsibility for a research project.”

**Why PhD?**

> “I'm interested in pursuing questions in greater depth and developing the ability to conduct independent original research.”

**Why this country?**

> “I've been looking at [COUNTRY/REGION] because [VERIFIED REASON]. I'm particularly interested in [GENUINE TRAINING OR RESEARCH GOAL].”

**Why this university/program?**

> “What caught my attention was [VERIFIED FEATURE]. It connects with [YOUR GOAL OR EXPERIENCE], and I'd like to learn more about [SPECIFIC ELEMENT].”

**Why this supervisor?**

> “Your work on [TOPIC] interested me, particularly [SPECIFIC ASPECT]. It relates to an issue I've encountered in [ACTUAL PROJECT], and I'd value the chance to understand your approach.”

#### J4. Core principles

1. **Answer the exact level of the question.** Degree, country, university, program, and supervisor each need a distinct rationale.
2. **Connect motivation to evidence.** Use authentic experiences and interests instead of generic adjectives.
3. **Use appropriate commitment language.** Considering a PhD and having decided on one are different claims.
4. **Don't confuse research skills with methodological research.** Using statistical methods to study health questions doesn't imply your research goal is to develop statistical methods.
5. **Check degree details.** MPhil, MSc by Research, MRes and PhD designs differ by institution.
6. **Don't invent institutional strengths or promises.** Verify course structures, supervisors, projects and funding separately.
7. **Give a concise initial answer, then expand.** A motivation interview should become a discussion, not a memorized speech.
8. **Don't imply a scholarship, PhD transfer, or placement is guaranteed.** Ask about them when appropriate.
9. **You can communicate genuine uncertainty.** Academic openness can be explained professionally.
10. **Leave negotiation for Lesson 07.** This section helps explain interest and fit; later lessons develop discussion of needs, constraints, terms and disagreement.
