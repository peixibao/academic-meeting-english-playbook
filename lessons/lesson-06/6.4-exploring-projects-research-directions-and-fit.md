# Lesson 06 · Topic-Specific Discussion (Replaceable)

## 6.4 Exploring Projects, Research Directions, and Scientific Fit

**Learning objectives:** Ask substantive questions about a potential supervisor's research; propose tentative questions without pretending to have a finalized thesis; connect existing skills to unfamiliar scientific fields; evaluate an idea's basic feasibility; and discuss research alignment without prematurely agreeing to a project or program.

**Boundary with Lesson 07:** Here we discuss *scientific content and possibilities*. Lesson 07 will focus on *negotiating expectations, boundaries, working arrangements, authorship discussions, practical constraints and disagreement*. Some requests overlap naturally, but the communication tasks differ.

**Examples are fictional and adaptable.** No sample project described here constitutes an actual vacancy, offer, supervisor commitment or verified institutional policy.

### A. Shifting from a professor's work to possible scientific directions

A first meeting may begin with your background or with the professor's current projects. You should be prepared for either sequence.

**A useful conversation pattern:**

1. **Learn about the project's question and setting.**
2. **Clarify what is already known or decided.**
3. **Identify a possible scientific connection.**
4. **Explore which questions remain open.**
5. **Discuss whether available data or designs could address them.**
6. **Leave room for further reflection or follow-up.**

**Example:**

Professor: “We're studying long-term outcomes after hospital discharge.”  
Student: “That's interesting. Are you focusing mainly on clinical outcomes, or also on patient-reported measures?”  
Professor: “Both, although the patient-reported data are still being collected.”  
Student: “I see. I'd be interested in understanding what kinds of questions the current data can support.”

Notice how the student learns more before proposing a detailed analysis.

#### A1. “Fit” has more than one meaning

- **Scientific fit:** Overlap between research questions and interests.
- **Training fit:** The skills you can develop in the research environment.
- **Project fit:** Whether your experience and available time suit a proposed project.
- **Supervision fit:** Whether expectations and working styles are compatible (Lesson 07).

An interesting paper alone does not establish that a supervisor has funding, a student opening or a suitable thesis project.

### B. Getting to know the professor's actual projects

#### B1. Questions about the research question

- “What are the main questions your group is working on at the moment?”
- “What originally led your team to that research question?”
- “Which aspects of the project are still open for investigation?”
- “Are there related questions that haven't been explored yet?”
- “What are the biggest unanswered questions in that area?”

#### B2. Questions about project stage

- “Is the project still in the planning stage?”
- “Has data collection already started?”
- “Is the analysis based primarily on an existing dataset?”
- “Are there any follow-up waves planned?”
- “How far along is the project?”

**How far along is... ?** = What stage has it reached?

> “How far along is the study?” — natural, conversational.

Compare:

> “Is the project underway?” — Has the project started?

> “Has recruitment wrapped up?” — Has recruitment finished? (*Wrap up* is a conversational phrasal verb.)

#### B3. Asking about the data and possible study designs

- “What kinds of data are currently available?”
- “Would the dataset allow you to examine change over time?”
- “Are the outcomes measured at multiple time points?”
- “Is there enough follow-up to investigate that question?”
- “What would be the main limitations of using the existing data?”

These questions explore scientific feasibility; they do not imply that you have already been given permission to access the dataset.

### C. Presenting possible research ideas without overselling them

#### C1. Interest is not a proposal

| Statement | Implication |
|---|---|
| “I'm interested in [FIELD].” | Broad orientation |
| “I'm curious about [QUESTION].” | Early research curiosity |
| “One possibility might be [APPROACH].” | Tentative idea |
| “A potential research question could be...” | More developed provisional idea |
| “I've prepared a formal proposal.” | A proposal actually exists |

A first meeting often aims to explore, not to secure immediate agreement on a thesis question.

#### C2. A practical idea structure

**Observation / gap → tentative question → possible approach → uncertainty.**

> “One question I wondered about is whether [EXPOSURE] is related to [OUTCOME] over time. If the dataset has repeated measurements, it might be possible to investigate that. I'd need to learn more about the variables and follow-up before knowing whether it's feasible.”

This is informed and open-ended. It avoids promising that the approach is definitely possible.

#### C3. Useful hedges for preliminary thinking

- “One possibility **might be**...”
- “I **wonder whether**...”
- “It **could be worth exploring**...”
- “I **haven't thought it through fully yet**, but...”
- “That **may depend on** the available data.”
- “I **would need to check** the assumptions first.”
- “I'm **still working out** what a feasible question might look like.”

**Think something through** = examine a question thoroughly, including implications.

> “I haven't thought the idea through in detail yet.”

**Work something out** = figure out or solve.

> “We're still working out the project scope.”

These expressions are useful when the idea is genuinely provisional.

### D. Evaluating feasibility scientifically

**Feasibility（可行性）** includes more than intellectual attractiveness.

#### D1. Common feasibility dimensions

| Dimension | Useful question |
|---|---|
| Data availability | “Do the relevant variables exist in the dataset?” |
| Measurement | “Are those outcomes measured consistently?” |
| Timing | “Are there data from enough time points?” |
| Sample size | “Would the sample be large enough for that analysis?” |
| Event count | “How many outcome events are available?” |
| Bias | “Would the design introduce any major selection issues?” |
| Resources | “What data collection or analysis work would be required?” |
| Ethics/access | “Would additional approvals or data-access arrangements be needed?” |

#### D2. Feasible vs. viable vs. practical

- **Feasible:** Can be done under available constraints.
- **Viable:** Capable of working successfully as an approach or plan.
- **Practical:** Reasonable and workable in reality.

Their usage overlaps:

> “That sounds interesting, but is it **feasible** with the data we have?”

> “We'd need to see whether that approach is **viable**.”

> “A secondary analysis may be more **practical** within the available time.”

#### D3. Answering “What would you do?”

Professor: “How would you approach that problem?”  
Student: “I'd probably start by clarifying the outcome definition and examining the available data. Then I'd consider what design or model best matches the question. I wouldn't want to choose a method before knowing more about the data structure.”

**I'd probably start by...** communicates a provisional workflow rather than a firm commitment.

#### D4. “Would it be possible to...”

> “Would it be possible to examine the outcome at multiple follow-up points?”

This asks about possibility, not permission to undertake the task. If you are asking whether *you* would be authorized to do it, make that separate: “Would students have access to those data?”

### E. Linking your experience to another field

The link may be a question, design, analytic skill, or shared scientific concern. It doesn't have to be the same disease area.

#### E1. Transferable experience

- “Although the clinical topic is different, I've worked with similar types of observational data.”
- “I haven't studied that condition specifically, but I'm familiar with some of the design issues.”
- “My background may be useful on the data analysis side, although I'd need to learn the disease-specific context.”
- “I'm interested in how the project connects clinical outcomes with population-level patterns.”
- “One thing I'd be interested in learning is how your group frames research questions in this field.”

#### E2. “Transferable” and “relevant”

**Transferable skills** = skills that can be applied in another project or context.

> “The statistical-analysis experience may be transferable to this project.”

**Relevant experience** = experience that directly matters for the proposed work.

> “My experience with survey data may be relevant to the questionnaire component.”

Avoid asserting that a skill transfers perfectly when the scientific assumptions or practical workflows are different.

#### E3. Showing openness without saying yes

- “I'd be interested in learning more about the project.”
- “It's an area I haven't worked in directly, but the questions sound interesting.”
- “I'd like to understand what the main day-to-day research tasks involve.”
- “I'm keeping an open mind about fields outside my current experience.”

Here, openness is a stance toward exploration, not an agreement to join a particular group.

### F. Asking about research opportunities at an appropriate stage

First establish an understanding of the *science*. Then ask about the *kinds of research experiences* that may be available.

#### F1. First-meeting questions

- “Are there any ongoing projects that might be suitable for an MSc thesis?”
- “Would there be opportunities for students to help refine a research question?”
- “Would the work involve mainly secondary analysis or also data collection?”
- “How do students usually become involved in the research process?”
- “Which scientific skills would a student be likely to develop in that project?”

#### F2. “Would there be opportunities...” vs. “Can I join...?”

**“Would there be opportunities...”** asks broadly about possibilities and is useful early in an exploratory meeting.

**“Can I join your group?”** is direct and sometimes appropriate, especially when the context is already a vacancy or formal application, but may press for a premature answer in an initial information-gathering conversation.

Neither phrase guarantees a supervisor can admit students to a specific program.

#### F3. Thesis, project, research placement and program

These are not interchangeable:

- **Thesis:** A substantial research document or research component within a degree.
- **Research project:** The investigation or activities the student works on.
- **Research placement:** A period of research experience within an organization or group; structure varies.
- **Degree program:** Formal academic program with its own admissions requirements.

A professor describing an interesting project doesn't automatically imply a thesis place or an offer of admission.

#### F4. Timing of sensitive questions

The fixed course's supervisor-question module recommends selecting **two or three relevant questions** for a first meeting and leaving other topics for later discussion. Publication potential, authorship, funding and detailed supervision terms may be relevant, but should arise when project context and roles are sufficiently clear. Lesson 07 will treat these questions as substantive negotiation and expectation-setting.

### G. Managing unexpected turns in research discussion

#### G1. The professor proposes a question you haven't considered

Professor: “What about a completely different population?”  
Student: “That's an interesting direction. I hadn't considered it before. Could I ask what motivates that population choice?”

#### G2. The professor says your idea is not feasible

Professor: “We don't have enough follow-up data to test that.”  
Student: “That makes sense. Perhaps the first step would be to understand what the dataset can support. Are there related questions that would be more feasible?”

Notice how the student follows the scientific constraint rather than defending the original idea reflexively.

#### G3. The professor asks whether you'd accept a project

Professor: “Would you want to work on that?”  
Student: “I'd be interested in learning more. I'd want to understand the scientific question, data and responsibilities before deciding whether it's a good fit.”

This preserves genuine autonomy. Detailed negotiation belongs to Lesson 07.

#### G4. You don't have a finalized thesis proposal

Professor: “Have you already developed a thesis proposal?”  
Student: “Not a finalized one. I have several broad interests and questions I'd like to explore. I'd be open to developing a feasible question in discussion with a supervisor, depending on the project and available data.”

“Not a finalized one” directly answers the question without inventing a proposal.

#### G5. The proposed field uses unfamiliar techniques

Professor: “Our group does a lot of machine learning work. Are you comfortable with that?”  
Student: “My hands-on experience has mostly been with [ACTUAL METHODS]. I haven't applied [UNFAMILIAR TECHNIQUE] directly. I'd be interested in knowing which parts of the project would require it and what training would be involved.”

Don't claim comfort or expertise solely to impress.

### H. Strategic question sequences and awkward situations

#### H1. A three-question sequence for a possible project

**1. Scientific purpose**  
“What are the main questions the project is trying to answer?”

**2. Evidence and feasibility**  
“What data are available to address those questions?”

**3. Student contribution**  
“Which aspects might an MSc student be able to contribute to?”

This develops from the science toward role opportunities, without beginning with a demand for a position.

#### H2. When you have read a paper but don't fully understand the project

> “I read your paper on [TOPIC], but I realize the current project may have evolved since then. Could you tell me how it builds on that earlier work?”

#### H3. When the supervisor's interests appear different from yours

> “My experience so far has been in a somewhat different area. I'd like to learn more about the kinds of questions your group investigates to see where there might be a useful connection.”

This is exploratory rather than pretending that every project is a perfect match.

#### H4. When the professor invites a speculative idea

> “As an initial thought, perhaps [IDEA]. I'm not sure yet whether the available data would support it, so I'd be interested in your view on its feasibility.”

#### H5. When the professor describes a highly specialized question

> “I haven't worked in that area before. Could I ask what the main scientific challenge is?”

#### H6. When two possible directions are discussed

> “Both directions raise interesting questions. The first seems more closely connected to my previous experience, while the second would take me into a new area. I'd want to learn more about each before comparing them seriously.”

### I. Extended two-way prospective-supervisor conversation

**Professor:** “Let me tell you a bit about the projects in our group.”  
**Student:** “Absolutely, I'd love to hear about them.”  
**Professor:** “One study examines long-term outcomes among patients receiving a particular treatment.”  
**Student:** “That's interesting. Is the main question about how outcomes change over time, or about differences between patient groups?”  
**Professor:** “Both, although we have better data for the between-group comparisons at the moment.”  
**Student:** “I see. Do you have measurements from multiple follow-up visits?”  
**Professor:** “Some participants have repeated measurements, but follow-up is incomplete.”  
**Student:** “That makes sense. I imagine the follow-up pattern would matter quite a bit for the analysis.”  
**Professor:** “Exactly. It's one of the issues we're working through.”  
**Student:** “Are there any specific questions that remain open?”  
**Professor:** “We're interested in whether baseline characteristics help explain differences in later outcomes.”  
**Student:** “That sounds like something I'd be interested in exploring. I've worked with observational data before, although the clinical field itself would be new to me.”  
**Professor:** “What kind of analysis have you done?”  
**Student:** “My experience has involved [REAL TECHNIQUES/EXAMPLES], particularly [MY ROLE]. I'd need to learn more about the specific data structure here, but some of the research-design questions sound familiar.”  
**Professor:** “Would you be interested in working on a thesis related to this?”  
**Student:** “I'd be interested in learning more. Could I ask what kinds of questions an MSc student might realistically take on, given the data available?”  
**Professor:** “There may be a focused secondary analysis, depending on the next phase.”  
**Student:** “I see. It would be helpful to understand what that analysis might involve and what stage the project is at.”  
**Professor:** “We can send you a short description and a couple of papers.”  
**Student:** “That would be very helpful. Thank you. I'd be glad to read them and think about the possible scientific connections.”

**Branch A — No projects available at present**  
Professor: “We don't currently have a suitable MSc project.”  
Student: “I understand. I appreciate you clarifying that. I'd still be interested in hearing your perspective on the field if you have a moment.”

**Branch B — Professor asks for an immediate idea**  
Professor: “Can you think of one question based on what I just described?”  
Student: “One question that comes to mind is [TENTATIVE QUESTION]. I'd need to look at the variables and follow-up patterns before knowing whether it's feasible.”

**Branch C — Professor discusses funding unexpectedly**  
Professor: “Funding is actually the main constraint.”  
Student: “I see. Could you clarify whether that's a project-specific constraint or a program requirement? I'd like to understand the context.”

**Branch D — Professor challenges the value of your background**  
Professor: “Your previous research isn't in this specialty. Why are you interested?”  
Student: “That's true; the specialty would be new to me. What interests me is [GENUINE QUESTION], and I've worked with some related research designs. I'd welcome the chance to understand the field before deciding whether I could contribute effectively.”

### J. Reusable expressions and decision checklist

| Purpose | Expression |
|---|---|
| Learn project purpose | “What are the main questions your team is investigating?” |
| Ask current status | “How far along is the project?” |
| Ask data availability | “What kinds of data are currently available?” |
| Ask follow-up structure | “Do you have repeated measurements?” |
| Explore a possible question | “One possibility might be...” |
| Hedge a tentative idea | “I haven't thought it through fully yet, but...” |
| Check feasibility | “Would that be feasible with the available data?” |
| Ask student opportunity | “Are there ongoing projects that might be suitable for an MSc thesis?” |
| Explore role | “Which aspects might a student contribute to?” |
| Connect distinct fields | “The disease area is new to me, but the study-design questions are familiar.” |
| Keep options open | “I'd be interested in learning more before deciding.” |
| Respond to no opening | “Thank you for clarifying the situation.” |
| Invite next information | “I'd be glad to read more about the project.” |

**Core principles:** Learn the problem before volunteering a solution. Distinguish scientific possibilities from confirmed opportunities. Discuss feasibility in relation to data, population, timing, measurement and constraints. Present questions as tentative when they are tentative. Use your real prior experience without claiming domain expertise you don't possess. Keep the dialogue two-way. Save detailed negotiation over working conditions and commitments for Lesson 07.
