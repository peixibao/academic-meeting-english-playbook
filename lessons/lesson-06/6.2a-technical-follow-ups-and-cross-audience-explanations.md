# Lesson 06 · Topic-Specific Discussion

## 6.2a Technical Follow-Ups and Explaining Research to Different Audiences

**Placement:** After 6.2 *Explaining a Study: Questions, Design, Findings, and Limitations* and before 6.3.  
**Status:** Curriculum-audit supplement for review; not yet uploaded to GitHub.  
**Purpose:** Handle a chain of technical questions and explain the same research accurately to specialist and nonspecialist listeners. The goal is to communicate the research you have genuinely done, not claim proficiency in every method mentioned here.

### A. What is a technical follow-up?

#### A1. The first question often leads to a harder second one

**First question:** “What kind of study was it?”  
**Follow-up:** “Why was that design appropriate?”  
**Next follow-up:** “What alternative explanation might account for your finding?”  
**Further follow-up:** “How could you test whether your conclusion depends on that assumption?”

Each question asks you to move from **description** to **justification**, **critical evaluation**, and **next steps**.

#### A2. Four functions to distinguish

| Function | Typical wording | What to answer |
|---|---|---|
| Describe | “What did you do?” | Accurate procedure and your role |
| Justify | “Why did you do it that way?” | Actual rationale and context |
| Evaluate | “What might be wrong with that?” | Limitations and alternatives |
| Extend | “What would you do next?” | A feasible, qualified next step |

**Important:** If a design or model was selected before you joined the project, you may explain its technical rationale as an interpretation, while stating you did not make the original choice.

#### A3. A four-step answer pattern

**Decision → Reason → Trade-off → Boundary**:

> “We used [METHOD]. The main reason was [REAL RATIONALE]. That helped us [PURPOSE], although it didn't address [LIMITATION].”

If the rationale is not known firsthand:

> “I wasn't involved in the original design decision, but my understanding is that [REASON]. I'd want to confirm that with the study team.”

### B. Explaining study design and alternative approaches

#### B1. Why a cross-sectional design?

> “The study used cross-sectional data, which allowed us to examine the relationship between variables at one point in time. It was useful for describing patterns in this sample, but it couldn't establish temporal order.”

**Likely follow-up:** “So can you make causal claims?”  
**Answer:** “The observed association alone wouldn't justify a causal conclusion. We'd need stronger design information and assumptions before interpreting it causally.”

#### B2. “Why not a cohort study?”

> “A longitudinal design could help establish how variables change over time and clarify their temporal sequence. Whether that would be feasible would depend on follow-up data, resources, and the actual research question.”

Avoid: “A cohort study would definitely prove causality.” Temporal order is useful, but confounding and other biases remain possible.

#### B3. Choosing outcomes and exposures

- “How was the outcome defined?”
- “Was that threshold prespecified?”
- “Was it based on a clinical guideline or a data-driven cut point?”
- “Would your conclusion change with another plausible definition?”

**Answer frame:**

> “We defined the outcome as [TRUE DEFINITION]. The reason for using that definition was [ACTUAL BASIS]. We also considered [ALTERNATIVE] if [TRUE]. One concern is [LIMITATION].”

An arbitrary threshold should not be described as clinically validated without evidence.

#### B4. “Why this population?”

> “The available population reflected [TRUE RECRUITMENT CONTEXT]. That is relevant to [QUESTION], although the sampling method limits how widely we can generalize.”

Distinguish **target population（目标总体）**, **source population（来源总体）**, and **analytic sample（分析样本）** when relevant.

### C. Explaining adjustment, confounding, and model choices

#### C1. Confounding in plain language

A **confounder（混杂因素）** may distort the observed relationship between an exposure and outcome if it is related to both in a causally relevant way. A good explanation depends on the actual study and assumptions; statistical significance alone is not a sound variable-selection rule.

> “We adjusted for [COVARIATES] to account for potential confounding, though adjustment cannot guarantee that all confounding has been eliminated.”

**Why those variables?**

> “They were selected based on [ACTUAL SCIENTIFIC RATIONALE / PREDEFINED PLAN]. I would be cautious about claiming that they fully control for confounding.”

#### C2. Adjust for / control for / account for

- “We **adjusted for** age and sex in the model.” — Specific model adjustment.
- “We **attempted to control for** measured confounders.” — More cautious when assumptions remain.
- “We **accounted for** the study design when calculating uncertainty.” — Could refer to design handling.

Don't say you “controlled for everything” simply because several covariates were in the model.

#### C3. Why regression?

> “The regression model allowed us to assess the association while including several covariates. The appropriate model depends on the outcome type, data structure, assumptions and estimand.”

Examples for explanation only:

- Binary outcome → logistic regression can be appropriate, depending on the target measure and context.
- Continuous outcome → linear regression can be suitable if its assumptions and goal are appropriate.
- Repeated observations → dependence between observations needs consideration; simply treating all observations as independent may be unsuitable.

These are categories, not a substitute for actual model checking.

#### C4. “Could you have used a different model?”

> “Possibly. One alternative might be [METHOD], depending on the question and outcome structure. I'd compare its assumptions and interpretability with the original approach before saying it was better.”

A proposed alternative is useful only if you understand what problem it solves.

#### C5. Handling unfamiliar methods

> “I haven't applied that technique myself, so I wouldn't want to overstate my knowledge. Could I explain how we addressed the problem in the analysis we actually conducted?”

This is an informed scope boundary, not a refusal to engage.

### D. Missing data, bias, and interpretation

#### D1. Different missing-data questions

- “How much data were missing?” — Quantify, if known.
- “Were missing outcomes concentrated in one group?” — Distribution of missingness.
- “Did you conduct a complete-case analysis?” — Analytical choice.
- “Did you consider multiple imputation?” — Alternative method.
- “What assumptions would that require?” — Underlying missingness mechanism and imputation model.

A generic answer like “We removed the missing values” may omit major consequences.

#### D2. MCAR, MAR, MNAR — when to use the labels

- **MCAR (missing completely at random):** Missingness independent of observed and unobserved data in the relevant setting.
- **MAR (missing at random):** Missingness can be explained by observed information, conditional on the analysis assumptions.
- **MNAR (missing not at random):** Missingness may depend on unobserved values even after conditioning on observed information.

These are conceptual mechanisms, not labels you can reliably infer from a single missingness count. Do not claim “our data were MAR” unless you have a defensible basis; MAR is generally an assumption that cannot be proven from observed data alone.

#### D3. Careful answer about missingness

> “We used [ACTUAL APPROACH] for the primary analysis. One concern is that the missing data might not be random with respect to [RELEVANT FACTOR]. We [DID / DID NOT] carry out a sensitivity analysis, so that remains a limitation.”

#### D4. Potential sources of bias

| Bias category | Question | Cautious language |
|---|---|---|
| Selection bias | Who enters the sample or analysis? | “Selection into the sample could affect...” |
| Information/measurement bias | Were variables measured accurately? | “Misclassification of the outcome is possible if...” |
| Confounding | Is another factor shaping the observed relationship? | “Residual confounding may remain.” |
| Reverse causation | Could the outcome influence the exposure? | “The temporal sequence is unclear.” |

Do not call every flaw “selection bias.” Name the mechanism when known.

#### D5. Generalizability vs. internal validity

**Internal validity（内部效度）** concerns whether an inference is credible for the studied setting given the design and bias structure.  
**Generalizability（外推性）** concerns application to other populations or settings.

> “The finding may be relevant to similar populations, but we should be cautious about extending it beyond the sampled setting.”

### E. Sensitivity analyses and challenging results

#### E1. Why a sensitivity analysis?

A **sensitivity analysis（敏感性分析）** examines how an inference changes under reasonable alternative definitions, assumptions, data subsets or analytical choices.

> “We examined whether the direction of the association changed when we used [ACTUAL ALTERNATIVE]. That helps assess how dependent the conclusion is on that analytical choice.”

Don't call an analysis a robustness check unless you really performed it and can explain what varied.

#### E2. “Was it statistically significant?”

> “The estimate was [ACTUAL ESTIMATE], with [REPORTED UNCERTAINTY]. The interpretation depends on both the size and precision of the association, not only whether the p-value crossed a threshold.”

**Statistical significance** and **clinical/practical importance** answer different questions. An inconclusive result is not proof of no association.

#### E3. Multiple testing and exploratory findings

> “That was an exploratory analysis, so I'd interpret the result cautiously, especially given the number of comparisons.”

Use **prespecified** only when the decision was actually made in advance. Use **exploratory** for analyses developed after looking at data, where appropriate.

#### E4. When asked “Did you expect that result?”

> “It differed from our initial expectation. We'd want to consider chance variation, potential biases and alternative explanations before building a strong interpretation around it.”

Avoid retrospectively claiming a surprising result was always predicted.

#### E5. When a professor points out a flaw

**Professor:** “Wouldn't the small number of events make that model unstable?”  
**Student:** “That's a valid concern. The number of events affects what the model can support. I'd want to inspect the event count, model complexity and uncertainty before drawing conclusions.”

You can recognize a concern without inventing what the team has already checked.

### F. Translating specialist research for other audiences

#### F1. One study, three levels of explanation

Consider this **fictional teaching example**: an observational analysis of sleep patterns and blood pressure. All results are omitted on purpose.

**General audience, about 20–30 seconds:**

> “We looked at whether teenagers with different sleep patterns also differed in their blood-pressure measurements. The goal was to understand a possible relationship that might deserve further study. Because the data were observational, the results couldn't tell us whether one caused the other.”

**Interdisciplinary panel, about 45–60 seconds:**

> “Our project examined the relationship between sleep-related characteristics and blood pressure among adolescents. We analyzed observational data and considered several characteristics that might affect the relationship. The key challenge was interpreting associations carefully, because study design and measurement limitations can affect the conclusions.”

**Specialist colleague, when asked for methods:**

> “We evaluated the association between [EXPOSURE] and [OUTCOME] using [ACTUAL MODEL], adjusting for [PRESPECIFIED OR JUSTIFIED COVARIATES]. The interpretation is subject to [ACTUAL BIAS / PRECISION / MISSINGNESS CONSTRAINT].”

None of these is an actual user study report. Replace variables, methods, and roles with verified facts only.

#### F2. “In plain language” vs. “In simple terms”

- “**In plain language**, we're asking...” — Reader/listener-friendly explanation.
- “**Put simply**, the study looks at...” — Concise reformulation.
- “**At a high level**, the key issue is...” — Big-picture orientation.
- “**To put it another way**, we're looking for...” — Restatement after misunderstanding.

Avoid “It's really simple” when the topic is genuinely complex; it can sound dismissive.

#### F3. Explain terminology without losing precision

| Technical term | One accessible explanation |
|---|---|
| Observational study | Researchers examine patterns without assigning the exposure as in a randomized trial |
| Confounding | Another relevant factor can distort the apparent relationship |
| Missing data | Some required measurements are unavailable for some participants |
| Association | Variables vary together in the observed data; causal direction not established |
| Confidence interval | An interval calculated by a statistical procedure to express estimate uncertainty, with interpretation depending on the method's assumptions |
| Sensitivity analysis | Check how conclusions change under reasonable alternative choices |

A good explanation usually provides the intuitive meaning **and** one limitation, rather than replacing every concept with an analogy.

#### F4. Why the research matters to a nonspecialist

> “This question matters because [REAL CONSEQUENCE]. If future studies establish [FURTHER EVIDENCE NEEDED], the findings could eventually inform [PLAUSIBLE APPLICATION]. At this stage, our study only addresses [CURRENT QUESTION].”

Do not assert a clinical policy implication from an association that has not been established as causal or clinically actionable.

#### F5. Funding panel vs. faculty discussion

A panelist might ask “Who benefits?” while a methods-focused supervisor asks “What's the estimand?” Both are valid but require different answers. Use the **same verified scientific facts** and change only the order, detail and vocabulary.

### G. Answering unexpected methodological questions without bluffing

#### G1. Distinguish four states

1. **I know the answer firsthand.** Give the actual answer.
2. **I understand the issue but didn't make the decision.** Explain carefully, credit the decision-maker.
3. **I have a plausible hypothesis but haven't verified it.** Mark it explicitly as tentative.
4. **I don't know enough to answer responsibly.** Say so, then specify what information would help.

#### G2. A response ladder

- “That's something we considered because...”
- “My understanding is that...”
- “One possible explanation is..., although I'd need to check...”
- “I wasn't involved in that decision, so I don't want to guess.”
- “I'd need to inspect [DATA/PROTOCOL] before giving a confident answer.”

#### G3. “I'd need to check” vs. “I'll check”

**I'd need to check** states an epistemic requirement.  
**I'll check** commits you to future action. In an interview, prefer the former if no follow-up task has been agreed.

#### G4. Keep the conversation going after uncertainty

**Professor:** “Was the model specified before you examined the data?”  
**Student:** “I don't know the exact timeline for that decision. I joined after the initial protocol was developed. I can explain how the analysis was implemented, and I would need to consult the protocol to answer the prespecification question.”

This avoids both false certainty and abrupt withdrawal.

### H. Ten follow-up scenarios with branching replies

#### Scenario 1 — Why the design?

**Professor:** “Why cross-sectional?”  
**Student:** “It allowed the team to examine a pattern in the available data. The limitation is that we couldn't establish which variable came first.”  
**Professor:** “Would a cohort design solve that?”  
**Student:** “It could help with temporal ordering, but we'd still need to consider confounding, retention and measurement.”

#### Scenario 2 — Why those covariates?

**Professor:** “Why adjust for age?”  
**Student:** “That was part of the adjustment strategy. The substantive rationale would need to reflect the causal assumptions and study question; I don't want to invent a rationale the team never used.”

#### Scenario 3 — Missing outcomes

**Professor:** “How many observations were excluded?”  
**Student:** “I don't have the verified count with me. I would need to check the final analysis dataset rather than give you an approximate number.”

#### Scenario 4 — Surprise null finding

**Professor:** “Did that prove there was no relationship?”  
**Student:** “No. The analysis didn't provide clear evidence of an association under that model; that doesn't establish that the true relationship is exactly zero.”

#### Scenario 5 — Multiple comparisons

**Professor:** “Was this planned?”  
**Student:** “It was exploratory, and I would treat the finding as hypothesis-generating rather than definitive.”

#### Scenario 6 — Reverse causation

**Professor:** “Could the outcome affect the exposure?”  
**Student:** “It's possible. Given the design, we couldn't confidently distinguish that direction from the alternative.”

#### Scenario 7 — Small sample

**Professor:** “Your subgroup is small. How reliable is that comparison?”  
**Student:** “I'd be cautious about precision and model stability. I'd want to examine the actual event counts and uncertainty before making a strong claim.”

#### Scenario 8 — Specialist method you've never used

**Professor:** “Why not apply [UNFAMILIAR METHOD]?”  
**Student:** “I'm aware of it at a general level but haven't applied it myself. Could I ask what advantage you'd expect it to offer for this question?”

#### Scenario 9 — Nonspecialist asks “So what?”

**Panelist:** “Why should the public care?”  
**Student:** “The broader concern is [ACTUAL HEALTH OR SCIENTIFIC QUESTION]. Our study contributes information about [LIMITED OBSERVATIONAL PATTERN], which could help shape more rigorous future research.”

#### Scenario 10 — Two interviewers disagree about the inference

**Interviewer A:** “It sounds promising.”  
**Interviewer B:** “But the design doesn't support a causal claim.”  
**Student:** “I agree that causal interpretation would be too strong. The contribution we can defend is [ACTUAL ASSOCIATIONAL OR DESCRIPTIVE CLAIM].”

### I. Extended dialogue: one project, two audiences

**Professor:** “Could you summarize your project?”  
**Student:** “We investigated whether [EXPOSURE] was associated with [OUTCOME] in [POPULATION]. The analysis was observational, and my role involved [ACTUAL TASK].”  
**Professor:** “Why use that model?”  
**Student:** “The outcome was [TRUE TYPE], and the model was chosen to address [ACTUAL QUESTION] while accounting for [ACTUAL COVARIATES].”  
**Professor:** “How did you deal with missing data?”  
**Student:** “We [ACTUAL PROCEDURE]. The key limitation is [KNOWN UNCERTAINTY]. I wouldn't claim we removed the possibility of missing-data bias.”  
**Professor:** “Would you choose the same approach again?”  
**Student:** “I'd first revisit the question and the data structure. If [CONDITION] were available, I'd consider [DEFENSIBLE ALTERNATIVE]; otherwise I'd focus on communicating the original design's limits.”

**Panelist from another discipline joins:**

**Panelist:** “Could you explain what the project is about without using statistical terms?”  
**Student:** “Of course. In plain language, we wanted to find out whether [EVERYDAY DESCRIPTION OF VARIABLES] tend to occur together. We studied existing observations, so we could describe a pattern but couldn't say that one causes the other.”  
**Panelist:** “What would be useful about finding that out?”  
**Student:** “It could help identify questions worth testing more carefully in future studies, especially if the pattern appears consistently and has a plausible clinical or public-health explanation.”  
**Professor:** “And what would the main next step be?”  
**Student:** “It would depend on data availability. I'd first check whether [FOLLOW-UP OR BETTER MEASUREMENT] exists, then refine the question accordingly.”

**Analysis:** The student changes vocabulary, answer length and emphasis, but the strength of the evidence does not change across audiences.

### J. Functional language and scientific accuracy checklist

#### J1. High-frequency technical follow-up phrases

| Purpose | Expression |
|---|---|
| Explain choice | “We chose that approach because...” |
| Explain trade-off | “The advantage is..., although the limitation is...” |
| Present a caveat | “That interpretation depends on...” |
| Identify alternative | “One alternative explanation would be...” |
| Discuss confounding | “Residual confounding may still be present.” |
| Discuss missingness | “Missingness could affect the interpretation if...” |
| Explain uncertainty | “The estimate was imprecise because...” |
| Distinguish significance | “Statistical significance doesn't necessarily imply clinical importance.” |
| Explain to nonspecialist | “In plain language, the question is...” |
| Suggest next step | “One way to investigate that further would be...” |
| Avoid false certainty | “I'd need to check the protocol to answer that accurately.” |
| State role limit | “I implemented the analysis but wasn't involved in the initial design decision.” |

#### J2. Useful verb and noun patterns

- “**Account for** potential confounders.”
- “**Adjust for** measured covariates.”
- “**Be associated with** an outcome.”
- “**Depend on** assumptions.”
- “**Be robust to** reasonable changes in specification.” — Only if evidence supports it.
- “**Be sensitive to** the threshold used.”
- “**Generalize to** another population.”
- “**Draw conclusions from** observational data.”
- “**Distinguish between** association and causation.”

#### J3. Pre-interview integrity checklist

- [ ] I can explain why the chosen study design fits the actual question.
- [ ] I can name at least one meaningful limitation and an alternative explanation.
- [ ] I know which methodological decisions were mine.
- [ ] I can distinguish outcomes, covariates, bias, confounding and missingness.
- [ ] I won't invent effect sizes, sample counts, p-values or model names.
- [ ] I can explain my research to a nonspecialist without misrepresenting causal conclusions.
- [ ] I can handle at least two levels of technical follow-up.
- [ ] I can acknowledge unknowns without promising a specific analysis I cannot perform.
