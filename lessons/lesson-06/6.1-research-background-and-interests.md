# Lesson 06 · Topic-Specific Discussion (Replaceable)

## 6.1 Presenting Your Research Background and Academic Interests

**Learning objectives:** Give a flexible research introduction; answer follow-up questions about projects and skills; separate your actual responsibilities from a team's achievements; describe evolving scientific interests without making unearned commitments; and make an introduction sound like a conversation rather than a memorized CV.

**Scope:** This is the *prospective-supervisor / research-meeting* version of the replaceable Lesson 06 topic module. The conversation strategies transfer to other subjects later. All project details in the sample dialogues are fictional illustrations, not statements about the learner's record.

### A. The communicative job: helping someone locate you academically

A useful research introduction lets the other person answer four questions:

1. **What are you studying or trained in?** — Degree, discipline, or relevant academic preparation.
2. **What have you actually worked on?** — Research types, topics and study designs.
3. **What did you do?** — Your own role, not merely the project team's work.
4. **What are you interested in exploring next?** — Current interests or questions, without pretending that your path is already fixed.

**Short example:**

> “I'm currently completing a degree in preventive medicine. Most of my research experience so far has involved observational studies, particularly projects related to clinical and population-health questions. I've worked on data analysis and interpreting findings, and I'm interested in building on that experience through more substantial research training.”

The wording is a *template*. Keep only clauses that describe your real experience. It is usually better to answer the question asked than to present a full autobiography.

#### A1. Introduction vs. explanation vs. discussion

- **Introduction:** “My experience has mainly been in observational epidemiology.”
- **Explanation:** “For one project, I helped analyze data from a cross-sectional study.”
- **Discussion:** “That experience made me curious about how a longitudinal design could address a related question.”

A supervisor can invite you to move from one layer to the next. You do not have to deliver all three at once.

#### A2. Brief answer plus optional expansion

> “I can give you a quick overview, and I'm happy to go into any of the projects in more detail.”

This is a useful first-meeting strategy because the professor can choose the depth and direction.

### B. Three adaptable introduction lengths

#### B1. The 15–20-second orientation

> “I'm currently studying [DEGREE/FIELD] at [UNIVERSITY]. I've mainly worked on [BROAD RESEARCH TYPE], and I'm particularly interested in [CURRENT AREA OR QUESTION].”

Use it when the other person asks: “Could you briefly introduce yourself?” or time is limited.

#### B2. The 30–45-second overview

> “I'm in the final year of [DEGREE]. My research experience has mainly involved [STUDY TYPE], including work on [TOPIC]. In those projects, I was involved in [VERIFIABLE RESPONSIBILITIES]. That experience got me interested in [FIELD OR TYPE OF QUESTION], and I'd like to gain more independent research experience.”

This adds an actual role and a forward-looking connection.

#### B3. The 60–90-second version

> “My academic background is in [FIELD], which has given me exposure to [RELEVANT AREAS]. I've worked on a few projects involving [DESIGN/DATA TYPE]. In one project, the research question was [QUESTION]; my role included [TASKS YOU PERFORMED]. One challenge was [REAL CHALLENGE], and I learned [INSIGHT]. I'm currently drawn to [AREA], though I'm still exploring different research fields. I'd be interested in hearing how that kind of background might connect with your group's work.”

The longer version makes sense only if invited. It leaves a natural opening for the professor to ask about a particular project, skill or interest.

#### B4. Why “Tell me about yourself” is context-dependent

At an academic meeting, “Tell me about yourself” often means **tell me about your academic and research background**, although the professor may also want to hear about motivations or plans. A reasonable first answer starts with the relevant background and offers detail if useful.

### C. Academic identity and tense choices

#### C1. “I'm studying” vs. “I studied” vs. “I have studied”

- **“I'm studying preventive medicine.”** — Current course of study.
- **“I studied epidemiology as part of my degree.”** — A completed course or past period.
- **“I've studied epidemiology and biostatistics.”** — Experience accumulated up to now.
- **“I'm in the final year of a five-year degree.”** — State of enrollment and program length.

Do not say *“I graduated from...”* before graduation. The simple present also works for identity: “I'm a final-year undergraduate student.”

#### C2. “I've worked on” vs. “I worked on”

**Present perfect** (*I've worked on...*) highlights cumulative research experience relevant now:

> “I've worked on several observational research projects.”

**Simple past** (*I worked on...*) presents a specific completed event or activity:

> “I worked on a study of health outcomes during my third year.”

Often both appear in the same answer:

> “I've worked on a few projects. In one of them, I was responsible for preparing the regression analysis.”

#### C3. “My research focuses on...” — check what it implies

**“My research focuses on X”** can suggest an established or relatively sustained research agenda. That can be appropriate for someone with concentrated work in X.

For a student with diverse or developing interests:

- “Most of my research experience **so far** has involved X.”
- “I've mainly worked on projects related to X.”
- “I'm currently interested in X.”
- “One area I'm drawn to is X.”

These communicate scope and time without making a permanent claim.

#### C4. Four expressions for interest and commitment

| Expression | Typical implication |
|---|---|
| “I've worked on...” | Prior experience |
| “I'm interested in...” | Current intellectual interest |
| “I'm considering...” | A possible direction, not decided |
| “I'm committed to pursuing...” | Stronger dedication or decision |

Choose the level of commitment that is true.

### D. Describing your research role accurately

**The distinction between a project's work and *your* contribution is essential.**

| Expression | Level of involvement |
|---|---|
| “I observed...” | Observation or shadowing |
| “I assisted with...” | Supporting role |
| “I contributed to...” | Meaningful partial involvement |
| “I was involved in...” | Participation; degree unspecified |
| “I was responsible for...” | Ownership of defined tasks |
| “I coordinated...” | Organized people or activities |
| “I led...” | Substantial leadership; use only if accurate |

#### D1. Useful concrete verbs

- “I **cleaned** the dataset.”
- “I **checked** variable definitions.”
- “I **conducted** statistical analyses.”
- “I **helped design** the questionnaire.”
- “I **coordinated** participant follow-up.”
- “I **interpreted** the results with the team.”
- “I **contributed to drafting** the manuscript.”

Every verb implies a different role. *Assisted in developing the questionnaire* differs from *designed the questionnaire independently*.

#### D2. “We found...” vs. “I found...”

In research discussions, **we** is natural for joint results:

> “We found an association between the variables.”

Use **I** when describing an action that you personally performed:

> “I prepared the descriptive statistics and ran the regression models.”

You can use both in one answer:

> “We examined the relationship between sleep patterns and health outcomes. My main contribution was the statistical analysis.”

#### D3. Publications and status accuracy

- “The project resulted in a **published paper**.” — Only if published.
- “We **submitted a manuscript**.” — Submitted; not necessarily accepted.
- “A manuscript is **under review**.” — Under journal review at the relevant time.
- “We're **preparing a manuscript**.” — Still preparing.
- “I **presented a poster** at a conference.” — Presentation, not necessarily journal publication.

A poster, preprint, manuscript, paper, protocol and conference abstract have different statuses; avoid interchanging them.

### E. Connecting previous experience to future curiosity

#### E1. The experience → lesson → direction bridge

> “Working with real-world data made me more aware of the limitations of cross-sectional analyses. It also got me interested in questions where following participants over time might be useful.”

The sentence explains *why* the next topic interests you, rather than listing unrelated areas.

#### E2. Field interest vs. methods training vs. methodological research

These are three separate propositions:

1. **Research field:** “I'm interested in clinical epidemiology and population health.”
2. **Methods as tools:** “I'd like to strengthen my statistical skills so I can study applied research questions.”
3. **Methodological research as the subject:** “I want to develop new statistical methods.”

Only the third claims an interest in developing methods themselves. A student can want rigorous methods training without wanting to work on methodology as their primary research topic.

#### E3. Keeping options open across *fields*

- “I'm still exploring different research fields.”
- “I currently lean toward population-based research, but I'm open to learning about other fields.”
- “I have some areas I'm drawn to, but I haven't settled on a single long-term direction.”

These express broad intellectual openness; they should not be reduced to indecision between two specific project ideas.

### F. Answering common supervisor follow-ups

#### F1. “Which project did you enjoy most?”

> “I particularly enjoyed the project on [TOPIC], partly because I was able to work through the research question, analysis and interpretation. What I found most engaging was [SPECIFIC ASPECT].”

*Enjoyed* asks about your experience; don't replace it with a generic claim that the topic is important.

#### F2. “What was the most challenging part?”

> “One challenge was [SPECIFIC ISSUE]. We approached it by [WHAT ACTUALLY HAPPENED]. It taught me [INSIGHT].”

If the issue remains unresolved, say so:

> “We haven't fully resolved it yet, but it has made me more careful about how we interpret the results.”

#### F3. “How independently did you work?”

> “I handled [TASK] independently, while [DESIGN/INTERPRETATION] involved discussion with my supervisor.”

Avoid treating independence as all-or-nothing.

#### F4. “What software have you used?”

> “I've used [SOFTWARE] for [SPECIFIC TASK]. I'm comfortable with [ACTUAL SKILLS], and I'm still developing my experience with [OTHER TASK].”

**“I'm familiar with”** = you know something about it; **“I've used”** = some hands-on experience; **“I'm proficient in”** = substantial competence. These should not be claimed interchangeably.

#### F5. “Where do you want to go next?”

> “At this point, I'd like to gain deeper research experience and learn more about different directions before committing to a long-term specialization.”

Useful when your longer-term plans are genuinely still open.

### G. Pragmatic choices that change the tone

| Slightly awkward or overstrong | More calibrated | Why |
|---|---|---|
| “I am an expert in statistics.” | “I've gained experience conducting regression analyses.” | Specifies evidence |
| “I did the whole study.” | “I took responsibility for the analysis, while the team handled recruitment.” | Accurate division |
| “I only did data cleaning.” | “One of my responsibilities was data cleaning and quality control.” | Neutral, concrete |
| “This project proves...” | “The findings suggest...” | Appropriate evidential strength |
| “I'm interested in everything.” | “I'm still exploring several research fields.” | More credible and precise |
| “I will definitely work in your lab.” | “I'd be interested in learning about your group's projects.” | Avoids unearned commitment |

#### G1. Pronunciation and word choice

- **research** can function as a noun and verb; pronunciation stress varies by dialect and speaker. Clarity matters more than forcing one universal pattern.
- **data** takes a singular or plural agreement depending on style and context; “The data were collected...” is common in scientific writing, while conversational usage varies.
- **analyze** / **analysis** / **analyses**: *analyze* (verb), *analysis* (one), *analyses* (plural). Say “I conducted several analyses,” not “several analysis.”
- **evidence** is usually uncountable: “some evidence,” not “an evidence.”

### H. Unexpected questions and flexible responses

**Situation 1 — Professor begins with “Tell me about yourself.”**

Professor: “Tell me a little about yourself.”  
Student: “Sure. I'll start with my academic background. I'm studying [FIELD], and most of my research experience has been in [RESEARCH TYPE]. I can give you an example if that would be helpful.”  
Professor: “Yes, tell me about one project.”

**Situation 2 — Professor asks for detail earlier than expected.**

Professor: “What model did you use?”  
Student: “For that particular analysis, we used [ACTUAL MODEL]. We selected it because [ACCURATE RATIONALE]. There are some limitations I can explain as well.”

**Situation 3 — Professor assumes you were the principal investigator.**

Professor: “So you designed the whole study?”  
Student: “The study design was developed by the research team. I contributed mainly to [YOUR OWN ROLE].”

**Situation 4 — Professor asks about a different field.**

Professor: “Have you considered research outside epidemiology?”  
Student: “Yes, I'm open to exploring other fields. My experience so far is mostly in epidemiology, so I'd be interested in learning how the questions and working methods differ.”

**Situation 5 — Professor asks a question you can't answer accurately.**

Professor: “How many participants had complete follow-up?”  
Student: “I don't have the exact number offhand, and I wouldn't want to give you an inaccurate figure. I could check the report.”

**Situation 6 — Professor compliments your experience.**

Professor: “You've worked on quite a range of projects.”  
Student: “Thank you! I've learned different things from them, and I'm still figuring out which directions I'd most like to pursue.”

### I. Extended branching academic dialogue

**Professor:** “Could you briefly walk me through your research background?”  
**Student:** “Of course. I'm completing my undergraduate training in [FIELD]. My experience has mainly involved observational research, including projects related to [TOPIC]. I've worked on several stages of the research process, especially [VERIFIED TASKS].”  
**Professor:** “What did you personally do in those projects?”  
**Student:** “In one project, I was responsible for [TASK]. Other members of the team handled [DIFFERENT TASK], and we discussed the interpretation together.”  
**Professor:** “What did you learn?”  
**Student:** “One thing that stood out was how much the data and study design affect what conclusions we can draw. It made me more interested in [GENUINE SCIENTIFIC QUESTION].”  
**Professor:** “Are you planning to specialize in that area?”  
**Student:** “I'm certainly interested in it at the moment, but I'm still exploring different research fields. I'd like to learn more about the questions your group is working on before narrowing my direction.”  
**Professor:** “That sounds reasonable. What kind of project would help you develop?”  
**Student:** “I'd value a project where I could take meaningful responsibility for part of the research process. I'm also interested in strengthening my ability to link research questions with suitable designs and analyses.”  
**Professor:** “I see. Let me tell you about one of our projects.”  
**Student:** “I'd be glad to hear about it.”

**Variation A — Asked to be briefer:**  
Professor: “Could you give me the short version?”  
Student: “Sure. My background is in [FIELD], and my strongest practical experience is [TASK]. I'm now looking to deepen my research experience.”

**Variation B — Asked for a publication:**  
Professor: “Was that work published?”  
Student: “The study [ACTUAL CURRENT STATUS]. I contributed to [MY ROLE].”

**Variation C — Asked about methods research:**  
Professor: “Are you interested in developing statistical methods?”  
Student: “My main interest at present is applying appropriate methods to substantive research questions. I'd be interested in strengthening my methods training, though I haven't decided to focus on developing methods as a research topic.”

### J. Reusable expressions and guiding principles

| Function | Natural expression |
|---|---|
| Short academic introduction | “I'm currently completing a degree in...” |
| Broad experience | “Most of my research experience so far has involved...” |
| Your own contribution | “My main responsibility was...” |
| Team contribution | “We worked together on...” |
| Describe learning | “One thing I learned from that was...” |
| Highlight a challenge | “The most challenging aspect was...” |
| Link to curiosity | “That got me interested in...” |
| Current interest | “At the moment, I'm particularly drawn to...” |
| Explore fields | “I'm keeping an open mind about different research fields.” |
| Offer expansion | “I'm happy to go into more detail if that's useful.” |
| Honest uncertainty | “I don't have the exact figure offhand.” |
| Invite the other person's input | “I'd be interested to hear how your group approaches that.” |

**Core principles:** Answer first, then expand on invitation. Use *I* for verifiable individual tasks and *we* for team activities. Describe methods as tools when you mean methods training. Be precise about research output status. Explain why an experience shaped an interest. Keep the introduction modular so it can follow the actual conversation rather than a fixed speech.
