# Lesson 06 · Topic-Specific Discussion (Replaceable)

## 6.3 Discussing a Professor's Paper and Asking Scientific Questions

**Learning objectives:** Refer to a paper accurately, explain what drew your attention, summarize its question and findings, ask informed research questions, connect the paper with your own experience, and respond constructively if the professor challenges your reading.

**Scope:** Focus on discussion language and scholarly reading, rather than making an elaborate performance of praise. Examples of scientific findings below are fictional placeholders. The paper's publication type and conclusions must be checked before using any claim in a real meeting.

### A. What makes a useful paper discussion?

A productive paper discussion normally includes some combination of:

1. **Identification:** Which paper or project are you referring to?
2. **Understanding:** What question, design, or result did you take from it?
3. **Specific interest:** What caught your attention and why?
4. **Reasoned question:** What do you genuinely want to learn from the author?
5. **Connection:** How does it relate to a question, skill or experience of yours?

**A compact opening:**

> “I read your paper on [TOPIC]. What caught my attention was how you handled [SPECIFIC RESEARCH DECISION]. I encountered a related issue in a project involving [ACCURATE EXPERIENCE], so I was curious how your team decided on that approach.”

You don't need all five elements in a fixed order. If the professor asks about your background first, you can bring in a paper at a natural point later.

#### A1. Reading a paper vs. evaluating the research

**“I read the paper.”** — You read it; doesn't guarantee full mastery.

**“I found the discussion of [TOPIC] particularly useful.”** — Specific appreciation.

**“I'm interested in the rationale for [METHOD].”** — Identifies an analytical decision you'd like to discuss.

**“I have a concern about [ASSUMPTION].”** — Introduces critique; you should be able to explain the concern.

A genuine technical question is usually stronger than ten generic compliments.

### B. Introducing a paper without sounding rehearsed

#### B1. Natural opening expressions

- “I **came across** your paper on [TOPIC].” — Discovered it, often incidentally.
- “I **read** your paper on [TOPIC].” — Direct.
- “I **had a chance to read** your recent paper on [TOPIC].” — Conversational and polite.
- “I **was looking through** some of your work and found the study on [TOPIC].” — Describes exploration.
- “One paper I was particularly interested in was your study of [TOPIC].” — Selective interest.

**Came across** is neutral, not a claim that you stumbled upon it randomly. Don't force “recent” if the work isn't recent.

#### B2. “What caught my attention was...”

This focus structure draws attention to a particular feature:

> “**What caught my attention was** your decision to follow participants over time.”

> “**What stood out to me was** the contrast between the primary and sensitivity analyses.”

> “**What I found particularly interesting was** how the authors defined the outcome.”

It is useful for explaining *why this paper*, rather than announcing that all of the professor's work is incredible.

#### B3. “Your paper” vs. “the paper” vs. “your group's work”

- **Your paper:** Natural when the professor is an author.
- **The paper:** Neutral; useful when you want to emphasize the work itself.
- **Your group's work:** Can refer to work conducted across a team or laboratory.

Avoid assuming that the professor personally collected data, chose every analytic technique, or led every decision simply because their name appears as an author. Ask about the team's work if unsure.

#### B4. Correctly identifying what you read

| Source | Safe wording |
|---|---|
| Original research article | “In your study, you examined...” |
| Study protocol | “In the protocol, you outlined plans to...” |
| Systematic review | “In your review, you synthesized evidence on...” |
| Commentary or perspective | “In your commentary, you argued that...” |
| Preprint | “I read the preprint of your study on...” |
| Conference abstract | “I saw the abstract of your presentation on...” |

A protocol usually does not contain final outcome results. A systematic review is not the same as a trial. A preprint isn't necessarily peer reviewed.

### C. Summarizing a paper's reasoning clearly

#### C1. A three-sentence mini-summary

> “As I understand it, the study aimed to [QUESTION]. The researchers used [DESIGN/DATA APPROACH], and the main finding was [VERIFIED RESULT]. What I found especially interesting was [PARTICULAR ASPECT].”

**As I understand it** allows the author to correct your interpretation without you pretending to be completely certain.

#### C2. “The authors found,” “the paper reports,” “the results suggest”

- “The authors **found** an association between...” — Describes a reported finding.
- “The paper **reports** a higher prevalence of...” — Closely attributes the statement to the source.
- “The results **suggest**...” — Frames an interpretation based on evidence.
- “The authors **argue that**...” — Attributes reasoning or conclusion, not necessarily an established fact.

**Reports / concludes / argues / demonstrates** carry different evidential force. Attribution often helps keep your discussion accurate.

#### C3. “As I understand it” vs. “If I understand correctly”

- “**As I understand it**, the outcome was defined using...” — Your current interpretation.
- “**If I understand correctly**, the outcome was measured at two time points?” — Explicitly invites confirmation.
- “**My reading was that** the main concern was selection bias.” — Describes how you interpreted the text.

A question can be more precise than a broad admission of confusion:

> “Could I check whether the sample was restricted before or after the missing-data exclusions?”

### D. Asking substantive questions about a paper

A useful question identifies a specific **decision, assumption, result, limitation or implication**.

#### D1. Questions about design

- “What motivated the choice of that study design?”
- “Was a longitudinal approach considered?”
- “How did you decide which population to recruit?”
- “Was that outcome definition established in advance?”
- “Did you encounter any challenges with recruitment or follow-up?”

**Why did you... ?** is grammatical and can be entirely appropriate, but **“What motivated the decision to...?”** can sound more exploratory and less like demanding a defense.

#### D2. Questions about analysis

- “How did you decide which covariates to include?”
- “Did you consider any alternative model specifications?”
- “What assumptions were most important for the analysis?”
- “How did you assess missing data?”
- “What was the rationale for choosing that cutoff?”
- “Was the interaction analysis planned in advance or exploratory?”

Select questions supported by the actual paper. Don't ask a question as if the authors failed to report something without first checking.

#### D3. Questions about interpretation

- “How do you interpret the difference between those two estimates?”
- “Do you think the pattern could reflect differences in measurement?”
- “How much weight would you give the subgroup findings?”
- “What do you see as the main limitation in interpreting that result?”
- “How might these findings inform the next study?”

#### D4. Questions about future work

- “Has your group followed up on that finding?”
- “Are there related questions you're interested in exploring?”
- “Would collecting data at additional time points help answer that question?”
- “What do you see as the most promising next direction?”

These are suitable for discussing the paper; probing actual student roles or making commitments belongs more substantially in 6.4 and Lesson 07.

### E. Connecting the paper to your experience naturally

#### E1. Paper → relevant connection → open question

> “Your discussion of missing data caught my attention because I encountered a similar issue in an observational study. In my case, we had to decide [TRUE DECISION]. I was curious how your team handled [RELATED ISSUE].”

The **connection should be real**. You can connect by research question, design, measurement, interpretation, or a technical problem, even when the clinical topics differ.

#### E2. “Reminded me of” vs. “related to” vs. “relevant to”

- “It **reminded me of** a challenge we encountered.” — Natural personal association.
- “It's **related to** something I've worked on.” — There is a substantive link.
- “It's **relevant to** my interest in population-level outcomes.” — Matters for your present interest.

**“That resonates with me”** is idiomatic and means it feels meaningful or familiar; in a technical conversation, specifying *what* resonates often makes the reply stronger.

#### E3. Linking different fields without forced similarity

> “The disease area is different from the projects I've worked on, but the questions around study design and data quality are familiar to me.”

> “I haven't worked in that exact field, though I can see a connection in how the studies approach population-based questions.”

You can have useful scientific overlap without claiming identical expertise.

### F. Critical thinking without sounding confrontational

#### F1. Curious questions vs. unsupported criticism

**Potentially accusatory:**

> “Why didn't you adjust for [VARIABLE]?”

**Genuinely exploratory:**

> “I noticed [VARIABLE] wasn't included in that model. Was there a particular reason for leaving it out?”

The second still allows rigorous discussion. Use the first when a direct, established relationship makes it natural and your concern is clear.

#### F2. “I wonder whether...”

> “I wonder whether differences in recruitment might explain part of that pattern.”

This introduces a possibility without asserting it as a fact.

**Grammar:** “I wonder **whether recruitment differences might explain** the pattern” uses *embedded statement order*. Avoid “I wonder whether **might recruitment differences explain**...”

#### F3. Expressing a methodological concern

- “One concern I had was the possibility of selection bias.”
- “I was wondering how robust that result was to different outcome definitions.”
- “Do you think residual confounding could be an issue?”
- “I wasn't sure how the authors addressed the missingness assumption.”
- “I may have missed it, but was there a sensitivity analysis for that?”

Only use a technical label when you know its meaning. A specific question about measurement or sampling is often clearer than vague reference to “bias.”

#### F4. A professor corrects your interpretation

Professor: “Actually, that model was unadjusted.”  
Student: “Ah, thanks for correcting me. I misread that part of the table. So the adjusted estimates are reported in the next column?”

This combines acknowledgment, correction and a focused follow-up. You don't need an elaborate apology.

### G. Grammar and register contrasts

#### G1. “Interested in” + noun / -ing

Correct:

- “I'm interested **in your work** on...”
- “I'm interested **in understanding** how the decision was made.”

Incorrect:

- “I'm interested **to understand** how...” — This construction is possible in some contexts (*interested to hear/learn*) but the safest general pattern for your research interest is *interested in + noun/-ing*.

**“I'd be interested to hear...”** is nevertheless an established idiomatic phrase meaning *I'd welcome hearing...*. Treat this as a phrase, not proof that any verb follows *interested to* freely.

#### G2. “Curious about” and “curious how/why/whether”

- “I'm curious **about** the design choice.”
- “I was curious **how** you handled missing data.”
- “I'm curious **whether** that was prespecified.”

Be aware of register: *I'm curious* is common and conversational; *I'd be interested in understanding* is slightly more formal.

#### G3. “On” vs. “about”

- “A paper **on** adolescent sleep.” — Topic.
- “A study **of** adolescent sleep patterns.” — Subject studied.
- “An article **about** data quality.” — Accessible conversational wording.

All can be natural; fixed collocations and the specific noun matter.

#### G4. “Your findings suggest” vs. “I think you proved”

The former describes tentative evidential implication; the latter often overstates causal or theoretical certainty. Scientific questions can be interesting precisely because the interpretation remains uncertain.

#### G5. Avoid flattery as a substitute for engagement

“I absolutely love all your papers” gives no evidence of engagement. A short, specific observation plus a genuine question makes for a more productive exchange.

### H. Unexpected paper-discussion scenarios

**Scenario 1 — The professor asks which paper you read.**

Professor: “Which of our papers caught your attention?”  
Student: “The study on [VERIFIED TOPIC]. I was particularly interested in [REAL DECISION OR FINDING], and I had a question about [ISSUE].”

**Scenario 2 — You read only the abstract.**

Professor: “Did you read the full paper?”  
Student: “I've read the abstract and the main summary, but I haven't worked through the full methods yet. I'd rather not pretend I have.”

Better to read the whole paper in advance when possible; always be truthful if asked.

**Scenario 3 — The professor's work has changed since publication.**

Professor: “We've actually moved away from that approach.”  
Student: “That's interesting. What led the group to change direction?”

**Scenario 4 — The professor challenges your criticism.**

Professor: “What specifically makes you think there's selection bias?”  
Student: “My question was about [RECRUITMENT/LOSS TO FOLLOW-UP FACT]. I may be missing information on [PROCESS], so I'd be interested in how you assessed it.”

**Scenario 5 — A technical term is unfamiliar.**

Professor: “We then used inverse probability weighting.”  
Student: “I'm familiar with the general idea, but I haven't applied it myself. Could you explain what role it played in this study?”

**Scenario 6 — Professor asks what you'd do next.**

Professor: “How would you build on this work?”  
Student: “One possibility I'd be interested in exploring is [FEASIBLE FOLLOW-UP]. I'd need to understand the available data and resources before saying whether it's viable.”

**Scenario 7 — Study is a protocol with no outcome data.**

Professor: “What did you think of the paper?”  
Student: “I found the proposed design interesting, especially [DETAIL]. Since it was a protocol, I was curious how recruitment has progressed rather than assuming outcome results are already available.”

### I. Extended two-way discussion with branches

**Professor:** “Was there any particular paper you wanted to discuss?”  
**Student:** “Yes. I read your paper on [TOPIC], and what stood out to me was the way the team defined [OUTCOME OR EXPOSURE].”  
**Professor:** “Why did that interest you?”  
**Student:** “I encountered a related measurement issue in an observational project. The clinical topic was different, but the challenge of choosing and interpreting variable definitions felt familiar.”  
**Professor:** “I see. What question did you have?”  
**Student:** “I noticed that the authors also considered [ALTERNATIVE DEFINITION, IF ACTUALLY REPORTED]. Was that primarily a robustness check, or was there a different scientific reason?”  
**Professor:** “Both, actually. We wanted to see whether the finding depended heavily on the definition.”  
**Student:** “That makes sense. Did the comparison change your confidence in the interpretation?”  
**Professor:** “It made us more cautious about one subgroup.”  
**Student:** “I can see why. The subgroup numbers looked relatively small, so I wondered how much weight you gave that result.”  
**Professor:** “Not much; we treated it as exploratory.”  
**Student:** “That's helpful. It also raises an interesting question about what kind of follow-up data would be most informative.”  
**Professor:** “Exactly. In fact, we're thinking about extending the study.”  
**Student:** “I'd be interested to hear what directions you're considering.”

**Branch A — Misunderstood an analytical choice:**  
Professor: “That wasn't a sensitivity analysis; it was the primary model.”  
Student: “Thanks for clarifying. I had the order of the analyses mixed up. Could I ask how the secondary model was intended to complement it?”

**Branch B — Paper outside your experience:**  
Professor: “Have you worked with this method before?”  
Student: “I haven't applied it directly. What interested me was the scientific question it helped answer. I'd be glad to learn more about how the method works.”

**Branch C — You don't want to pretend you've read everything:**  
Professor: “Did you see our newest follow-up paper?”  
Student: “I haven't read that one yet. I'd be interested in taking a look—what question does it address?”

### J. High-value expression bank and paper-discussion checklist

| Purpose | Expression |
|---|---|
| Introduce paper | “I had a chance to read your paper on...” |
| Highlight detail | “What stood out to me was...” |
| Attribute finding | “The paper reports that...” |
| Check reading | “If I understood correctly, the authors...” |
| Ask rationale | “What motivated the decision to...?” |
| Ask alternate design | “Was an alternative approach considered?” |
| Ask interpretation | “How do you interpret that finding?” |
| Raise concern | “I was wondering whether... might have affected...” |
| Connect experience | “I encountered a related issue in...” |
| Return to a detail | “Could I pick up on the point about...?” |
| Admit unfamiliar method | “I haven't used that method directly.” |
| Ask next direction | “Has your group followed up on that finding?” |
| Respond to correction | “Thanks for clarifying—I had that part mixed up.” |

**Reading checklist for an actual professor's article:** Identify publication type and date; question; design and population; outcome/exposure definitions; primary result and uncertainty; main limitation; your genuine connection; and one or two substantive questions. If evidence is absent from the paper, don't act as if you have it. A question about unreported information is fine when framed accurately.

**Core principles:** Specificity beats generic enthusiasm. Ask about the author's decisions without assuming every choice was theirs personally. Distinguish results from interpretations, reports from opinions, protocols from completed studies, and interest from expertise. Use uncertainty honestly. A paper discussion should be reciprocal and curious, not a scripted oral examination.
