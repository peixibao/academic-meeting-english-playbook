# Lesson 06 · Topic-Specific Discussion

## 6.4a Defending an Early Research Idea: Novelty, Design, Feasibility, and Alternatives

**Placement:** After 6.4 *Exploring Projects, Research Directions, and Scientific Fit*, immediately before the existing 6.5 *Motivation and Program Fit*.  
**Status:** Curriculum-audit supplement for learner review; not yet uploaded to GitHub.  
**Purpose:** Discuss and defend a **preliminary research idea** under follow-up questions. A formal proposal interview may demand greater depth; an initial prospective-supervisor chat may only need a brief question and curiosity. Never imply that a tentative idea is an approved project.

### A. What does it mean to “defend” a research idea?

#### A1. Questions you might hear

- “What exactly is your research question?”
- “Why is it important?”
- “What is new about the idea?”
- “What would your hypothesis be?”
- “How would you study that?”
- “Do you have the data and resources to do it?”
- “What if the result doesn't support your hypothesis?”
- “What is your backup plan?”
- “How would the project be suitable for an MPhil or a PhD?”

**Defend（论证、答辩）** means explain the reasoning and limitations, respond to challenges, and revise when evidence calls for it. You do not need to argue that every decision you first proposed was perfect.

#### A2. Three levels of readiness

| Situation | What you should be prepared to do |
|---|---|
| Exploratory PI chat | Explain a broad question, interest and uncertainty; learn about feasibility |
| Formal research-degree interview | Justify a workable plan at the level requested by the program |
| Proposal-focused funding interview | Explain significance, approach, deliverables and potential limitations to the relevant audience |

The actual invitation and program requirements determine the expected specificity.

#### A3. A seven-anchor idea map

1. **Problem / gap** — What remains uncertain?
2. **Question** — What exactly would you investigate?
3. **Rationale** — Why might the answer matter?
4. **Proposed data / design** — How could you study it?
5. **Feasibility** — What resources or permissions are needed?
6. **Limitations** — What might your design fail to establish?
7. **Fallback / next step** — What could you revise?

Use this map to reason, not as a compulsory seven-minute speech.

### B. Moving from a broad interest to a researchable question

#### B1. Broad interest vs. question

**Broad interest:** “I'm interested in adolescent health.”

**Still broad:** “I want to study factors affecting adolescent health.”

**More researchable:** “I would be interested in examining whether [SPECIFIC EXPOSURE] is associated with [DEFINED OUTCOME] among [POPULATION] using [AVAILABLE DATA TYPE].”

A more specific question can still be preliminary when the data and measurement have not been verified.

#### B2. Population, exposure, outcome, and design

A useful framework for many observational questions:

- **Population:** Who is being studied?
- **Exposure / predictor:** Which characteristic or measurement is of interest?
- **Outcome:** What is being measured or predicted?
- **Time:** At what time(s) are variables observed?
- **Design/data:** What kind of evidence is available?

Not every research discipline uses these labels, and not every project fits PICO or PECO. Choose a framework suitable for the question.

#### B3. Research question vs. hypothesis

- **Question:** “Is X associated with Y in population Z?”
- **Hypothesis:** “We hypothesize that higher X will be associated with higher Y.”
- **Objective:** “To assess the association between X and Y.”

A hypothesis should be justified, testable and clearly connected to what the study can measure. Exploratory research may start with a question without a strong directional hypothesis.

#### B4. “I wonder whether...” vs. “I propose to...”

- “I **wonder whether**...” — Exploratory curiosity.
- “One possible question **would be**...” — Tentative option.
- “I **would propose** examining...” — More developed suggestion.
- “My proposed project **will**...” — Stronger commitment and specificity; use when appropriate.

In an initial conversation, don't accidentally present a brainstorm as a settled approved thesis.

### C. Explaining the research gap and significance

#### C1. A gap must be grounded

> “From the literature I've reviewed, one question that appears less clearly addressed is [NARROW ISSUE]. I'd want to check the evidence more systematically before calling it an established gap.”

Avoid: “No one has ever studied this.” unless a sufficiently thorough search really justifies it.

#### C2. Novelty can take several forms

- Studying a population or setting for which relevant evidence is limited.
- Improving measurement or examining different outcome definitions.
- Asking a different temporal, comparative or causal question.
- Analyzing an existing dataset in a scientifically meaningful new way.
- Combining evidence across settings or disciplines.

**Novel（新颖的）** is not the same as **important（重要的）** or **feasible（可行的）**. An unusual idea can still be trivial; a useful replication may be valuable even with limited novelty.

#### C3. “Why does this matter?”

> “The question matters because [SPECIFIC SCIENTIFIC OR CLINICAL UNCERTAINTY]. Understanding it could help [PLAUSIBLE NEXT STEP], although a single observational study would not be enough to justify [STRONGER APPLICATION].”

Identify the actual contribution at the level your design allows.

#### C4. Literature vs. your own intuition

- “**Previous studies report**...” — Requires real sources.
- “**My reading suggests**...” — A personal synthesis; specify scope.
- “**I suspect**...” — Hypothesis, not observed fact.
- “**The results would show**...” — Avoid predicting results as if certain.

### D. Choosing a design and describing a provisional analytic plan

#### D1. “How would you test that?”

> “I'd start by clarifying the outcome definition, population and data structure. If [RELEVANT VARIABLES] were available, one possible approach would be [DEFENSIBLE DESIGN/ANALYSIS]. I'd then need to assess [ASSUMPTIONS OR LIMITATIONS].”

The conditional clause matters: you are discussing a plan that depends on actual data availability.

#### D2. Design alternatives

**Cross-sectional:** Useful for describing patterns at one time; limited temporal inference.  
**Prospective cohort:** Can examine change and temporal sequence, but may involve follow-up, attrition and confounding.  
**Randomized trial:** Appropriate for some intervention questions if ethical and feasible, but many exposures cannot or should not be randomized.  
**Secondary analysis:** Uses existing data; possibilities depend on how data were collected.

No design is automatically “best” before the question and constraints are known.

#### D3. A minimal analysis-plan explanation

> “The primary outcome would be [DEFINED OUTCOME]. I would examine [EXPOSURE / COMPARISON] and consider [RELEVANT COVARIATES], guided by the substantive question. The model would depend on [OUTCOME TYPE / DATA STRUCTURE], and I'd want to assess missingness and measurement quality first.”

Do not name a specific model merely to sound advanced. If you say “I'll use multiple imputation,” be ready to discuss its assumptions and justification.

#### D4. Confounding vs. prediction vs. causation

- **Associational question:** Are X and Y related in observed data?
- **Prediction question:** Can available information predict Y accurately in the target setting?
- **Causal question:** What would changing X do to Y under stated assumptions?

These questions can require different design, adjustment and performance criteria. Don't treat them as interchangeable just because all use regression.

#### D5. “What makes that design appropriate?”

A full answer links the design to its goal:

> “The design would be appropriate for [SPECIFIC TYPE OF QUESTION] because [EXPLANATION]. It would still leave [LIMITATION], which is why I'd frame the conclusion as [PROPER EVIDENCE CLAIM].”

### E. Feasibility: moving from ideas to real constraints

#### E1. Eight feasibility checks

| Dimension | What you must establish | Useful interview question |
|---|---|---|
| Dataset | Access and completeness | “Are those variables available?” |
| Measurement | Consistent exposure/outcome definition | “How were those outcomes measured?” |
| Sample | Adequate size and events | “How many relevant observations are available?” |
| Timeline | Project can be completed in degree period | “Would that be manageable within the thesis timeline?” |
| Expertise | Suitable methodological supervision | “What expertise would the project require?” |
| Ethics | Approvals and appropriate data use | “Would additional ethics approval be needed?” |
| Resources | Staff, software, recruitment, funding | “What resources would this require?” |
| Deliverable | Achievable thesis or study output | “What would the core deliverable be?” |

Do not assert that funding, ethics approval or data access is available until verified.

#### E2. Feasible / viable / practical / realistic

- **Feasible:** Can be done with constraints.
- **Viable:** Capable of proceeding or succeeding over time.
- **Practical:** Workable in practice.
- **Realistic:** Consistent with available time, skills, evidence and resources.

> “The question sounds scientifically interesting, but I'd need to assess whether it's feasible within the available timeline.”

#### E3. Sample size and event counts

**Professor:** “Would the dataset be large enough?”  
**Student:** “I'd need to know the expected effect size or precision goal, outcome frequency, design and proposed analysis before making that assessment.”

Avoid claiming “300 participants is enough for every analysis” or “a small p-value proves sample adequacy.”

#### E4. Study timeline

> “I would break the work into data assessment, question refinement, analysis, interpretation and writing, allowing time for revisions. The exact milestones would depend on approval and access.”

Milestones are useful planning tools; do not promise to publish by a date as though journal acceptance is under your control.

#### E5. Ethics and data permissions

> “Before using the dataset, I would need to confirm the approved uses, access arrangements and any additional ethics requirements.”

Do not promise to share protected data to show enthusiasm.

### F. Anticipating criticism, uncertainty, and alternative explanations

#### F1. “What could go wrong?”

A credible answer identifies a **substantive risk** with a mitigation plan:

> “One major concern would be [RISK]. I'd want to check [INDICATOR / DATA SOURCE] early. If the issue were substantial, we might need to [ALTERNATIVE / NARROWER AIM].”

#### F2. Alternative explanations

- Residual or unmeasured confounding.
- Selection into the sample or follow-up.
- Measurement error or misclassification.
- Reverse causation in observational data.
- Model dependence and small sample sizes.
- Random variation and multiple testing.

These are possible categories, not an automatic checklist to attach to every design.

#### F3. How to respond to a methodological objection

**Professor:** “Why would that model be appropriate?”  
**Student:** “I'd need to verify [ASSUMPTION] first. If it didn't hold, one alternative would be [JUSTIFIED CHANGE]. I wouldn't want to settle on the model before reviewing the data.”

You can be open to changing the approach without surrendering the scientific question.

#### F4. What if the hypothesis is unsupported?

> “An estimate that doesn't support the hypothesized relationship would still be informative if the study was well designed and sufficiently precise. I'd examine the uncertainty and alternative explanations rather than adjust the hypothesis after seeing the result.”

A null or inconclusive result isn't automatically a failed thesis.

#### F5. What if there are no data?

> “If those variables aren't available, the initial question might not be feasible. I'd ask whether a related question could be addressed using existing measures before considering new data collection.”

This is conditional reasoning, not an assurance that an alternative project exists.

#### F6. What if a professor points out that the idea is already well studied?

> “That's helpful context. I may have overlooked some of the literature. Could we distinguish whether the unresolved issue is the overall relationship or the narrower question of [POPULATION / DEFINITION / DESIGN]?”

If the gap disappears, revise the project. Defending an idea doesn't mean defending a false novelty claim.

### G. Graduate-degree scope and discussion language

#### G1. MPhil / research master's vs. PhD level

A **research master's project** often needs a focused, manageable question, suitable training and a defensible thesis. A **PhD project** typically requires a deeper original contribution and sustained independent research, but actual scope and entry requirements differ by institution and discipline.

- “For a master's thesis, I might begin with a well-defined analysis using existing data.”
- “At doctoral level, I'd need to think more carefully about the original contribution and how the work could develop into a coherent research program.”

Don't claim that an MPhil guarantees a PhD transfer or that every PhD must take a fixed number of years without institution-specific evidence.

#### G2. Not every applicant needs a completed proposal

**Initial meeting:** “I have a few questions I'd like to explore, but I'd want to develop the precise proposal in light of the group's projects and available data.”

**Interview requesting a written proposal:** “My current proposal focuses on [ACTUAL SUBMITTED QUESTION]. The central design choice is [ACTUAL CHOICE], and the main challenge I'm anticipating is [REAL CHALLENGE].”

The second answer must reflect the proposal you actually submitted.

#### G3. Hypothetical / conditional language

- “**If** we had repeated measures, **we could** examine change over time.”
- “**If** the outcome were rare, **we would need to consider** model stability.”
- “It **might be possible** to...”
- “A **potential** alternative would be...”
- “I'd **start by checking**...”

**Would** and **could** commonly mark conditional plans. Avoid stacking *might maybe perhaps* so heavily that your answer loses meaning.

#### G4. “Push back” diplomatically when you disagree

- “I see the concern. My reasoning was...”
- “That's a fair point. One distinction I'd make is...”
- “Could I explain why I considered that approach?”
- “I would be cautious about assuming [CLAIM], because...”
- “You may be right that the original question needs narrowing.”

A good defense addresses evidence, not personal status.

### H. Ten interview challenges and response branches

#### Scenario 1 — “What's new about it?”

**Professor:** “How is this different from existing studies?”  
**Student:** “The possible contribution would be [VERIFIED DIFFERENCE]. I'd still want to review [RELEVANT LITERATURE] before making a firm novelty claim.”

#### Scenario 2 — “What is your actual hypothesis?”

**Professor:** “You've described an interest. What would you test?”  
**Student:** “A testable hypothesis would be that [SPECIFIC RELATIONSHIP], if [MEASUREMENTS] were available. I'd want to justify that direction using prior evidence.”

#### Scenario 3 — “Why that outcome?”

**Professor:** “Why not use a continuous outcome?”  
**Student:** “That could be worthwhile. The choice would depend on the substantive question, measurement properties and analysis plan. I'd compare the information lost or gained through each definition.”

#### Scenario 4 — “You've only got 12 months.”

**Professor:** “That may be too much work for a master's.”  
**Student:** “I agree feasibility is important. One narrower first step might be [FOCUSED ANALYSIS], provided the required data already exist.”

#### Scenario 5 — “Your sample is too small.”

**Professor:** “How would you handle low event counts?”  
**Student:** “I'd examine whether the proposed model and comparisons are supportable. If not, I'd simplify the analysis or reconsider the question rather than force an unstable model.”

#### Scenario 6 — “The project cannot be randomized.”

**Professor:** “How would you avoid causal overinterpretation?”  
**Student:** “I'd define the question carefully and state the design limits. If we were aiming for a causal interpretation, we'd need a defensible strategy and assumptions rather than relying on an adjusted association alone.”

#### Scenario 7 — “There's no access to that dataset.”

**Professor:** “We don't have permission to use those records.”  
**Student:** “Thank you for clarifying. In that case, the original idea isn't feasible here. Are there existing, appropriately accessible datasets that could support a related question?”

#### Scenario 8 — “What if your finding is null?”

**Professor:** “Would that undermine your thesis?”  
**Student:** “It would change the interpretation, but the project could still be informative. I'd focus on the estimate's uncertainty, study quality, and what the result does and doesn't establish.”

#### Scenario 9 — “Why does this matter to a funding panel?”

**Panelist:** “Explain why we should fund it.”  
**Student:** “The research addresses [VERIFIED IMPORTANT QUESTION]. Our proposed approach could provide [SPECIFIC EVIDENCE], and its feasibility would depend on [RESOURCES, TIMELINE AND APPROVAL].”

#### Scenario 10 — “You're not ready to defend a full proposal.”

**Professor:** “This sounds too preliminary for a formal proposal.”  
**Student:** “I understand. At this stage, I've formulated [QUESTION] and identified [INITIAL APPROACH], but I'd need to do more work on [LITERATURE / DATA / DESIGN]. I'd appreciate knowing what level of detail the program expects.”

### I. Extended dialogue: challenging an idea without pretending certainty

**Professor:** “Do you have any research ideas you'd like to explore?”  
**Student:** “I have an initial question about whether [EXPOSURE] is associated with [OUTCOME] in [POPULATION]. It's still preliminary, and I would need to learn more about the available data.”  
**Professor:** “Why is that question interesting?”  
**Student:** “From what I've read, [ACTUAL UNCERTAINTY] makes the question worth examining. I don't yet know how extensively the narrower question has been studied.”  
**Professor:** “What design would you use?”  
**Student:** “If an existing dataset has the relevant measurements, a secondary observational analysis might be a manageable first step. I'd need to check the timing of measurement and the outcome definition.”  
**Professor:** “Why not use a more sophisticated causal method?”  
**Student:** “That depends on the causal question and whether the necessary assumptions can be supported. I wouldn't want to choose a method before we know the structure and limitations of the data.”  
**Professor:** “What if the relevant variables aren't there?”  
**Student:** “Then the exact question wouldn't be feasible with that dataset. I'd explore whether a related, narrower question could be answered, or reconsider the data source.”  
**Professor:** “And what if the results don't support your hypothesis?”  
**Student:** “I'd still evaluate the estimate and its uncertainty carefully. A well-designed analysis can be informative even if it doesn't support the initial expectation.”  
**Professor:** “How much of this would you plan to complete during an MPhil?”  
**Student:** “I'd want to define a realistic core thesis question first, possibly using existing data. Any larger follow-up would need to be planned separately, taking the program's structure and supervision into account.”  
**Professor:** “That sounds more manageable. What would you want to learn from the group?”  
**Student:** “I'd be interested in how your team evaluates project feasibility and how students are involved in refining the question and analysis plan.”

**What makes the exchange useful:** The student is scientifically engaged but keeps the project provisional, links decisions to evidence, recognizes feasibility and scope, and asks about the real supervision environment.

### J. Functional phrase bank, response patterns, and checks

#### J1. Twelve useful proposal-discussion expressions

| Function | Expression |
|---|---|
| Introduce gap | “A question that seems unresolved is...” |
| Frame idea | “One possible research question would be...” |
| Frame hypothesis | “We would hypothesize that...” |
| Ground significance | “This matters because...” |
| Choose a first step | “I'd start by checking whether...” |
| Propose conditionally | “If those variables were available, we could...” |
| Express feasibility | “It may be feasible within the thesis timeline if...” |
| Discuss limitation | “The main limitation I'd anticipate is...” |
| Respond to objection | “That's a fair concern. One alternative would be...” |
| Address null result | “An inconclusive result would still need careful interpretation.” |
| Reassess gap | “I'd want to review the literature more thoroughly before claiming novelty.” |
| Keep scope honest | “It's an initial idea, not a finalized proposal.” |

#### J2. Grammar distinctions

- **Propose + -ing / noun:** “I propose examining...” / “I propose a study of...”
- **Suggest + -ing / that-clause:** “I suggest examining...” / “I suggest that we examine...”
- **Aim to + verb:** “The project aims to examine...”
- **Hypothesize that + clause:** “We hypothesize that X is associated with Y.”
- **Depend on + noun / -ing:** “Feasibility depends on data access.”
- **Consistent with + noun:** “The finding is consistent with the hypothesis.”
- **Be feasible for / within:** “Feasible within the available timeline.”
- **Account for / adjust for:** “Adjust for prespecified covariates.”
- **What if + present:** “What if the data aren't available?”
- **If + past, would + verb:** “If we had more data, we would consider...”

#### J3. The proposal-defense quick map

| Professor asks | Give first | Then be ready to add |
|---|---|---|
| “What's the question?” | Population, exposure, outcome, comparison | Definitions and timeframe |
| “Why does it matter?” | One justified reason | Literature support and scope |
| “What's new?” | Narrow, verified distinction | Limits to novelty claim |
| “How?” | Design or data option | Assumptions and alternatives |
| “Can it be done?” | Requirements and constraints | Timeline, resources, ethics |
| “What could go wrong?” | Most important risk | Mitigation or narrower plan |
| “What if it's null?” | Honest interpretation | Estimate precision and next step |
| “Why this degree?” | Program-appropriate scale | Link to verified degree structure (6.5) |

#### J4. Final integrity checklist

- [ ] I can distinguish a broad interest, a research question, a hypothesis and a full proposal.
- [ ] I can justify why the question matters without inventing a literature gap.
- [ ] I know which data, permissions and resources are genuinely confirmed.
- [ ] I can articulate at least one plausible limitation and one backup option.
- [ ] I can describe null findings without calling the project a failure.
- [ ] I can adapt the explanation to a short PI chat or a formal interview.
- [ ] I can revise the original idea when a professor identifies a valid issue.
- [ ] I do not portray hypothetical data, statistical results or study approval as established facts.
