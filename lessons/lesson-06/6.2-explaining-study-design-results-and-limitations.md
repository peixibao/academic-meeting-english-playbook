## 6.2 Explaining a Study: Questions, Design, Findings, and Limitations

**Learning objectives:** Explain a real research project in conversational English; distinguish research question, design, population, variables, methods, results and interpretation; describe observational findings responsibly; communicate limitations without apologizing for the study; and respond to technical follow-up questions.

**Scope:** The illustrations below use *hypothetical* health research projects. Replace bracketed details with verified information. Do not present illustrative numbers, methods or outcomes as your own findings.

### A. A conversation-friendly map of a research study

A clear explanation answers these questions, often in the listener's chosen order:

1. **Why?** — The question or gap the study addresses.
2. **Who or what?** — Population, setting and data source.
3. **How?** — Design, measures and main analytical approach.
4. **What did you find?** — Results with appropriate uncertainty.
5. **What does it mean, and what can't it tell us?** — Interpretation, limitations and next questions.
6. **What did you contribute?** — Your role.

A useful skeleton:

> “The study looked at [QUESTION] among [POPULATION]. We used [DESIGN/DATA]. We measured [MAIN EXPOSURE/OUTCOME], and the analysis focused on [APPROACH]. The results [ACCURATE RESULT]. One limitation is [ISSUE], so we have to be cautious about [INTERPRETATION]. My own role was [TASK].”

You can start with the answer the professor requested. If they ask specifically about results, you may describe the finding before explaining the entire background.

#### A1. “Walk me through”

> “Could you walk me through that study?”

**Walk someone through something** = explain it step by step. It does not require a presentation or screen share.

Other natural replies:

- “Sure. Let me start with the research question.”
- “Absolutely. The basic idea was to investigate...”
- “I can give you the overview first, then go into the methods if you'd like.”

### B. Describing questions, hypotheses and objectives

#### B1. “Look at,” “examine,” “investigate,” “assess”

| Verb | Typical use | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Look at** | Natural conversational overview | “We looked at sleep habits and cardiometabolic markers.” |
| **Examine** | Professional neutral analysis | “We examined the association between X and Y.” |
| **Investigate** | Explore a scientific question | “We investigated whether X was associated with Y.” |
| **Assess** | Evaluate or measure | “We assessed the prevalence of Y.” |
| **Estimate** | Calculate a quantity using data/model | “We estimated the prevalence of Y.” |
| **Compare** | Evaluate differences across groups | “We compared outcomes across age groups.” |

**Look into** = investigate an issue, often in conversation: “We wanted to look into whether the relationship differed by sex.”

#### B2. Aim, objective, hypothesis, research question

- **Research question:** “Is X associated with Y in this population?”
- **Aim/objective:** “Our primary aim was to examine the association between X and Y.”
- **Hypothesis:** “We hypothesized that higher X would be associated with Y.”

A study can be exploratory and have no formal directional hypothesis. Don't invent one for a better-sounding answer.

#### B3. “Whether” vs. “if” in embedded research questions

- “We wanted to see **whether** X was associated with Y.”
- “We wanted to see **if** X was associated with Y.”

Both are common and grammatical here. **Whether** often reads slightly more formal and can be clearer when alternatives are explicit: “whether X or Y was the better predictor.”

**Embedded question word order:**

Correct: “We investigated **whether age was associated** with the outcome.”  
Incorrect: “We investigated **whether was age associated** with the outcome.”

### C. Explaining design, participants and data sources

#### C1. Study designs and what they permit

| Design | Useful spoken description | Caution |
|---|---|---|
| Cross-sectional | “Exposure and outcome were measured at roughly the same time.” | Temporality generally difficult to establish |
| Cohort / longitudinal | “We followed participants over time.” | Longitudinal data alone don't guarantee causality |
| Case-control | “We compared people with the outcome to controls and examined prior exposures.” | Sampling and bias considerations |
| Randomized controlled trial | “Participants were randomly assigned to intervention groups.” | Describe actual allocation and outcomes |
| Secondary analysis | “We used existing data collected for another purpose.” | The original dataset may constrain variables and design |
| Systematic review | “We systematically identified and synthesized existing studies.” | Not an analysis of new participants by default |

Be precise: **retrospective** refers to how research is oriented or assembled relative to events; it is not interchangeable with *cross-sectional*. A study can be a retrospective cohort. If unsure, describe the timing instead of guessing at a label.

#### C2. Recruitment and sampling language

- “We **recruited** participants from [SETTING].”
- “Participants were **sampled** using [ACTUAL PROCEDURE].”
- “The analysis **included** [N] eligible participants.”
- “Data were **collected** using questionnaires and clinical measurements.”
- “We **excluded** participants who met [VERIFIED CRITERIA].”
- “We **restricted** the analysis to people with complete data on [VARIABLES].”

**Sampled vs. recruited vs. analyzed**: These may describe three different numbers. Don't conflate them.

#### C3. Survey and measurement terms

- **Exposure** — variable or condition being investigated as a possible predictor.
- **Outcome** — measured endpoint or dependent variable.
- **Covariate** — variable included in a model; its causal role depends on context.
- **Confounder** — variable that distorts the exposure–outcome relationship under a specified causal understanding; don't label every covariate a confounder automatically.
- **Effect modifier / interaction** — whether a relationship differs across a variable's levels; statistical interaction depends on the scale and model.

Practical phrases:

> “Our primary outcome was [Y], defined as [PRE-SPECIFIED DEFINITION].”

> “We treated [X] as a continuous variable and also examined categories in a secondary analysis.”

> “We adjusted for [LIST] based on [ACTUAL RATIONALE].”

### D. Describing statistical analyses without drowning the conversation

#### D1. Give purpose first, technique second

**Less effective:** “I used logistic regression, linear regression, chi-square tests, and interaction terms...”

**Clearer:**

> “We wanted to examine whether several exposures were associated with a binary outcome, so we used logistic regression.”

The listener can then ask about the model, covariates, sample size or assumptions.

#### D2. Statistical verbs and prepositions

| Phrase | Example |
|---|---|
| **Adjust for** | “We adjusted for age and sex.” |
| **Control for** | “We controlled for several prespecified covariates.” |
| **Account for** | “The analysis accounted for clustering by site.” |
| **Stratify by** | “We stratified the analysis by age group.” |
| **Compare across** | “We compared prevalence across regions.” |
| **Test for interaction** | “We tested for interaction between X and Z.” |
| **Model as** | “We modeled age as a continuous variable.” |
| **Sensitivity analysis** | “We ran a sensitivity analysis using an alternative definition.” |

**Adjust for** doesn't automatically mean the model has removed every source of confounding. Describe what was done, not what the model guarantees.

#### D3. “Regression analysis” and related forms

- “We **ran** a regression model.” — Common conversational register.
- “We **fitted** a logistic regression model.” — Technical; *fit* is also acceptable (past *fit* or *fitted* depending on variety and context).
- “We **estimated** the association.” — Focus on estimate.
- “We **used** regression models.” — General, neutral.

Do not say *“we did an association”*; say “We **examined an association**” or “We **tested whether** X was associated with Y.”

#### D4. Distinguishing analysis from methodological research

> “I used regression methods to study a clinical question.”

describes *application* of methods.

> “I developed a new regression estimator.”

claims methodological research. Both are meaningful, but they are different achievements.

### E. Presenting results and evidential strength

#### E1. Association vs. effect vs. cause

**“X was associated with Y.”** — Describes an observed statistical relationship; a good default for observational findings.

**“The estimated effect of X on Y...”** — Implies a causal estimand or causal interpretation, which requires appropriate design and assumptions.

**“X caused Y.”** — Much stronger causal statement; typically unjustified merely by a cross-sectional association.

Compare:

- “Participants with higher X **tended to have** higher Y.”
- “We **observed a positive association** between X and Y.”
- “The data **suggest** a possible relationship.”

These are often appropriate for observed associations, but avoid automatically adding hedges when a claim can be stated precisely with effect estimates and uncertainty.

#### E2. Significant vs. meaningful

**Statistically significant** means that a finding meets a prespecified inferential threshold under the analysis; it does not mean clinically important, large or causal.

- “The association was **statistically significant** in the adjusted model.” — If actually true.
- “The estimate was **imprecise**, with a wide confidence interval.” — If true.
- “The difference may be **clinically meaningful**, but that requires context.” — Separate practical interpretation.

Do not say “There was no association” merely because *p* exceeds a chosen threshold. A safer factual description may be “We didn't find clear evidence of an association in this sample,” together with estimate and interval when relevant.

#### E3. Odds, risk, rate and prevalence

- **Odds ratio (OR):** ratio of odds, *not* generally a risk ratio.
- **Risk ratio (RR):** compares probabilities/risks over a specified period.
- **Hazard ratio (HR):** ratio of hazards in time-to-event modeling; not an ordinary risk ratio.
- **Prevalence:** proportion with a condition at a specified time or period.
- **Incidence:** new cases or events over a defined interval and population at risk.

**Good spoken phrasing:**

> “The adjusted odds ratio was [ESTIMATE], with a 95% confidence interval of [LOWER] to [UPPER].”

Replace values only with verified results. Avoid “X times more likely” as a default paraphrase of an odds ratio when the outcome isn't rare, because odds and risk differ.

#### E4. How to report a null or inconclusive result

- “We didn't observe a clear association in this sample.”
- “The confidence interval was relatively wide, so the estimate was uncertain.”
- “The direction was similar across models, but the evidence was limited.”
- “We couldn't draw a firm conclusion from this analysis.”

You can report an inconclusive result calmly; don't invent stronger conclusions.

### F. Explaining limitations and difficult questions

#### F1. Limitation vs. bias vs. uncertainty

- **Limitation:** a constraint on the study or its interpretation, e.g., limited follow-up, smaller sample.
- **Bias:** systematic deviation that may affect an estimate or inference.
- **Uncertainty:** imprecision or incomplete knowledge, e.g., wide confidence intervals.

They overlap but do not mean exactly the same thing.

#### F2. Useful limitations language

- “One limitation is that...”
- “A potential source of bias is...”
- “We couldn't establish temporal ordering.”
- “That limits our ability to draw causal conclusions.”
- “The findings may not generalize to other populations.”
- “We had limited statistical power for that subgroup analysis.”
- “The small number of events makes those estimates unstable.”

**Generalizability** concerns transfer to other populations; **internal validity** concerns validity for the study population and design. They are not interchangeable.

#### F3. When you don't know why a variable was included

Professor: “Why was that covariate selected?”  
Student: “I wasn't involved in the original selection decision, so I wouldn't want to guess at the rationale. I can tell you what was included in the models, though.”

This is preferable to constructing an explanation after the fact.

#### F4. When challenged on a result

Professor: “Could the association reflect reverse causation?”  
Student: “That's a plausible concern given the study design. We couldn't establish which came first, so we'd need to be careful about a causal interpretation.”

Acknowledge a methodological issue; avoid instant agreement with unverified assumptions.

### G. Grammatical and pragmatic precision

#### G1. “Associated with,” not “associated to” in this use

> “X was **associated with** Y.”

Other patterns:

- “A relationship **between** X **and** Y.”
- “Differences **in** outcome **across** groups.”
- “An increase **in** the outcome.”
- “An association **of** X **with** Y.” — More formal noun construction.

#### G2. “Compared with/to” and “than”

> “The prevalence was higher **in group A than in group B**.”

> “**Compared with** group B, group A had a higher estimated prevalence.”

Both *compared with* and *compared to* occur; *compared with* is often preferred for analytic contrasts. Avoid dangling comparators:

Awkward: “Compared with group B, prevalence in group A...” can be valid, but be sure comparison units match.  
Clear: “Prevalence in group A was higher than prevalence in group B.”

#### G3. “Consistent with,” “support,” and “demonstrate”

- “Our findings are **consistent with** previous research.” — Compatible, not independently confirmed.
- “The results **support** the hypothesis.” — Some evidential support, calibrated to study quality.
- “The study **demonstrates**...” — Can sound stronger; specify exactly what was shown.

#### G4. Useful hedges with real meaning

- “may” / “might” — possibility.
- “appears to” — inference from available evidence.
- “suggests” — cautious evidential implication.
- “in this sample” — scope limited to analyzed sample.
- “under this model specification” — results conditional on analytical choices.

Avoid hedging every factual sentence. “We collected data from [N] participants” can be stated directly if verified.

### H. Realistic difficulty scenarios

**Scenario 1 — Professor asks for study design before the results.**

Professor: “Was that cross-sectional or longitudinal?”  
Student: “It was cross-sectional. Exposure and outcome were measured during the same study period, so we were cautious about the direction of the relationship.”

**Scenario 2 — Professor hears a causal claim.**

Professor: “So the exposure caused the outcome?”  
Student: “The data showed an association. With this design, we couldn't establish a causal effect.”

**Scenario 3 — Question about missing data.**

Professor: “How did you handle missing observations?”  
Student: “We used [ACTUAL APPROACH]. The extent and pattern of missingness were [VERIFIED DESCRIPTION]. I'd be careful about claiming the results are unaffected by missing data.”

**Scenario 4 — Asked for a specific statistic.**

Professor: “What was the adjusted odds ratio?”  
Student: “I don't have the exact number in front of me, so I'd prefer to check it rather than quote it incorrectly. The general pattern was [ONLY IF VERIFIED].”

**Scenario 5 — “Why didn't you use a randomized design?”**

Student: “The data came from an observational study, and random assignment wasn't part of the protocol. We therefore framed the analysis around associations and their limitations.”

**Scenario 6 — Professor spots a conflict between analyses.**

Professor: “Your main analysis and sensitivity analysis don't seem to agree.”  
Student: “That's an important point. I'd want to check the outcome definitions, included participants and model specifications before interpreting the difference.”

**Scenario 7 — You used a method but didn't develop it.**

Professor: “Did you work on the regression methodology itself?”  
Student: “I applied existing regression methods to the study question. My role was in the applied analysis rather than developing a new method.”

### I. Extended dialogue with branching responses

**Professor:** “Tell me about one study you've worked on.”  
**Student:** “Sure. The study examined whether [EXPOSURE] was associated with [OUTCOME] in [POPULATION]. It used [VERIFIED DESIGN], and my primary role was [MY RESPONSIBILITY].”  
**Professor:** “How did you analyze the data?”  
**Student:** “Our main question concerned [TYPE OF OUTCOME], so we used [ACTUAL MODEL]. We also considered [ACTUAL COVARIATES] based on [REAL BASIS].”  
**Professor:** “What did you find?”  
**Student:** “The main result was [VERIFIED FACTUAL RESULT]. I would describe it as an association, given the study design.”  
**Professor:** “What were the limitations?”  
**Student:** “Two stood out. First, [LIMITATION AND ITS CONSEQUENCE]. Second, [LIMITATION AND ITS CONSEQUENCE]. Those limitations affect how strongly we can interpret the finding.”  
**Professor:** “What would you do differently if you repeated it?”  
**Student:** “If resources allowed, I'd consider [FEASIBLE CHANGE] to address [ISSUE]. I'd also want to understand whether that would change the validity or feasibility of the study.”  
**Professor:** “That's a thoughtful answer.”  
**Student:** “Thank you. Working through those issues was one of the most useful parts of the project.”

**Branch A — Professor wants only the headline:**  
Professor: “Could you give me the takeaway in one sentence?”  
Student: “The study [WHAT WAS EXAMINED] and found [VERIFIED RESULT], with [MAJOR INTERPRETIVE CAVEAT].”

**Branch B — Professor asks what *you* contributed:**  
Professor: “Did you personally choose the model?”  
Student: “The initial analysis plan was [WHO DECIDED]. I was responsible for [YOUR VERIFIED ROLE], including [TASK].”

**Branch C — Professor questions the interpretation:**  
Professor: “I'm not sure that conclusion follows.”  
Student: “I see your concern. Could I ask which part of the inference you find least convincing? I may need to describe the limitation more carefully.”

### J. Reusable language and scientific integrity checklist

| Purpose | Expression |
|---|---|
| Introduce aim | “The primary aim was to examine whether...” |
| State design | “We conducted a [DESIGN] study.” |
| Describe data | “The analysis included data from...” |
| Define outcome | “Our primary outcome was defined as...” |
| State your role | “I was responsible for...” |
| Introduce approach | “To address that question, we used...” |
| Describe association | “We observed an association between...” |
| Give estimate | “The adjusted estimate was..., with a confidence interval of...” |
| Qualify results | “The estimate was imprecise.” |
| Interpret cautiously | “That limits the conclusions we can draw.” |
| Limit causal claim | “We couldn't establish a causal relationship from this design.” |
| Admit uncertainty | “I'd need to check the exact number.” |
| Invite follow-up | “I can go into the modeling decisions if that would be useful.” |

**Core principles:** Give the question and main result before an exhaustive list of methods. Distinguish sample, design, variables and model choices. State the actual estimate before evaluating it. Keep odds, risks, hazards, prevalence and incidence distinct. Don't turn a cross-sectional association into a causal finding. Report limitations calmly and accurately. Identify what you did personally and what the team did collectively.
