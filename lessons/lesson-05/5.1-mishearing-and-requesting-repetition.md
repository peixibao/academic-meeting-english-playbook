# Lesson 05 · Unexpected Situations & Recovery

## 5.1 Mishearing, Missing Words, and Requesting Repetition

**Learning objectives:** Recognize what went wrong in listening; distinguish not hearing from not understanding; request repetition naturally; target the missing word or phrase; verify names, numbers and technical terms; manage repeated listening difficulties without derailing an academic conversation.

**Connection to the fixed syllabus:** Lesson 01 introduced a few emergency phrases. Lesson 05 provides the full repair system. The reusable GitHub reference *Listening, clarification and repair* proposes three stages: ask for repetition → identify the unclear part → restate your interpretation before answering. This section develops the first two stages; Section 5.2 develops comprehension and meaning repair.

### A. Identify the listening problem before choosing the phrase

A useful distinction in **conversation repair（会话修复）** is between not receiving the words clearly and not grasping their meaning. Both are normal in academic meetings.

| What happened? | Appropriate first response | Why |
|---|---|---|
| You didn't hear a word | “Sorry, I didn't quite catch that.” | Signals a listening gap |
| You heard only the beginning | “I caught the first part, but missed the end.” | Narrows the missing stretch |
| You heard the sentence, but lost the reasoning | “I didn't quite follow that last point.” | Requests help with meaning; developed in 5.2 |
| The speaker spoke too quickly | “Could you say that a little more slowly?” | Targets speed |
| You heard an unfamiliar acronym | “Could you repeat the acronym?” | Targets a specific item |
| The audio disconnected | “The audio cut out after ‘sample size.’” | Identifies a technical cause; developed in 5.4 |

**Example:**

Professor: “We used a generalized estimating equation to account for clustering.”  
Student: “Sorry, I didn't quite catch the name of the method. Could you say it again?”  
Professor: “Generalized estimating equations—GEE.”  
Student: “GEE, got it. Thank you.”

The student asks only for the missing information, then allows the conversation to continue.

#### A1. Listening trouble is not a single category

Three increasingly specific descriptions:

- “Sorry, I missed that.” — A general listening problem.
- “Sorry, I missed the **last part**.” — Identifies the location.
- “Sorry, did you say the **primary** or **secondary** outcome?” — Identifies the exact ambiguity.

The more specific your request, the easier it is for the other speaker to repair the problem quickly.

### B. Catch, hear, make out, miss, and get

These verbs are related, but they are not interchangeable in every context.

#### B1. “I didn't quite catch that”

A natural American English expression for not hearing or processing what someone just said.

- “Sorry, I didn't quite catch that.”
- “I didn't catch the last word.”
- “I caught most of it, but I missed the name of the software.”

**Quite** softens the statement. You can omit it: “Sorry, I didn't catch that.”

**Past tense** is natural because the missed speech has just occurred: *didn't catch*. For ongoing difficulty, use the present progressive: “I'm having trouble catching what you're saying because the connection is breaking up.”

#### B2. “I couldn't hear you”

This emphasizes an **audibility problem**: volume, microphone, background noise, or connection.

- “Sorry, I couldn't hear the last part.”
- “I can hear you now, but your audio was cutting out.”
- “Could you speak a little louder?”

If you heard every word but couldn't understand the reasoning, “I couldn't hear” inaccurately describes the problem; use “I didn't follow” or a targeted clarification request instead.

#### B3. “I couldn't make that out”

**Make something out** can mean distinguish sounds or details despite difficulty.

> “Sorry, I couldn't quite make out the name of the dataset.”

It often suggests indistinct sound, noise or unclear articulation. “Make out” has other meanings outside this context; here it simply concerns recognizing what was said.

#### B4. “I missed...”

Very flexible and conversational:

- “I missed the last sentence.”
- “Sorry, I missed what you said after ‘recruitment.’”
- “I think I missed one of the numbers.”

“Missed” need not blame the speaker, although the situation may explain why it happened.

#### B5. “I didn't get that”

This may mean either **I didn't hear it** or **I didn't understand it**.

To remove ambiguity, add a noun:

- “I didn't get the **last word**.” — Listening.
- “I didn't get the **distinction between the two models**.” — Understanding.

In academic conversation, specific wording is preferable when the difference matters.

### C. Requesting repetition: natural alternatives and register

| Expression | Register | Typical use |
|---|---|---|
| “Sorry, could you say that again?” | Neutral-professional | Universal default |
| “Could you repeat that, please?” | Neutral-professional | Direct, polite |
| “Sorry, one more time?” | Conversational | Quick repair |
| “Could you say the last part again?” | Neutral-professional | Targeted repetition |
| “Could you run that by me again?” | Friendly-professional | Repeat or explain an idea; informal |
| “I missed that—could you repeat it?” | Neutral-professional | Identifies the problem |
| “Come again?” | Informal and tone-sensitive | Very short request; can sound abrupt |
| “Pardon?” | Correct, can sound formal or regionally marked | Single-word repair |
| “Sorry?” | Common in conversation, intonation-dependent | Very brief request |

#### C1. Why “Sorry, could you say that again?” is a strong default

It combines a small acknowledgment of trouble with a clear action request. In spoken professional English it is simple, natural, and sufficiently polite.

> “Sorry, I didn't quite catch that. Could you say it again?”

This is also the answer to a common real-world question: **“抱歉，我没听清，可以再说一遍吗？”**

#### C2. “What?” and “What did you say?”

Both are grammatical. Bare “What?” may be perfectly normal among familiar people, yet it can sound abrupt if delivered sharply. “What did you say?” can signal genuine nonhearing, but in some contexts it may sound confrontational or surprised.

With an unfamiliar professor, add a contextual marker:

- “Sorry, what was that last part?”
- “Sorry, could you repeat that?”

#### C3. “Once more,” “one more time,” and “again”

These are common alternatives; you can say:

- “Could you say it **again**?”
- “Could you say it **one more time**?”
- “Could you say that **once more**?”

“Once more” can sound slightly more deliberate. “One more time” is very common in American conversation.

### D. Grammar and the scope of repetition

#### D1. Ask someone to repeat something

**Patterns:**

- “Could you **repeat** that?”
- “Could you **say** that again?”
- “Could I ask you to **repeat** the last word?”
- “Would you mind **repeating** that?”

Notice **would you mind + -ing**, already introduced in Lesson 03.2.

Avoid: “Could you repeat again it?”  
Prefer: “Could you repeat it?” / “Could you say it again?”

“Repeat that again” is heard in everyday English but contains a redundant idea; “repeat that” or “say that again” is more concise.

#### D2. “Say that again” vs. “go over that again”

**Say it again** often requests essentially the same words.

**Go over it again** often requests another walkthrough or review, perhaps with added explanation.

Compare:

Professor: “The meeting is on the fifteenth.”  
Student: “Sorry, could you **say the date again**?”

Professor: “We handled clustering by changing the variance estimation.”  
Student: “Could you **go over the reasoning again**?”

This second request focuses more on re-explaining the idea. Neither meaning is rigid; context matters.

#### D3. “What did you say after...?”

Use this when you remember a precise anchor:

> “Sorry, what did you say **after ‘adjusted model’**?”

Or:

> “I caught the part about recruitment, but I missed what came next.”

This is usually more efficient than asking for the entire paragraph to be repeated.

#### D4. Repetition vs. rephrasing

“Could you **repeat** that?” asks for the same information again.

“Could you **rephrase** that?” asks for it in different words.

“Could you **explain** that?” asks for more meaning or detail.

Choose *rephrase/explain* when repeating the same words is unlikely to solve the problem. Section 5.2 addresses this distinction fully.

### E. Capturing names, numbers, acronyms, and technical terms

Academic meetings contain information that is difficult to infer from context. Specific confirmation is valuable.

#### E1. Names and spelling

- “Sorry, could you spell the surname?”
- “Did you say Dr. **Miller** or Dr. **Milner**?”
- “Could I check the spelling of that?”

**Spell** asks for the letters of a word. “How do you spell that?” is natural and not impolite.

#### E2. Numbers, dates, and units

- “Did you say **fifteen** or **fifty**?”
- “Was that **0.05** or **0.5**?”
- “Just to confirm, you said **three months**, right?”
- “Was that **milligrams** or **micrograms**?”

If it affects a decision or scientific claim, repeat the full number **with its units**.

#### E3. Acronyms

Professor: “We used GEE for the repeated observations.”  
Student: “Sorry, was that **G-E-E**? Could you remind me what it stands for?”

Two separate requests are possible: (1) confirm the letters; (2) request the meaning. Confusing them can cause unnecessary repetition.

#### E4. Asking someone to type a term in the chat

For video meetings:

> “Would you mind putting the acronym in the chat? I want to make sure I have it right.”

A useful fallback if pronunciation alone hasn't resolved the ambiguity.

#### E5. Partial repetition as a repair strategy

Professor: “It's a nested case-control analysis.”  
Student: “A **nested case-control** analysis?”  
Professor: “Exactly.”

Repeating a key expression with a questioning intonation can efficiently invite confirmation. For important details, an explicit “Did I get that right?” may be clearer.

### F. Asking for slower or clearer speech

#### F1. Speed

- “Could you speak a little more slowly?”
- “Would you mind slowing down a little?”
- “Sorry, I lost the last part. Could you go over it a little more slowly?”

“A little” softens the request. “Please speak slowly” is grammatical but may sound more like an instruction in an ordinary one-to-one exchange.

#### F2. Volume and microphone

- “Sorry, you're coming through quite quietly. Could you turn up your microphone?”
- “I'm having a little trouble hearing you. Would you mind speaking up?”
- “Could you move a little closer to the mic?”

**Speak up** usually means speak louder; it can also mean voice your opinion in other contexts.

#### F3. Accent and pronunciation

When a speaker's accent is unfamiliar, avoid turning your repair request into an evaluation of their speech. Target the content:

> “Sorry, I missed the name of the method. Could you say it once more?”

or the pace:

> “Could we go through that last sentence a little more slowly?”

The same strategy works irrespective of which variety of English anyone speaks.

### G. A three-stage recovery strategy

The fixed course reference recommends an escalating sequence.

#### Stage 1 — Simple repetition

> “Sorry, I didn't quite catch that. Could you say it again?”

#### Stage 2 — Target the unresolved part

> “I caught the first part about recruitment, but missed the number of sites.”

#### Stage 3 — Verify the meaning before proceeding

> “So there were eight participating sites in total—is that right?”

If the words are clear but their meaning isn't, switch to Section 5.2's rephrasing or explanation strategies.

#### G1. Avoid an endless loop of identical repetition requests

If you ask twice and still cannot distinguish a word, change modality or technique:

- “Could you spell that?”
- “Could you type the term in the chat?”
- “Could you explain what the acronym stands for?”
- “Could I check whether I understood the key point correctly?”

#### G2. Recover and move on

Short responses once the issue is resolved:

- “Got it. Thanks.”
- “Ah, that makes sense.”
- “Thank you—that's much clearer.”
- “Right, I have it now.”

You don't need a long apology every time you request repetition.

### H. Unexpected situations: branching dialogues

#### Scenario 1 — You miss the final word

Professor: “The primary outcome was measured at six...”  
Student: “Sorry, I missed the last word. Could you repeat it?”  
Professor: “Six months.”  
Student: “Six months. Got it.”

#### Scenario 2 — You confuse two numbers

Professor: “We recruited fifty participants in the pilot.”  
Student: “Sorry, did you say fifteen or fifty?”  
Professor: “Fifty—five-zero.”  
Student: “Thanks for confirming.”

#### Scenario 3 — You hear an unfamiliar technical term

Professor: “We used inverse probability weighting.”  
Student: “Sorry, could you repeat the name of the approach?”  
Professor: “Inverse probability weighting.”  
Student: “Thank you. Could you also explain briefly how it was used in this study?”

The first request repairs the sound; the second repairs understanding.

#### Scenario 4 — The professor speaks quickly

Professor: “We screened records, removed duplicates, applied eligibility criteria, and then invited participants for assessment.”  
Student: “Sorry, could you go over those steps a little more slowly?”  
Professor: “Sure. First we screened records...”  
Student: “Thank you.”

#### Scenario 5 — A surname is unclear

Professor: “You might want to read the review by Milner.”  
Student: “Could I check the author's surname? Was that Milner?”  
Professor: “Yes, M-I-L-N-E-R.”  
Student: “Perfect, thank you.”

#### Scenario 6 — The speaker repeats something and it's still unclear

Professor: “The acronym is MICE.”  
Student: “Sorry, I didn't quite catch the letters. Could you spell them?”  
Professor: “M-I-C-E.”  
Student: “Thank you. Multiple imputation by chained equations, right?”  
Professor: “That's right.”

#### Scenario 7 — You're uncertain whether the issue is audio or comprehension

Professor: “That decision follows from the identifiability assumptions.”  
Student: “I heard ‘identifiability assumptions,’ but I don't think I followed the implication. Could you explain that link?”  
Professor: “Sure—the key is that...”

Accurately naming the problem saves time.

#### Scenario 8 — A meeting chair moves on too quickly

Chair: “Great, let's move to the next item.”  
Student: “Before we move on, could I check one number from the previous slide?”  
Chair: “Sure.”  
Student: “Was the follow-up period six or sixteen weeks?”  
Chair: “Sixteen weeks.”  
Student: “Thanks.”

### I. Extended academic conversation: repairing a missed explanation

Professor: “We've had two cohorts, and in the newer cohort we used a different instrument to measure sleep timing.”  
Student: “I see.”  
Professor: “The earlier questionnaire wasn't directly comparable to the newer diary-based measure.”  
Student: “Sorry, I missed the name of the second measure. Could you say it again?”  
Professor: “Diary-based sleep timing.”  
Student: “Diary-based sleep timing—thank you.”  
Professor: “Exactly. We then compared the distributions by age.”  
Student: “Could I check one detail? Did you compare the distributions within each cohort or pool both cohorts together?”  
Professor: “Within each cohort first, then in a combined sensitivity analysis.”  
Student: “Got it. So the initial comparison was cohort-specific.”  
Professor: “That's right.”  
Student: “Thanks—that clears it up.”

**Teaching analysis:**

| Student expression | Communication function |
|---|---|
| “I see.” | Listener feedback; does not claim the floor |
| “I missed the name...” | Targeted listening repair |
| “Could you say it again?” | Request repetition |
| Repeats “Diary-based sleep timing” | Checks accurate capture |
| “Could I check one detail?” | Moves into a substantive clarification |
| “So the initial comparison...” | Verifies interpretation |
| “That clears it up.” | Marks resolution and returns to normal discussion |

### J. Reference bank and core principles

#### J1. Choose the right request quickly

| Need | Recommended wording |
|---|---|
| General repetition | “Sorry, I didn't quite catch that. Could you say it again?” |
| Last part | “I missed the last part. Could you repeat it?” |
| Specific term | “Could you repeat the name of the method?” |
| Spelling | “Could you spell that for me?” |
| Number | “Did you say fifteen or fifty?” |
| Speed | “Could you go over that a little more slowly?” |
| Full explanation | “Could you go over the reasoning again?” |
| Word or meaning unclear | “I heard the term, but could you explain what it means here?” |
| Verification | “So the interval was six weeks—is that correct?” |
| Resolution | “Got it. Thanks for clarifying.” |

#### J2. Key distinctions

1. **Catch / hear**: usually describe missing sounds or words.
2. **Follow / understand**: usually describe missing the reasoning or meaning.
3. **Repeat**: say the words again; **rephrase**: use different words; **explain**: add meaning or detail.
4. **Go over**: often means review or walk through, not necessarily verbatim repetition.
5. Specific requests are usually more efficient than general ones.
6. Verification is especially important for names, numbers, acronyms, dates and units.
7. Polite requests can be short; a repeated apology is unnecessary.
8. If repetition doesn't solve the issue, change the repair strategy.
