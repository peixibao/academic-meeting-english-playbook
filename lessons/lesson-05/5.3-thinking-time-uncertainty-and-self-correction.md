# Lesson 05 · Unexpected Situations & Recovery

## 5.3 Thinking Time, Uncertainty, Self-Correction, and Misunderstanding

**Learning objectives:** Respond when a question catches you off guard; request thinking time without losing the conversation; state uncertainty accurately; distinguish unfamiliarity from a lack of experience; repair a mistaken answer; correct your own wording; and recover from a misunderstanding without pretending to know more than you do.

**Connection to earlier lessons:** 4.1 explained how to hold the speaking turn. Here we focus on the **unexpected need to pause, rethink, correct, or revise your answer**. Lesson 07 will treat deeper disagreement, boundaries, and negotiation separately.

### A. A question catches you off guard

A prospective supervisor might ask:

> “Which part of your previous project would you approach differently if you were starting again?”

You may understand the question perfectly but need a moment to think. This is a different problem from mishearing (5.1) or failing to follow an explanation (5.2).

#### A1. Four situations to separate

| Problem | Useful response | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Need time to think | “That's a good question. Let me think for a moment.” | Signals genuine reflection |
| Need to organize a complicated answer | “There are a couple of ways I could approach that.” | Previews structure |
| Don't know the answer | “I'm not sure, actually.” | Avoids false certainty |
| Misunderstood the question | “Oh, I see—I thought you were asking about the data, not the timeline.” | Repairs interpretation |

These strategies serve different functions. Saying “Could you repeat the question?” will not solve a problem that is actually about formulating an answer.

#### A2. A short pause is normal

You can pause silently for a moment. You don't have to fill every gap with *um, actually, basically,* or *you know*. A brief planning signal may help if the pause is longer than expected:

> “Let me think about how best to explain that.”

This is natural professional speech, especially when the question requires analysis.

### B. Asking for thinking time

#### B1. Common expressions and nuance

| Expression | Typical effect | Register |
|---|---|---|
| “Let me think for a second.” | Short thinking pause | Conversational |
| “Let me think about that for a moment.” | Deliberate consideration | Professional |
| “That's a good question.” | Acknowledgment; may precede thought | Neutral-professional |
| “That's an interesting question.” | Indicates intellectual interest | Professional |
| “Let me gather my thoughts.” | Organize a complex response | Professional |
| “Give me a second to think.” | Direct, informal | Friendly-professional |
| “I'd need a moment to think that through.” | Need to reason through a problem | Professional |
| “Let me see how to put this.” | Search for suitable wording | Conversational |

Use “That's a good question” sincerely; if you repeat it before every answer, it can begin to sound like automatic filler.

#### B2. “Think about” vs. “think through”

**Think about** = consider a question, idea or possibility.

> “Let me think about that for a moment.”

**Think something through** = examine the steps, implications, or consequences carefully.

> “I'd need a little time to think that through.”

The second may signal that the issue is complex enough to require reasoning, not just a momentary recall.

#### B3. “Let me gather my thoughts”

**Gather my thoughts** = organize what you want to say.

Professor: “How would you prioritize the different objectives?”  
Student: “Let me gather my thoughts for a second. I think I'd start by distinguishing the primary research question from the secondary objectives.”

It can sound more deliberate than “Um... wait...” without being theatrical.

#### B4. “Off the top of my head”

This idiom means **without careful preparation or checking**.

> “Off the top of my head, I can think of two possibilities.”

It signals that your answer is preliminary. For numerical facts, scientific results, or institutional rules, don't use the phrase as permission to guess recklessly. State what you can recall and note if verification is needed.

#### B5. The difference between planning and stalling

A planning phrase should lead to an answer or a clear acknowledgment of uncertainty.

Natural:

> “Let me think for a moment. The main issue might be feasibility, particularly the data requirements.”

Less effective if prolonged:

> “Well... you know... that's a really good question... so, basically... what I'm trying to say is...”

You can take time without producing a stream of fillers.

### C. Expressing uncertainty accurately

#### C1. Degrees of certainty

| Expression | Typical strength | Example |
|---|---|---|
| “I'm certain that...” | Very high | “I'm certain that the study was cross-sectional.” |
| “I'm fairly confident that...” | Substantial, not absolute | “I'm fairly confident we used that cutoff.” |
| “I believe...” | Personal assessment or recollection | “I believe that was the primary endpoint.” |
| “As far as I recall...” | Memory limitation | “As far as I recall, the analysis used logistic regression.” |
| “I think...” | Tentative opinion or belief | “I think that might be relevant.” |
| “I'm not entirely sure...” | Explicit uncertainty | “I'm not entirely sure how the procedure works.” |
| “I don't know.” | No reliable answer | “I don't know the exact figure.” |

“I'm not sure” is an ordinary professional answer, especially when it is followed by a useful distinction or an appropriate next step.

#### C2. “I don't know” vs. “I'm not sure”

**I don't know** directly states lack of knowledge.  
**I'm not sure** leaves open the possibility that you have a tentative impression but insufficient confidence.

Professor: “How many subjects were excluded from the final analysis?”  
Student: “I don't know the exact number offhand. I'd need to check the final report.”

**Offhand** = without checking or planning. Here, it describes immediate recall.

#### C3. “As far as I know” vs. “as far as I recall”

- “As far as I **know**, the program accepts applications in the fall.” — Limited current knowledge.
- “As far as I **recall**, the paper reported that result in Table 2.” — Limited memory of earlier information.

When it is important, confirm current dates, formal requirements and exact statistics rather than relying on recall.

#### C4. “I wouldn't want to speculate”

This communicates that an answer would be unsupported.

> “I wouldn't want to speculate about the recruitment numbers without checking the records.”

It's a useful response when accuracy matters. It can be followed by a commitment you can actually fulfill, such as checking a source after the meeting.

#### C5. “I don't want to give you the wrong impression”

Use this when you worry that your wording might suggest an inaccurate level of experience or certainty:

> “I don't want to give you the wrong impression: I understand the basic idea, but I haven't implemented that method myself.”

This is especially valuable when a professor draws an inference from your résumé or from a previous answer.

### D. No firsthand experience: honest yet informative replies

A professor may ask whether you've personally used a particular method or performed a specific task.

#### D1. Four distinct levels

| Reality | Natural wording |
|---|---|
| No knowledge | “I'm not familiar with that method yet.” |
| Conceptual knowledge only | “I'm familiar with the general idea, but I haven't used it myself.” |
| Limited direct experience | “I've had some exposure to it, though my hands-on experience is limited.” |
| Genuine working experience | “I've used it to analyze ...” (only if true) |

**Have exposure to** is a common professional expression meaning have some contact with, training in, or acquaintance with a subject. It does not prove technical proficiency.

#### D2. “Not yet” has a specific implication

> “I haven't used that software yet.”

**Yet** can suggest that using it remains possible or expected in the future. It doesn't necessarily promise that you'll learn it.

If the technique is outside your interests and you don't want to imply interest, simply say:

> “I haven't used that software.”

#### D3. Avoid inventing proficiency through polite enthusiasm

A response such as “I'm sure I could master that quickly” makes a prediction about your future ability. Use it only if you have a basis for believing it.

A more precise alternative:

> “I haven't worked with that approach directly. Could you tell me which skills would be needed for the project?”

#### D4. “I'm familiar with” vs. “I'm comfortable with”

- **Familiar with X** = you recognize or understand X to some degree.
- **Comfortable with X** = you feel capable or at ease using or discussing X.

Someone can be familiar with a method but not comfortable implementing it independently. This distinction matters in a research interview.

### E. Self-correction: repairing your own words

Speakers regularly notice that something they said was incomplete, inaccurate or misleading. In conversation analysis, this is called **self-repair（自我修复）**.

#### E1. Useful self-correction phrases

- “Sorry, I meant...”
- “Let me correct that.”
- “Actually, what I meant was...”
- “Let me rephrase that.”
- “More precisely...”
- “To be more specific...”
- “I should clarify...”
- “I misspoke.”

**Misspeak** means say something incorrectly, for example an incorrect number or the wrong word.

#### E2. Wrong word or number

Student: “The sample included four hundred participants—sorry, **three** hundred.”

In informal speech, such immediate corrections are natural. If the value is critical, say the full corrected value clearly:

> “Sorry, let me correct that: the sample included **three hundred participants**.”

#### E3. Clarifying scope

Student: “I worked on the recruitment—actually, to be more precise, I helped with participant coordination, while another team member managed the recruitment process.”

This is a factual clarification of roles, not an apology for being involved.

#### E4. Correcting overstatement

Student: “The model demonstrated a causal effect—sorry, I should be more precise. We observed an association, and the study design doesn't by itself establish causality.”

This illustrates correcting an academic overstatement. Scientific precision matters more than maintaining the appearance of a perfectly smooth answer.

#### E5. “Actually” — placement and tone

**Actually** can correct your own wording, gently challenge an assumption, or introduce new information. Tone determines whether it feels helpful or sharp.

- “Actually, let me correct that number.” — Self-repair.
- “Actually, I was referring to a different study.” — Clarification.
- “Actually, that's wrong.” — Can feel confrontational if directed at another person.

For self-correction, *actually* is common. For correcting another person's statement, add the specific correction and appropriate framing.

### F. Repairing a misunderstanding between speakers

Sometimes the other person interprets your answer differently from what you intended—or you interpret their question incorrectly.

#### F1. You answered a different question

Professor: “I was asking about your **role** in the study, rather than the overall design.”  
Student: “Oh, I see. I misunderstood the question. My main role was preparing the statistical analysis.”

Useful responses:

- “Oh, I see—I misunderstood what you were asking.”
- “Thanks for clarifying. In that case...”
- “I see, you mean my specific contribution.”
- “Right, I was answering a slightly different question.”

#### F2. Your comment is interpreted too broadly

Professor: “So you're willing to work on any type of project?”  
Student: “Let me clarify: I'm open to exploring different research fields, but I'd still want to understand the project and decide whether it aligns with my interests.”

This repair matters because **openness to exploration is different from agreeing to any work assignment**.

#### F3. Someone attributes a claim you didn't make

Professor: “Are you saying that the results are unreliable?”  
Student: “I should clarify. I was pointing to a limitation in interpretation, not saying that the results are unusable.”

This is direct and respectful. A strong clarification can protect the substance of your contribution.

#### F4. “What I was trying to say is...”

This phrase allows you to restate an idea after a failed explanation:

> “What I was trying to say is that the recruitment strategy could affect the generalizability of the findings.”

It doesn't imply your entire earlier statement was mistaken; it signals a communication repair.

#### F5. “I see where the confusion came from”

A conversational way to recognize why two interpretations occurred.

> “I see where the confusion came from. When I said ‘independent,’ I meant independent analysis, not working without supervision.”

This can be useful when your own wording created ambiguity.

### G. Recovery strategies under pressure

#### G1. A three-part response to a difficult question

**Acknowledge → state what you know → mark the remaining uncertainty.**

Professor: “Why was that covariate chosen?”  
Student: “That's a useful question. My understanding is that it was included because of the conceptual model, though I'd need to check the protocol to confirm the original rationale.”

Use this response only if the stated rationale is actually supported by your understanding; otherwise say you don't know.

#### G2. “I'd need to check” vs. “I'll check”

- “I'd **need to check** the final report.” — States what is required for a reliable answer.
- “I'll **check** the final report and email you the figure.” — Makes a future commitment.

Do not casually promise a follow-up you cannot deliver. If a follow-up is needed, agree on what you'll send and when.

#### G3. “Can I come back to that?”

This can mean you'd like to address the question later in the same meeting.

> “That's an important point. Could I come back to it after we've discussed the study design?”

The phrase may also sound as though you're postponing an answer; offer a reason when helpful.

#### G4. “Let me start with...”

If you can answer only part of a large question:

> “Let me start with the part I know best, which is the data analysis.”

This is clearer than improvising on aspects you don't understand.

#### G5. When you're corrected in front of others

Researcher: “Actually, that estimate came from the sensitivity analysis.”  
You: “You're right—thanks for catching that. Let me correct the distinction.”

If the correction is accurate, acknowledge and incorporate it. If you disagree on the underlying facts, a clarifying question is appropriate:

> “Could we check which model that figure came from? I may be looking at a different table.”

No need to turn a correctable detail into a social crisis.

### H. Unexpected situations: branching dialogues

#### Scenario 1 — Asked for an opinion you haven't formed

Professor: “Which research direction would you prioritize?”  
Student: “Let me think for a moment. Off the top of my head, I can see two possibilities, but I'd want to learn more about their feasibility.”  
Professor: “That's fair.”

#### Scenario 2 — Exact figure not available from memory

Professor: “What was the response rate?”  
Student: “I don't know the exact percentage offhand, and I wouldn't want to guess. I'd need to check the study report.”  
Professor: “Of course.”

#### Scenario 3 — No firsthand software experience

Professor: “Have you used SAS?”  
Student: “I haven't used SAS directly. My hands-on experience has mainly been with other tools, so I'd need training if that were required.”  
Professor: “Good to know.”

#### Scenario 4 — You realize you named the wrong variable

Student: “We adjusted for income—sorry, I meant **parental education**, not income.”  
Professor: “Okay.”  
Student: “That was the variable included in the model.”

#### Scenario 5 — The professor misunderstood your interest

Professor: “So you'd be happy to take on a wet-lab project?”  
Student: “I should clarify my earlier wording. I'm open to learning about different research areas, but my current preference is for clinical and population-based research.”  
Professor: “I see.”

#### Scenario 6 — You misheard the question's scope

Professor: “I'm asking how you evaluated the model, not how you selected the predictors.”  
Student: “Oh, I see—I misunderstood. In terms of model evaluation...”

#### Scenario 7 — Another person corrects your description

Researcher: “That wasn't the primary analysis; it was exploratory.”  
Student: “Thank you for correcting me. I should distinguish the primary analysis from the exploratory work.”

#### Scenario 8 — The question requires more time than the meeting allows

Professor: “Could you explain the derivation of that estimator?”  
Student: “I can explain the broad intuition, but I'd need to review the derivation to give you an accurate step-by-step explanation. Would it be useful if I started with the intuition?”  
Professor: “Yes, please.”

### I. Extended dialogue: a surprising question and two repairs

Professor: “What was the most important limitation of your project?”  
Student: “That's a good question. Let me think for a moment.”  
*Brief pause.*  
Student: “I would say the main limitation was the relatively small sample for one subgroup.”  
Professor: “How did you handle that statistically?”  
Student: “We used a weighted analysis—sorry, let me correct that. We did **not** have validated sampling weights. We reported unweighted estimates and discussed the limitation.”  
Professor: “Thanks for clarifying. And did you run a sensitivity analysis?”  
Student: “I want to make sure I answer accurately. I can describe the analyses I ran, but I'd need to check which one was designated as the sensitivity analysis in the final report.”  
Professor: “That's fine. What did you run?”  
Student: “We compared alternative definitions of the outcome and examined how the estimates changed.”  
Professor: “I see. Was that part of the primary analysis plan?”  
Student: “I may have misunderstood your earlier question. If you're asking whether those comparisons were prespecified, I'd need to check the protocol before claiming that.”  
Professor: “Yes, that's what I meant.”  
Student: “Thanks for clarifying the distinction.”

**This dialogue is a hypothetical language exercise, not a factual claim about any named user's project.**

| Student move | Function |
|---|---|
| “Let me think...” | Request thinking time |
| “Sorry, let me correct that.” | Self-repair of inaccurate terminology |
| “We did not have validated weights.” | Correct substance rather than bluff |
| “I want to answer accurately...” | Mark epistemic responsibility |
| “I may have misunderstood...” | Repair interpretation |
| “I'd need to check the protocol...” | Avoid unsupported assertion |

### J. Functional phrase bank and core principles

#### J1. Reusable expressions

| Need | Expression |
|---|---|
| Brief thinking time | “Let me think for a second.” |
| Careful consideration | “I'd need a moment to think that through.” |
| Organize thoughts | “Let me gather my thoughts.” |
| Preliminary answer | “Off the top of my head...” |
| Not sure | “I'm not entirely sure about that.” |
| Don't know exact fact | “I don't know the exact figure offhand.” |
| Memory limitation | “As far as I recall...” |
| No unsupported guess | “I wouldn't want to speculate.” |
| No direct experience | “I'm familiar with the idea, but I haven't used it myself.” |
| Correct your words | “Sorry, I meant...” |
| Correct a fact | “Let me correct that.” |
| Rephrase | “What I meant was...” / “Let me rephrase that.” |
| Improve precision | “More precisely...” |
| Correct the question interpretation | “Oh, I see—I misunderstood what you were asking.” |
| Prevent misinterpretation | “I should clarify...” |
| Differentiate check vs. promise | “I'd need to check...” / “I'll check and get back to you.” |
| Postpone within the meeting | “Could I come back to that in a moment?” |

#### J2. Core principles

1. Needing time to think is different from needing a question repeated.
2. A short, natural planning phrase is usually enough.
3. Separate what you **know**, **remember tentatively**, and **don't know**.
4. Don't fabricate numbers, experience, certainty, methods or future plans.
5. Correct imprecise wording as soon as you notice it.
6. Use “I misunderstood the question” when you answered a different question; then answer the intended one.
7. Distinguish genuine interest from a commitment, and familiarity from hands-on proficiency.
8. “I'd need to check” describes a condition; “I'll check” makes a promise.
9. An accurate correction is more valuable than a superficially flawless answer.
