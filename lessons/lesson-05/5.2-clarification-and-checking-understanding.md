# Lesson 05 · Unexpected Situations & Recovery

## 5.2 Not Following, Asking for Clarification, and Checking Understanding

**Learning objectives:** Distinguish hearing words from understanding ideas; request rephrasing, examples, and explanations; locate a specific logical gap; clarify ambiguous terminology; paraphrase a speaker's intended meaning; and handle repeated misunderstandings in professional academic conversations.

**Connection to 5.1:** Section 5.1 repairs missing *sounds and words*. This section repairs missing *meaning and logic*. The original GitHub reference includes “Could you rephrase the question?” and “Just to make sure I've got this right...” as important recovery tools.

### A. “I heard the words, but I didn't follow the point”

Consider a professor saying:

> “We decided against including that variable because adjusting for a collider could introduce bias.”

You may have heard every word correctly and still not understand the statistical reasoning. Repeating the same sentence may not help. You need a question about the **relationship between ideas**.

#### A1. Three listening-comprehension states

| Situation | What you can say | What it asks for |
|---|---|---|
| You missed the words | “I didn't catch the last sentence.” | Repetition |
| You heard them but don't know a term | “Could you explain what ‘collider’ means here?” | Definition or context |
| You know the words but lost the argument | “I didn't quite follow why including that variable would introduce bias.” | Reasoning |

The third is often the most academically productive because it tells the speaker exactly where their explanation stopped making sense to you.

#### A2. “I didn't quite follow that”

Natural professional American English:

- “Sorry, I didn't quite follow that.”
- “I think I lost you on the last step.”
- “I'm following so far, but I'm not sure how that conclusion follows.”
- “I'm with you up to the part about recruitment. Could you explain what happens next?”

**Follow** in this context means understand a sequence of ideas.

Compare:

- **“I didn't catch that.”** — I missed the sound/words.
- **“I didn't follow that.”** — I couldn't track the meaning or logic.
- **“I'm lost.”** — Informal, means confused; a bit broad.
- **“You lost me at...”** — Conversational idiom locating the point where understanding broke down.

#### A3. “You lost me at...” — register caution

> “You lost me at the part about the second model.”

This is a natural idiom, meaning “I was following until that point.” It can sound light or teasing in a casual meeting. For a first meeting with a professor, a slightly more neutral alternative is:

> “I was following up to the second model, but I'm not sure I understood the next step.”

Either can work with an appropriately friendly tone.

### B. Repeat, rephrase, clarify, explain, elaborate, walk through

These verbs specify **the kind of help you need**.

| Verb/phrase | Typical request | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Repeat** | Say the words again | “Could you repeat the last sentence?” |
| **Rephrase** | Express the same idea in other words | “Could you rephrase the question?” |
| **Clarify** | Remove ambiguity or uncertainty | “Could you clarify what you mean by ‘independent’?” |
| **Explain** | Provide meaning or reasoning | “Could you explain why that model was chosen?” |
| **Elaborate on** | Add information or detail | “Could you elaborate on the recruitment process?” |
| **Walk me through** | Explain sequentially | “Could you walk me through the analysis steps?” |
| **Give an example** | Make an abstract point concrete | “Could you give me an example of what that would involve?” |
| **Break down** | Divide a complex issue into smaller parts | “Could you break that down a little?” |

#### B1. “Could you rephrase that?”

Professor: “Which estimand is most appropriate for the treatment-policy strategy?”  
Student: “I understand the general topic, but could you rephrase the question?”  
Professor: “Sure. I'm asking which treatment effect we want to estimate if participants discontinue treatment.”

Rephrasing can make the task clearer without adding an entirely new topic.

#### B2. “Could you elaborate on that?”

This often asks for **more detail** about a point you already partly understand.

Professor: “Our group collaborates with a clinical research network.”  
Student: “Could you elaborate on what that collaboration looks like for students?”

**Grammar:** *elaborate on + noun / topic* is the common pattern.

- Natural: “Could you elaborate **on** that?”
- Natural: “Could you elaborate a little?”
- Less natural in this use: “Could you elaborate me that?”

#### B3. “Could you walk me through...?”

**Walk someone through something** = explain the sequence step by step.

- “Could you walk me through the main steps?”
- “Would you mind walking me through the workflow?”
- “Could you walk me through how you approached the analysis?”

This expression is common across American academic, medical, and workplace environments.

#### B4. “Could you break that down?”

**Break something down** = divide an explanation into manageable parts.

> “I'm not familiar with that framework yet. Could you break it down a little?”

Friendly-professional. In a more formal context, “Could you explain the main components?” is a suitable alternative.

### C. Locating the exact uncertainty

The best clarification requests identify whether the difficulty concerns a **term, step, assumption, comparison, scope, or implication**.

#### C1. Clarify terminology

- “What does ‘feasible’ mean in this context?”
- “When you say ‘validation,’ are you referring to internal or external validation?”
- “How are you defining ‘exposure’ in this study?”
- “Is that term being used in its statistical sense?”

A term can have different definitions in different disciplines. Asking *in this context* avoids assuming a single universal meaning.

#### C2. Clarify a step or process

- “I'm with you up to the data-cleaning stage. What happens after that?”
- “Could you explain the step between recruitment and follow-up?”
- “I understand the first model, but how did you move to the second?”

#### C3. Clarify an assumption

- “What assumption does that conclusion depend on?”
- “Are we assuming that those measurements are comparable?”
- “Does this approach require the observations to be independent?”

These are substantive questions, not admissions of ignorance.

#### C4. Clarify the scope

- “Do you mean this particular project or the group more generally?”
- “Are you referring to MSc students specifically?”
- “Would that apply to every study site?”

This is very useful in exploratory supervisor meetings because a general statement about a program may not apply to a specific project.

#### C5. Clarify the implication

- “Does that mean we'd need another round of recruitment?”
- “So would this affect the feasibility of the project?”
- “Am I right in thinking that this would change the analysis plan?”

These questions connect an explanation to its practical consequences.

### D. Asking “What do you mean by...?” diplomatically

“What do you mean by that?” is common and grammatically correct. Intonation can make it sound genuinely curious or skeptical.

#### D1. Target the phrase

Compare:

> “What do you mean?”

This is broad and may sound challenging in some contexts.

> “When you say ‘independent work,’ what does that look like in practice?”

This is specific and easy to answer.

Other alternatives:

- “Could I ask what you mean by ‘independent’?”
- “When you say ‘flexible timeline,’ do you mean the deadline could change?”
- “Could you clarify how you're using the term ‘validation’?”

#### D2. “In what sense?”

This can be useful when a statement has several interpretations.

Professor: “This project is fairly ambitious.”  
Student: “In what sense? Do you mean the recruitment requirements or the analytical complexity?”

Tone matters. Adding possible interpretations makes the question sound focused rather than combative.

#### D3. Embedded question grammar

Compare:

- Direct: “**What does** the term mean?”
- Embedded: “Could you explain **what the term means**?”
- Direct: “**Why did** you choose this method?”
- Embedded: “Could you explain **why you chose this method**?”
- Direct: “**How does** the project work?”
- Embedded: “I'd like to understand **how the project works**.”

After the reporting/request frame, the embedded wh-question generally uses **statement word order**, as introduced in Lesson 03.2. This grammar supports clearer clarification requests.

### E. Checking understanding through paraphrasing

**Paraphrasing（释义复述）** is expressing what you believe the speaker meant in your own words. It helps prevent errors when facts or responsibilities matter.

#### E1. Reliable confirmation frames

- “Just to make sure I understood, you're saying that...”
- “So, if I've got this right, ...”
- “My understanding is that... Is that correct?”
- “Let me check that I've understood you correctly.”
- “In other words, ... Is that fair?”
- “So the key distinction is...?”

#### E2. “If I've got this right”

A very common American English phrase.

> “So, if I've got this right, the primary analysis uses the full sample, and the sensitivity analysis uses the complete cases?”

It makes your understanding **provisional** until the other speaker confirms it.

#### E3. “In other words...”

**In other words** introduces a restatement.

Professor: “The two measures were collected using different instruments, so direct comparison would be difficult.”  
Student: “In other words, an apparent difference between cohorts might partly reflect measurement differences?”  
Professor: “Exactly.”

The paraphrase should stay close to the original idea. Do not add claims that weren't made without framing them as an inference.

#### E4. “Is that fair?”

**Is that fair?** here means “Is that an accurate representation of what you said?”

> “So the main limitation is the sample size rather than the design itself—is that fair?”

It is conversational and professional. “Is that correct?” or “Have I understood that correctly?” are slightly more explicit alternatives.

#### E5. When the professor corrects your paraphrase

Student: “So the students would lead the full analysis?”  
Professor: “Not necessarily. They might lead one part of it.”  
Student: “Ah, I see—one component of the analysis, depending on the project. Thanks for clarifying.”

A corrected paraphrase is useful information. Repairing it immediately is often better than pretending the misunderstanding never occurred.

### F. Scientific clarification: question types and precision

Academic conversations can require more specific clarification than ordinary small talk.

#### F1. Distinguishing association, prediction, and causation

Professor: “We found that the variable was associated with the outcome.”  
Student: “Just to clarify, are you interpreting that as an association, or is there a causal analysis planned?”

The question distinguishes different research aims without assuming causation from an observed relationship.

#### F2. Distinguishing an example from a requirement

Professor: “A student could contribute to trial design.”  
Student: “Is that one possible type of project, or would trial design be a core requirement?”

*Could* presents possibility; *would be expected* often presents hypothetical duties. This distinction prevents accidentally treating an illustration as a commitment.

#### F3. Distinguishing preliminary from final results

- “Are those preliminary findings or finalized results?”
- “Has that analysis been completed, or is it still in progress?”
- “Are you describing the original plan or the current approach?”

Be careful with unpublished or evolving research: accurately label its status.

#### F4. Clarifying alternatives

- “Are you suggesting option A **instead of** option B, or would you consider using both?”
- “Would this be a replacement for the existing model or an additional sensitivity analysis?”
- “Do you mean the first cohort only or both cohorts?”

A structured **A-or-B** question is often easier to answer than “Could you explain everything again?”

#### F5. “Why?” and professional tone

“Why did you choose that method?” is a legitimate research question. To make the purpose explicit:

- “I'm curious about the rationale for choosing that method.”
- “Could you explain what led you to that choice?”
- “What were the main considerations behind the decision?”

These invite reasoning rather than implying that the decision is obviously wrong.

### G. What if you're still confused after clarification?

A common mistake is to keep saying “I understand” after each explanation even when uncertainty remains.

#### G1. Name what you now understand—and what remains unresolved

> “I understand why the first model includes age and sex. I'm still unsure why the second model adds the additional covariate.”

This signals progress and focuses the next explanation.

#### G2. Ask for a concrete example

> “Could you give me a simple example of how that would work?”

> “Would it help to look at one hypothetical participant?”

Examples can clarify abstraction. Be explicit if you need an illustration rather than an actual confidential record.

#### G3. Ask to see the relevant part

- “Could you show me where that appears in the analysis plan?”
- “Is that described in the paper you mentioned?”
- “Would it be possible to look at the relevant figure?”

For video calls, the speaker may also type a formula or term in chat. Technical recovery appears in 5.4.

#### G4. Respect meeting time without faking comprehension

If the explanation is too large for the moment:

> “I see the broad idea, though I still have a question about the assumptions. Would it be alright if I read more about it and followed up later?”

Use this only when you're genuinely willing and able to follow up. It is not a substitute for understanding a commitment you must make immediately.

#### G5. Distinguish unanswered from unresolved

- **Unanswered question:** “We haven't discussed who would handle recruitment yet.”
- **Unresolved confusion:** “I still don't understand how recruitment would be coordinated.”

The distinction helps you ask for the appropriate next step.

### H. Branching situations and repairs

#### Scenario 1 — You understand the words, but not the reason

Professor: “We excluded that predictor because it could change the interpretation of the model.”  
Student: “I heard the point about excluding it, but I didn't quite follow the reasoning. Could you explain what would change in the interpretation?”  
Professor: “Sure. Adding it would mean...”  
Student: “Ah, I see.”

#### Scenario 2 — A general word has a specific meaning

Professor: “We expect students to work independently.”  
Student: “Could I clarify what ‘independently’ means here? Would students still have regular supervision meetings?”  
Professor: “Yes, definitely. We usually meet weekly.”  
Student: “That helps. Thank you.”

#### Scenario 3 — You need the question rephrased

Professor: “How do you position your project in relation to the broader methodological literature?”  
Student: “Could you rephrase that slightly? Do you mean how the analytical approach relates to previous work?”  
Professor: “Yes, exactly.”  
Student: “Okay, then...”

#### Scenario 4 — You mistakenly infer an offer

Professor: “There may be an opportunity for a student to work on the project.”  
Student: “Just to make sure I've understood, is that a possible opportunity you're considering, rather than a confirmed opening?”  
Professor: “Exactly, it's only a possibility right now.”  
Student: “Thanks for clarifying.”

#### Scenario 5 — You're unsure about a technical term

Professor: “We'll need to deal with clustering.”  
Student: “When you say ‘clustering,’ are you referring to participants within sites?”  
Professor: “Yes, by hospital.”  
Student: “Got it—hospital-level clustering.”

#### Scenario 6 — Your paraphrase is wrong

Student: “So students are required to develop an entirely independent research question?”  
Professor: “No, the question is usually developed jointly.”  
Student: “Ah, thanks for correcting me. So it's a collaborative process.”

#### Scenario 7 — You need an example

Professor: “We tailor the analysis to the estimand.”  
Student: “I understand that the estimand determines the target, but could you give a concrete example of how it affects the analysis?”  
Professor: “Suppose...”

#### Scenario 8 — The answer has two possible interpretations

Professor: “Students typically join a project after the initial phase.”  
Student: “Could I check what you mean by ‘initial phase’? Is that after protocol development, or after recruitment begins?”  
Professor: “Usually after the protocol is approved.”  
Student: “Thanks—that's clear.”

### I. Extended dialogue: from confusion to shared understanding

Professor: “In our trial we distinguish the treatment-policy estimand from a hypothetical estimand.”  
Student: “I caught the two terms, but I don't think I fully follow the distinction. Could you explain it briefly?”  
Professor: “One asks about the effect regardless of treatment discontinuation; the other asks about a scenario in which a particular event didn't occur.”  
Student: “I see. So the difference concerns the treatment-effect question we're trying to answer, rather than just which statistical model we use?”  
Professor: “Yes, that's an important distinction.”  
Student: “That's helpful. Could you give me an example of how the choice would affect a study?”  
Professor: “Suppose some participants discontinue treatment. The estimand tells us how that event enters the question.”  
Student: “So if I've got this right, we need to define the target question before choosing how to handle discontinuation analytically?”  
Professor: “Exactly.”  
Student: “Thank you. That cleared things up.”

**Note:** This is a simplified language-practice illustration of trial estimands, not a technical treatment of estimand frameworks. Its purpose is to demonstrate how a listener isolates, paraphrases and confirms a distinction.

| Student move | Function |
|---|---|
| “I caught the two terms...” | Distinguish hearing from understanding |
| “Could you explain...?” | Request reasoning |
| “So the difference concerns...?” | Paraphrase an emerging interpretation |
| “Could you give me an example...?” | Ask for concrete illustration |
| “If I've got this right...” | Verify the final interpretation |
| “That cleared things up.” | Mark repair completion |

### J. Functional phrase bank and core principles

#### J1. Reusable expressions

| Communication need | Natural expression |
|---|---|
| Lost the reasoning | “Sorry, I didn't quite follow that last point.” |
| Locate confusion | “I'm with you up to the second step.” |
| Informal pinpointing | “You lost me at the part about the second model.” |
| Different wording | “Could you rephrase that?” |
| More detail | “Could you elaborate on that?” |
| Step-by-step | “Could you walk me through how that works?” |
| Simpler parts | “Could you break that down a little?” |
| Concrete illustration | “Could you give me an example?” |
| Meaning of a phrase | “When you say ‘independent,’ what do you mean in this context?” |
| Clarify implication | “Does that mean we'd need to change the plan?” |
| Check interpretation | “So, if I've got this right...” |
| Paraphrase | “In other words, you're suggesting...” |
| Invite correction | “Is that a fair summary?” |
| Correct your interpretation | “Ah, I see—I misunderstood that part.” |
| Still unclear | “I follow the first step, but I'm still unsure about the second.” |

#### J2. Core principles

1. Recognize whether the difficulty is **words**, **definitions**, **reasoning**, or **implications**.
2. “I didn't quite follow” is appropriate when you've heard the words but lost the meaning.
3. Different verbs request different forms of help: repeat, rephrase, clarify, explain, elaborate, walk through, break down.
4. Name the term, step, assumption, or comparison you're uncertain about.
5. Paraphrasing is a provisional check, not a declaration that you understood perfectly.
6. Accept correction to a paraphrase; it is productive conversational information.
7. If confusion remains, ask for an example, a specific figure or a narrower explanation.
8. Don't state that you understand an important academic claim when you do not.
