# Lesson 05 · Unexpected Situations & Recovery

## 5.4 Technical Disruptions, Video-Call Problems, and Recovering the Meeting

**Learning objectives:** Describe audio, video, screen-sharing and connection problems in natural American English; distinguish a technical interruption from a comprehension problem; request specific troubleshooting actions; recover from disrupted turn-taking; reconnect, recap and, when necessary, arrange a follow-up without losing the purpose of the meeting.

**Connection to earlier sections:** Sections 5.1–5.3 cover what happens when the words, meaning or answer fail. Section 5.4 covers **the communication channel itself**. It integrates the turn-taking skills of Lesson 04, but focuses specifically on recovering after unexpected disruptions.

### A. Recognizing the problem and reporting it clearly

A short, precise description is usually the best first response to a technical issue.

| Problem | Natural expression | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| You cannot hear someone | “I can't hear you.” | No usable audio |
| Audio stops briefly | “Your audio cut out for a second.” | Some sound was lost |
| Audio intermittently fails | “Your audio is cutting in and out.” | Unstable audio |
| Audio is distorted | “You're breaking up.” | Words are fragmented or unclear |
| Audio delayed | “There's a bit of a delay.” | Sound/response arrives late |
| Video freezes | “Your video froze.” | Picture stopped updating |
| Screen isn't visible | “I can't see your screen.” | Screen sharing not visible |
| You are muted | “I think you're on mute.” | Microphone disabled |
| Echo/feedback | “I'm hearing an echo.” | Repeated sound |
| Your connection is failing | “I think my connection is unstable.” | Problem may be on your side |

#### A1. State what you observe, not what you cannot know

- “I'm **not getting any audio**.” — Observable fact.
- “Your microphone must be broken.” — Diagnosis without evidence.

Prefer concrete observations before speculating about the cause.

#### A2. “I can't hear you” vs. “I didn't hear you”

- **“I can't hear you.”** — The problem is happening right now.
- **“I couldn't hear you for a few seconds.”** — Describes a past technical interruption.
- **“I didn't catch that.”** — The words were missed; the cause may or may not be technical.

Use tense to communicate whether the problem is ongoing or already over.

### B. Audio problems: cut out, break up, drop, and come through

These phrasal verbs are especially common in calls.

#### B1. “The audio cut out”

**Cut out** = stop unexpectedly, often temporarily.

- “Sorry, the audio cut out for a second.”
- “Your audio cut out right after ‘sample size.’”
- “I lost the last sentence when the audio cut out.”

Notice the distinction:

**“Your audio cut out.”** — Calls attention to what you experienced from their audio stream.

**“My audio cut out.”** — Suggests the problem was on your side or with your own device's sound.

If you're unsure, the neutral “The audio cut out” avoids diagnosing whose connection caused the issue.

#### B2. “You're breaking up”

**Break up** in a phone/video context = sound fragments intermittently.

- “Sorry, you're breaking up a little.”
- “You're breaking up—could you say that last part again?”
- “I can hear you now, but you were breaking up earlier.”

This expression also has unrelated meanings, such as ending a romantic relationship. The context makes the technical meaning clear.

#### B3. “Cutting in and out”

> “Your audio keeps cutting in and out.”

This indicates recurring interruptions, often more informative than simply saying “the audio is bad.”

#### B4. “Come through”

For audio or video, **come through** = be received successfully.

- “I can hear you, but your voice is coming through very quietly.”
- “Your audio is coming through clearly now.”
- “I'm not getting any sound from the presentation.”

“Come through” can also mean succeed or help someone in other contexts; here it describes transmission.

#### B5. “The call dropped”

**The call dropped** = the call ended unexpectedly because the connection failed.

- “Sorry, the call dropped.”
- “I got disconnected for a moment.”
- “I think I lost the connection.”

After rejoining:

> “Sorry, I got disconnected. I'm back now.”

#### B6. “Lag” and “delay”

**Lag（延迟／卡顿）** is a delay between an action and its visible or audible result.

- “There seems to be a bit of lag.”
- “I think there's an audio delay.”
- “Sorry, we may have started talking at the same time because of the delay.”

This links technical trouble with turn-taking from Lesson 04.

### C. Microphones, speakers, echo, and audio troubleshooting

#### C1. Muted microphones

- “I think you're on mute.”
- “It looks like your microphone is muted.”
- “We can't hear you yet—would you mind checking your mic?”

The phrase **“You're on mute”** is very common in video meetings. It means the microphone isn't currently transmitting audio.

#### C2. Troubleshooting your own microphone

- “Can you hear me now?”
- “Let me check my microphone settings.”
- “I think my mic was muted.”
- “I'll switch to my headset.”
- “Give me a moment to reconnect my microphone.”

If the issue resolves:

> “Okay, I think that fixed it. Can you hear me clearly?”

#### C3. Echo and feedback

- “I'm hearing an echo.”
- “There's some feedback on the call.”
- “I think the sound is coming from two devices.”
- “Would you mind muting the second device?”

An echo repeats the sound; feedback may produce ringing or other audio artifacts. These labels overlap in ordinary conversation.

#### C4. Speaker volume and output device

- “I think my audio is going to the wrong output device.”
- “Let me switch from the laptop speakers to my headphones.”
- “Could you speak a little louder? You're coming through quietly.”

For a private meeting, headphones can reduce echo and distractions, although no specific device is universally required.

#### C5. Don't overexplain troubleshooting

Long hardware descriptions often slow the meeting down. A useful pattern is:

**Name the problem → propose one action → confirm the result.**

> “I'm not getting any sound. Let me reconnect my headphones. Can you hear me now?”

### D. Screen sharing, documents, and visual interruptions

#### D1. Starting and checking a share

- “Would it be okay if I shared my screen?”
- “Can you see my screen?”
- “Are you able to see the slide?”
- “I'll switch to the figure for a moment.”
- “Let me pull up the relevant table.”

**Pull up** = open or display a document, web page or other material on a device.

#### D2. Something isn't visible

Professor: “Can you see the figure?”  
Student: “I can see the slides, but the figure is a little small. Would you mind zooming in?”  
Professor: “Sure, how's that?”  
Student: “Much better, thanks.”

Other expressions:

- “I can see your screen, but the text is hard to read.”
- “Could you zoom in on the table?”
- “Would you mind going back one slide?”
- “The shared screen hasn't appeared on my end yet.”

#### D3. “On my end” and “on your end”

These idioms identify which participant is experiencing something.

- “Everything looks fine **on my end**.”
- “I can see the slides **on my end**.”
- “Is the screen sharing working **on your end**?”

Very natural in American workplace and academic calls.

#### D4. Screen-sharing the wrong window

Student: “I think I'm sharing the wrong window. Give me a second to switch.”  
Professor: “No problem.”  
Student: “How about now—can you see the figure?”

If you accidentally display private or confidential material, stop the share promptly and reselect the intended window. Don't share private documents merely to make the meeting easier to follow.

#### D5. Annotating or referring to an image

- “If you look at the upper-right panel...”
- “Could you scroll down a little?”
- “The result I'm referring to is in the second column.”
- “Would you mind highlighting the relevant value?”

Precise spatial language helps when participants do not see exactly the same layout.

### E. Recovering interrupted content and turn-taking

Technical disruptions often affect **what was said** and **who should speak next**.

#### E1. Identify the lost anchor

Less precise:

> “Could you start over?”

More efficient:

> “I heard the explanation up to the part about recruitment. I missed what came after that.”

Another example:

> “The audio cut out just after you mentioned the second cohort. Could you pick up from there?”

**Pick up from there** = resume at that point.

#### E2. “Where were we?”

A very common idiom for resuming a conversation after an interruption.

Professor: “Okay, we're reconnected. Where were we?”  
Student: “We were discussing how MSc students get involved in the projects.”

Alternatives:

- “We were talking about the analysis plan.”
- “You were explaining the recruitment strategy.”
- “I think we were just about to discuss the timeline.”

#### E3. “Pick up where we left off”

**Pick up where we left off** = continue from the point reached before interruption.

> “Great, now that the connection is back, shall we pick up where we left off?”

This expression is useful for meetings, classes, projects, and discussions resumed after a break.

#### E4. “You were saying...”

This invites a speaker to resume a previous contribution.

> “Sorry about the interruption. You were saying something about the project's eligibility criteria?”

It is natural and polite. You can provide the last topic as a cue.

#### E5. “Go ahead” after an audio delay

Professor: “I was just going to—”  
Student: “Sorry—go ahead.”  
Professor: “Thanks. I was saying that...”

A short interruption-repair phrase can resolve overlap. The key issue here is the **technical source** of overlap; Lesson 04.2 covers conversational interruptions more generally.

### F. Rejoining, switching channels, and arranging alternatives

#### F1. Rejoining after disconnection

A straightforward sequence:

1. “Sorry, I got disconnected.”
2. “I'm back now. Can you hear me?”
3. “I missed the last part—could you pick up from the point about [topic]?”

Example:

Student: “Sorry, I got disconnected. I'm back now.”  
Professor: “No worries. Can you hear us?”  
Student: “Yes, clearly. I missed the last part about the timeline—could you repeat it?”

#### F2. Switching devices

- “Would you mind if I rejoined from my phone?”
- “I'll try switching to a different connection.”
- “Let me turn off my video and see if the audio improves.”
- “I can hear you now. I think switching networks helped.”

These phrases describe a troubleshooting attempt, not a guaranteed technical solution.

#### F3. Using the chat as a fallback

- “Could you type the acronym in the chat?”
- “I'm having trouble with my microphone. Could I put my question in the chat?”
- “Would you mind sending the title afterward?”

Check that you are sending a message to the intended participants, particularly in larger calls.

#### F4. Rescheduling if the meeting cannot continue

- “It looks like the connection isn't stable enough to continue.”
- “Would it make sense to reschedule the remaining discussion?”
- “I apologize for the technical difficulties. Would another time work for you?”
- “I can follow up with a few possible times by email.”

Only promise a follow-up you intend to carry out. If part of the meeting occurred, specify what remains to be discussed.

#### F5. Sensitive topics and recording

A suggestion to record the call may raise consent or institutional privacy questions. You can request a written summary or ask permission before recording; do not assume recording is allowed.

> “Would you prefer that I take notes, or is there a written resource I should consult afterward?”

### G. Professional phrasing: responsibility, politeness, and tone

#### G1. Apologize once, then solve the problem

Natural:

> “Sorry, the audio cut out. Could you repeat the last sentence?”

Repeating lengthy apologies after every failed syllable usually adds unnecessary friction. Acknowledge the interruption, ask for a specific repair, and continue.

#### G2. Don't assert that the fault is on their side

Compare:

- “Your internet is bad.” — Assumes responsibility and can sound accusatory.
- “I'm having trouble hearing you; the audio keeps breaking up.” — Describes your experience.
- “There may be a connection issue.” — Neutral possibility.

Use evidence to describe what happened, not to assign blame.

#### G3. Distinguish chat messages from spoken requests

If speaking is impossible, a concise chat message can convey the problem:

> “My microphone isn't working at the moment. I can hear everyone and will type my question here.”

In a video meeting this is often more useful than repeatedly trying to speak while muted.

#### G4. “Bear with me”

**Bear with me** = please be patient while I do something.

- “Bear with me for a second while I reconnect.”
- “Just bear with me while I pull up the figure.”

Correct spelling: **bear**, not *bare*. *Bare* means uncovered; *bear* in this idiom means tolerate or be patient.

#### G5. “Give me a second” vs. “one moment, please”

- “Give me a second.” — Conversational, common American English.
- “One moment, please.” — More formal, polite.
- “Just a second while I fix the screen share.” — Neutral-professional.

All can work in academic calls; tone and relationship determine fit.

### H. Branching technical scenarios

#### Scenario 1 — The professor is muted

Professor: *speaks, but no sound comes through*  
Student: “Sorry, I think you're on mute.”  
Professor: “Oh! Can you hear me now?”  
Student: “Yes, perfectly. Thank you.”

#### Scenario 2 — The last sentence cuts out

Professor: “The main challenge was the recruitment process, because...”  
*Audio drops.*  
Student: “Sorry, your audio cut out after ‘recruitment process.’ Could you pick up from there?”  
Professor: “Of course. The challenge was...”

#### Scenario 3 — Audio is breaking up repeatedly

Student: “I'm having trouble hearing the last few sentences—your audio is breaking up a little.”  
Professor: “Let me switch microphones.”  
Student: “Sure, take your time.”  
Professor: “How's this?”  
Student: “Much clearer, thanks.”

#### Scenario 4 — Screen share doesn't appear

Professor: “You should be able to see the figure now.”  
Student: “I can't see the shared screen on my end yet.”  
Professor: “Let me try again.”  
Student: “There it is. Thanks.”

#### Scenario 5 — Text too small

Professor: “The result is in Table 3.”  
Student: “I can see the table, but the text is a little small. Would you mind zooming in?”  
Professor: “Sure.”  
Student: “That's much easier to read.”

#### Scenario 6 — Your own laptop loses audio

Student: “I think I lost audio on my end. Let me reconnect my headphones.”  
*Pause.*  
Student: “Okay, I can hear you now. Sorry about that—where were we?”  
Professor: “We were discussing the data.”

#### Scenario 7 — Accidental overlap because of lag

Professor: “I think the—”  
Student: “Could I—”  
Student: “Sorry, I think there's a delay. Go ahead.”  
Professor: “Thanks. I was asking about the data source.”

#### Scenario 8 — Chat fallback

Student: “I can hear you, but my microphone has stopped working. Would it be okay if I typed my question in the chat?”  
Professor: “Absolutely.”  
Student: *types the question*  
Professor: “Good question. Let me explain.”

#### Scenario 9 — Connection fails during an explanation

*Student leaves the call unexpectedly and rejoins.*  
Student: “Sorry, my connection dropped. I'm back now.”  
Professor: “Welcome back.”  
Student: “Thanks. I heard the part about the study design but missed the discussion of data access. Could we pick up there?”  
Professor: “Sure.”

#### Scenario 10 — Disruption makes the meeting impractical

Student: “I'm sorry—the connection is still unstable, and I'm having trouble following the discussion.”  
Professor: “That's frustrating.”  
Student: “Would it make sense to reschedule the remaining questions? I can email a couple of possible times.”  
Professor: “Yes, let's do that.”  
Student: “Thank you. I appreciate your flexibility.”

### I. Extended video meeting: technical interruption and recovery

Professor: “I'll share a slide showing the structure of the project.”  
Student: “Great.”  
Professor: “Can you see my screen?”  
Student: “Not yet—I'm still seeing the meeting window.”  
Professor: “Let me try again.”  
Student: “Okay, I can see it now.”  
Professor: “The first phase involves a literature review, and then we move into...”  
*Audio becomes distorted.*  
Student: “Sorry, you're breaking up a little. I heard the part about the literature review, but missed what comes next.”  
Professor: “We move into developing the analysis plan.”  
Student: “Got it. Literature review, then the analysis plan.”  
Professor: “Exactly. After that, students work with the study data.”  
*Student's connection drops; student rejoins.*  
Student: “Sorry, my connection dropped for a moment. Can you hear me now?”  
Professor: “Yes, welcome back.”  
Student: “Thanks. I missed the end of the project overview. Could we pick up where we left off?”  
Professor: “Of course. We were discussing the stage when students begin data analysis.”  
Student: “Right, thank you. Could I ask whether students would be working with an existing dataset at that stage?”  
Professor: “That depends on the specific project.”  
Student: “I see. That helps.”  
Professor: “Any other questions?”  
Student: “I have one more, but I also want to be mindful of the time.”  
Professor: “We have a few minutes. Go ahead.”

#### I1. Communication analysis

| Expression | Function |
|---|---|
| “Not yet—I'm still seeing the meeting window.” | Precise screen-share problem |
| “You're breaking up a little.” | Diagnose audio reception, not speaker competence |
| “I heard the part about...” | Target the missing content |
| “Literature review, then analysis plan.” | Confirm repaired information |
| “My connection dropped.” | Explain unexpected departure |
| “Can you hear me now?” | Verify restored audio |
| “Could we pick up where we left off?” | Resume conversation |
| “I want to be mindful of the time.” | Adapt after delay |

### J. Functional phrase bank and core principles

#### J1. Essential expressions

| Situation | Say this |
|---|---|
| No sound | “Sorry, I can't hear you.” |
| Other person muted | “I think you're on mute.” |
| Momentary audio loss | “The audio cut out for a second.” |
| Fragmented sound | “You're breaking up a little.” |
| Intermittent sound | “Your audio keeps cutting in and out.” |
| Delay | “I think there's a bit of lag.” |
| Your connection fails | “Sorry, I got disconnected.” |
| Rejoined | “I'm back now. Can you hear me?” |
| Slide not showing | “I can't see your shared screen yet.” |
| Slide too small | “Would you mind zooming in?” |
| Wrong window | “I'm sharing the wrong window. Let me switch.” |
| Need patience | “Bear with me for a second.” |
| Resume from point | “Could you pick up from the part about recruitment?” |
| Resume after pause | “Shall we pick up where we left off?” |
| Ask where conversation stopped | “Where were we?” |
| Invite continuation | “You were saying something about the timeline?” |
| Change troubleshooting approach | “I'll try switching to another connection.” |
| Chat fallback | “Could I put my question in the chat?” |
| Meeting cannot continue | “Would it make sense to reschedule the remaining discussion?” |

#### J2. Core principles

1. Say **what failed**: audio, video, screen sharing, connection, or the content you missed.
2. Prefer observable descriptions over unsupported blame.
3. Use present tense for an ongoing problem and past tense for a completed interruption.
4. Identify the last words you heard to avoid asking someone to repeat a whole explanation.
5. Confirm the repair with a brief paraphrase or “Got it, thanks.”
6. When the call recovers, explicitly resume the relevant topic.
7. Switch to a different method—headphones, alternate connection, or chat—if repetition alone is insufficient.
8. Don't share confidential screens or record meetings without appropriate permission.
9. If the meeting cannot proceed, arrange a specific reasonable follow-up rather than forcing an unintelligible call.
10. A technical interruption is ordinary; clear, calm repair language is part of competent professional communication.
