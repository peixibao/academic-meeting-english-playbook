# Lesson 04 · Turn-Taking & Conversation Management

## 4.2 Interruptions and Overlapping Speech

**Learning objectives:** Distinguish accidental overlap from an intentional interruption; recognize when an interruption is appropriate; enter a conversation without sounding aggressive; respond when someone interrupts you; recover after simultaneous speech; and handle interruptions across one-on-one meetings, seminars, and research-group discussions.

**Connection to 4.1:** Section 4.1 introduced *taking, holding, and yielding the floor*. This section focuses on moments when those actions compete: two people speak at once, someone needs to ask an urgent question, or a speaker's contribution is cut short. The lesson is about **managing the interaction**, rather than simply memorizing polite requests.

### A. Understanding interruption and overlap

#### A1. They are not always the same thing

**Overlap（重叠发言）** occurs when two people speak at the same time. It may happen accidentally, or it may be a natural part of cooperative conversation.

**Interruption（打断）** occurs when someone begins speaking in a way that disrupts another person's ongoing turn. Depending on context, it can be helpful, neutral, or unwelcome.

Compare:

**Accidental overlap**

Professor: “I think the main issue is—”  
Student: “Could we also—”  
*Both stop.*  
Student: “Oh, sorry. Go ahead.”

**Supportive overlap**

Professor: “The biggest challenge was getting enough—”  
Student: “Participants?”  
Professor: “Exactly. Getting enough participants.”

**Disruptive interruption**

Professor: “There are two limitations we should consider. The first—”  
Student: “Yeah, but my analysis already addressed that.”

The final example risks sounding defensive because the student dismisses the professor before hearing the explanation. A well-timed interruption can sometimes be necessary; the important questions are **why it happens, where it happens, and how the conversation continues afterward**.

#### A2. Why speakers interrupt

| Purpose | Example | Typical judgment |
|---|---|---|
| Ask a clarification that matters immediately | “Sorry, could I clarify what you mean by *eligible*?” | Often appropriate at a useful pause |
| Prevent a consequential misunderstanding | “Sorry, one important correction: those figures are from the pilot sample.” | Often justified |
| Add a directly relevant point | “Can I jump in with one point?” | Appropriate when timed carefully |
| Signal enthusiastic agreement | “Exactly!” | Can be supportive when brief |
| Finish someone else's sentence | “Recruitment?” | Depends on relationship and timing |
| Redirect a discussion that is running long | “Could we come back to the deadline for a moment?” | Often appropriate for a chair or facilitator |
| Repeatedly talk over another person | Speaking continuously while they try to continue | Frequently disruptive |

**Conversational implication:** An interruption is not defined as rude simply because someone speaks before the other person has completely finished. Nor does a polite phrase automatically make poor timing acceptable.

### B. Ways to interrupt: subtle differences

#### B1. A graded phrase bank

| Expression | Function | Register and effect |
|---|---|---|
| “Sorry, can I ask something?” | Briefly enter with a question | Neutral/friendly professional |
| “Can I jump in here?” | Join an active discussion | Natural American workplace English |
| “Could I jump in for a second?” | Request a brief turn | Slightly softer |
| “Sorry to interrupt, but...” | Acknowledge a clear interruption | Neutral-professional |
| “If I could just add something...” | Introduce a point tentatively | Slightly more formal |
| “Could I come in on that point?” | Contribute to an ongoing topic | Professional; also common in international settings |
| “Before you move on, could I ask...?” | Stop a topic transition | Direct but considerate |
| “If I may...” | Formal entry | More ceremonious; context-dependent |
| “One quick clarification, if I may...” | Ask a limited question | Formal-professional |

A natural default in an American one-on-one meeting is often **“Sorry, could I jump in for a second?”** When there is already a clear pause, a simpler **“Could I ask something about that?”** works well.

#### B2. “Jump in,” “cut in,” “butt in,” and “talk over”

These expressions are related, but their connotations differ.

- **Jump in** — Begin participating, often in a collaborative or neutral way: “Feel free to jump in with questions.”
- **Cut in** — Interrupt someone's speech or activity; sometimes mildly negative: “Sorry to cut in, but we need to confirm the deadline.”
- **Butt in** — Intrude into a conversation without being invited; often negative and informal: “I didn't want to butt in while they were talking.”
- **Talk over someone** — Continue speaking while another person is also trying to speak, often implying disregard: “I felt I was being talked over.”
- **Step in** — Intervene to help, manage, or take responsibility: “The chair stepped in to move the discussion along.”
- **Break in** — Interrupt or enter suddenly; can sound more forceful: “She broke in with a question.”

**Pragmatic caution:** “I don't mean to butt in” is sometimes used playfully, but **butt in** is a poor default for a first meeting with a professor. “Jump in” is much more adaptable.

#### B3. “Sorry to interrupt” vs. “Sorry for interrupting”

Both are grammatical, but timing often differs:

- **“Sorry to interrupt, but...”** — Used as you're about to interrupt or begin interrupting.
- **“Sorry for interrupting.”** — Often apologizes for an interruption that has occurred.

Examples:

> “Sorry to interrupt, but could I quickly check which dataset we're discussing?”

> “Sorry for interrupting earlier. Please go on.”

They can overlap in real speech, and you do not need to treat the distinction as a rigid rule.

### C. Choosing the right moment

#### C1. Turn-transition relevance places

In conversation analysis, a **transition-relevance place (TRP, 潜在话轮交接点)** is a point where one speaker's contribution could reasonably be complete and another speaker might begin.

A TRP can be signaled by a combination of grammar, intonation, timing, and conversational purpose.

Professor: “We considered two approaches, and ultimately chose the second one.”  
*Brief pause.*  
Student: “Could I ask what drove that decision?”

This is a relatively safe entry point.

Compare:

Professor: “We considered two approaches. The first was...”  
Student: “Why did you choose the first one?”

Here the student entered before the professor had explained the alternatives.

#### C2. When interrupting early is useful

There are circumstances where waiting for the complete explanation is inefficient or potentially misleading.

**Example: A crucial factual distinction**

Professor: “So these results were based on the full cohort of 600 participants—”  
Student: “Sorry to interrupt—just to clarify, the analysis used the subgroup with complete outcome data.”  
Professor: “Ah, thanks for clarifying. How many participants were included?”

The student makes a precise, relevant correction, without implying a mistake about a real user's research. In your own meetings, use **the numbers and facts actually supported by your records**.

**Example: An unfamiliar term affecting the explanation**

Professor: “We used a noninferiority framework, then assessed—”  
Student: “Could I quickly clarify what noninferiority means in this particular context?”  
Professor: “Of course.”

Detailed strategies for not hearing, understanding, or following speech remain in **Lesson 05**. Here we're learning **when and how to enter the turn**.

#### C3. The “one quick question” promise

“Can I ask one quick question?” creates an expectation of brevity. A question that requires a ten-minute methodological discussion may not be quick.

Instead:

> “There's a related question I'd be interested in discussing when we have a moment.”

Or:

> “Could we come back to this point after you finish the overview?”

Use a brief-entry phrase when your contribution really is brief.

### D. Handling simultaneous starts and accidental overlap

#### D1. Three simple repairs

**Yield immediately:**

> “Oh, sorry—go ahead.”

**Offer the other person the first turn:**

> “You go first.”

**Resume after permission:**

> “Thanks. What I was going to say was...”

Example:

Professor: “I think we should—”  
Student: “Could I—”  
Student: “Sorry, go ahead.”  
Professor: “I was just going to ask about your experience with data analysis.”  
Student: “Of course. Most of my experience has been with observational datasets.”

No extended apology is needed after a minor collision.

#### D2. “Go ahead,” “you go ahead,” and “after you”

- **“Go ahead.”** — Allows or invites the other person to continue.
- **“You go ahead.”** — Explicitly yields the floor to the other person.
- **“After you.”** — Polite and sometimes playful; also used in physical settings such as doorways.

In a video meeting, **“Sorry, you go ahead”** is reliably clear.

#### D3. If both speakers keep yielding

Professor: “No, you go ahead.”  
Student: “Thanks. I just wanted to ask whether the timeline is fixed.”

If the other person has already handed you the turn, take it. An endless exchange of “No, you first!” becomes awkward.

### E. Returning to your point after interruption

An interruption may be supportive, critical, or simply a related question. After responding, you may need to return to your unfinished thought.

#### E1. Recovery phrases

| Expression | Typical use |
|---|---|
| “As I was saying...” | Resume an earlier point; may sound impatient in some contexts |
| “To finish that thought...” | Complete an interrupted contribution |
| “What I was going to add is...” | Return to an unspoken point |
| “The other point I wanted to make was...” | Resume a multi-part explanation |
| “Coming back to my original point...” | Return after a longer detour |
| “Just to complete the picture...” | Add missing context |
| “And that relates to what I was saying about...” | Connect interruption to original idea |

**Important nuance:** “As I was saying” can be completely neutral, but when stressed sharply it may sound like a rebuke (“As I *was* saying...”). “To finish that thought” or “What I was going to add is...” often sounds more collaborative.

#### E2. Multi-turn example

Student: “There are two reasons I found the project useful. First, I learned how to organize the analysis—”  
Professor: “Were you responsible for the coding?”  
Student: “Yes, I worked on the analysis code. And to finish that thought, the second thing I learned was how to communicate the limitations clearly.”  
Professor: “That's important.”

This is a natural way to answer the interruption without losing your original structure.

#### E3. If the interruption introduces an important new point

Sometimes you should abandon your prepared continuation because the professor has raised something more important.

Professor: “Were you responsible for the participant recruitment?”  
Student: “I contributed to participant coordination, though my main role was analysis.”

The student answers the question instead of insisting on completing the previous explanation. **Good turn management requires flexibility about sequence without distorting the facts.**

### F. When someone repeatedly interrupts you

In a first academic meeting, interruptions may occur because a professor is asking targeted questions or has limited time. Avoid inferring disrespect from a single overlap. Repeated interruptions may still prevent you from making an important point.

#### F1. Gentle continuation

- “Absolutely. I just wanted to finish one point about the study design.”
- “Yes, and there's one detail that may help clarify that.”
- “Could I finish that thought? It relates directly to your question.”
- “If I could complete that point briefly...”

**Example:**

Student: “The primary outcome was defined using—”  
Professor: “What about the sample size?”  
Student: “The analysis included 300 participants in this hypothetical example. Could I briefly finish explaining the outcome definition? It affects how we interpret the estimates.”

The student connects the request to the substantive discussion.

#### F2. A more assertive boundary

In a familiar research team, it can be appropriate to say:

> “I'd like to finish my point, and then I'd be happy to hear your perspective.”

Or:

> “I think we're talking over each other. Could I finish this explanation first?”

These are direct, but still professional. Match the response to the relationship and the seriousness of the problem.

#### F3. When the chair or professor manages the turn

Professor: “Let's let Peixi finish her point first, then we'll hear from the others.”  
Student: “Thanks. To finish the point, the sensitivity analysis gave a similar pattern.”

The chair is managing the conversational floor. You generally don't need to restart from the beginning.

### G. Responding to interruptions of different kinds

#### G1. Clarification interrupt

Professor: “Sorry, can I clarify something? Was this an observational study?”  
Student: “Yes, it was. More specifically, it was cross-sectional.”

A concise answer often enables you to continue.

#### G2. Correction interrupt

Professor: “One small correction—that outcome was assessed at six months, not three.”  
Student: “Ah, thank you. Six months. So the follow-up period was longer than I initially understood.”

The student accepts the correction, updates the information, and continues.

#### G3. Redirect from the professor

Professor: “Actually, could you tell me more about your contribution rather than the project's overall background?”  
Student: “Of course. My main responsibility was preparing the statistical analysis.”

The professor has changed the requested scope. Follow that direction without defensiveness.

#### G4. Friendly completion

Student: “The hardest part was understanding the assumptions behind—”  
Professor: “The regression model?”  
Student: “Exactly. And checking whether they were reasonable for the data.”

Finishing another speaker's sentence can indicate engagement, but should not become habitual or overconfident.

#### G5. You accidentally interrupt an important point

Student: “Sorry, I think I jumped in a bit early. Please finish your point.”  
Professor: “No problem. I was going to say that the final decision depends on the data available.”

Short acknowledgment and return to listening are sufficient.

### H. Unexpected scenarios

#### Scenario 1 — You need to prevent a misconception

Professor: “So the project included a randomized intervention—”  
Student: “Sorry to jump in—one quick clarification: it was an observational study.”  
Professor: “Oh, I see. Thanks. Tell me more.”

**Lesson:** Brief, specific correction may justify a timely interruption.

#### Scenario 2 — Two people begin at once in a video meeting

Professor: “I wanted to ask—”  
Student: “Could I—”  
Student: “Oh, sorry, you go ahead.”  
Professor: “Thanks. I wanted to ask what drew you to this area.”

**Lesson:** Resolve overlap quickly and clearly.

#### Scenario 3 — Your point is cut off by a related question

Student: “There were three main challenges. The first involved missing data—”  
Professor: “How much data was missing?”  
Student: “That varied by variable. To finish the broader point, the other challenges were measurement consistency and interpretation.”

**Lesson:** Respond to the interruption and recover your structured answer.

#### Scenario 4 — The professor's explanation is long

Professor: “We recruit across multiple sites, and each site has its own procedures. Then there's central coordination, which...”  
*Natural pause.*  
Student: “Could I clarify one thing before we move on? Are the measurements standardized across sites?”

**Lesson:** Enter at a pause, and explain why the question relates to the ongoing explanation.

#### Scenario 5 — A group participant dominates the discussion

Researcher A: “And another thing we should do is...”  
Chair: “Thanks. Let's pause there and hear from the others. Peixi, would you like to add anything?”  
Student: “Yes. I'd like to build on the earlier point about data quality.”

**Lesson:** A chair can manage turns more directly than an ordinary participant.

#### Scenario 6 — Someone asks you to speak first

Professor: “No, please go ahead. What were you going to say?”  
Student: “Thanks. I was going to ask how the team usually defines the research question for a new project.”

**Lesson:** Accept the offered floor instead of repeatedly yielding.

#### Scenario 7 — You must finish one essential point

Professor: “Alright, let's move on.”  
Student: “Before we move on, could I briefly clarify one point about my role? It was primarily analysis rather than data collection.”

**Lesson:** Keep the correction brief and relevant.

#### Scenario 8 — An interruption feels like a disagreement

Student: “One possible interpretation is...”  
Professor: “I'm not sure that follows from the design.”  
Student: “That's fair. Could I explain the reasoning behind my initial interpretation, and then hear your concerns?”

**Lesson:** Respond to the actual methodological issue; use turn-management language to allow both explanations.

### I. Extended dialogue: a meeting with several overlaps

**Professor:** “I'd like to start with your research experience. Tell me about one project.”

**Student:** “Sure. One project involved observational data. My main responsibility was the statistical analysis, and there were two things I learned from it.”

**Professor:** “Were you using regression models?”

**Student:** “Yes. I worked with regression-based analyses. And the first thing I learned was how important it is to connect the model to a specific research question.”

**Professor:** “That sounds right. What was the second thing?”

**Student:** “The second was interpreting the limitations carefully, especially when some variables had missing information.”

**Professor:** “I see. In our group, we sometimes work with quite different types of datasets. One of our projects is based on—”

**Student:** “Could I—”

**Professor:** “—a clinical trial dataset.”

**Student:** “Sorry, I jumped in too early. Please go ahead.”

**Professor:** “No worries. I was just saying we have several datasets with different designs.”

**Student:** “Got it. Could I ask how you decide which questions an MSc student would work on?”

**Professor:** “Usually we discuss the available data and the student's interests.”

**Student:** “That makes sense. And just to build on what you said, I'd be interested in learning how different study designs affect what questions can be answered.”

**Professor:** “Absolutely. That's an important part of the research process.”

**Student:** “Thanks. One related question: would an MSc student typically be responsible for a complete analysis, or contribute to a larger team effort?”

**Professor:** “It depends on the project. Let me explain the usual arrangement.”

**Student:** “Of course.”

#### I1. Breakdown of the turn-management moves

| Expression | Function |
|---|---|
| “There were two things I learned...” | Holds the floor and previews structure |
| Answering “Were you using regression models?” | Responds to an interruption |
| “And the first thing...” | Resumes earlier explanation |
| “Sorry, I jumped in too early.” | Repairs accidental interruption |
| “Please go ahead.” | Yields the floor |
| “Could I ask...?” | Takes a turn at a relevant moment |
| “Just to build on what you said...” | Adds a related contribution |
| “One related question...” | Signals a short follow-up |
| “Of course.” | Yields floor for the professor's explanation |

### J. Functional phrase bank and principles

#### J1. The phrase bank

| Communication need | Expression |
|---|---|
| Briefly enter a discussion | “Could I jump in for a second?” |
| Acknowledge interruption | “Sorry to interrupt, but...” |
| Clarify immediately | “One quick clarification, if I may...” |
| Stop a transition | “Before we move on, could I ask...?” |
| Yield during overlap | “Oh, sorry—you go ahead.” |
| Accept the offered floor | “Thanks. What I was going to say was...” |
| Resume a point | “To finish that thought...” |
| Continue after a detour | “Coming back to my original point...” |
| Recover an earlier idea | “The other point I wanted to make was...” |
| Politely protect a turn | “Could I finish that thought?” |
| Identify simultaneous speech | “I think we're talking over each other.” |
| Correct an error briefly | “Sorry, one important clarification...” |
| Encourage someone to finish | “Please go on.” |
| Invite a participant | “I'd be interested in hearing what you think.” |

#### J2. Core principles

1. **Overlap and interruption differ.** Speaking simultaneously can be accidental or supportive; disrupting someone's contribution may require repair.
2. **Timing matters as much as wording.** A polite opener does not justify repeatedly cutting someone off.
3. **Jump in** is usually a better friendly-professional default than **butt in**.
4. **Repair overlap concisely.** “Sorry—go ahead” is often enough.
5. **Recover your thought when needed.** “To finish that thought...” is useful after a digression.
6. **Prioritize accuracy.** Correct a consequential factual misunderstanding promptly and respectfully.
7. **Respect assigned turns.** In chaired meetings, the moderator may control speaking order.
8. **Maintain boundaries without escalating.** “Could I finish that point?” can be professional when genuinely needed.
