# Lesson 04 · Turn-Taking & Conversation Management

## 4.3 Topic Changes and Transitions

**Learning objectives:** Recognize when speakers are shifting topics; distinguish smooth transitions from abrupt changes; move from small talk into an academic agenda; connect related ideas; return to an earlier topic; redirect a discussion; and handle unexpected topic changes without losing track of the conversation.

**Connection to earlier sections:** Lesson 02 covered sustaining a topic with follow-up questions. Lesson 04.1 covered access to the speaking floor, and 04.2 covered overlap and interruption. This section asks a different question: **Once you have the floor, how do you move the conversation from one subject to another?**

### A. How topics change in natural conversation

#### A1. Four kinds of topic movement

**Topic management（话题管理）** is the process of introducing, developing, changing, returning to, and closing topics during interaction.

| Movement | What happens | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Topic expansion** | Explore the same subject in more detail | Recruitment → recruitment challenges |
| **Related transition** | Move to a connected subject | Recruitment challenges → study feasibility |
| **New-topic introduction** | Begin a largely different subject | Research projects → program logistics |
| **Topic return** | Revisit something raised earlier | “Coming back to your earlier point about supervision...” |

These movements need different kinds of transition language. A statement such as “That reminds me...” often establishes a logical connection; “By the way...” frequently signals a more incidental topic change.

#### A2. What makes a transition natural?

A natural transition often involves three steps:

1. **Acknowledge the current topic.**
2. **Signal the relationship between the current and next topic.**
3. **Introduce the new topic or question.**

Professor: “Most of our projects involve collaborations with clinicians.”  
Student: “That makes sense. **Speaking of collaboration**, how do students usually work with researchers from other departments?”

This shift is easy to follow because *collaboration* links the old and new topics.

Contrast:

Professor: “Most of our projects involve collaborations with clinicians.”  
Student: “What's the tuition fee?”

The fee question may be legitimate, but the transition is abrupt. The student could say:

> “Thanks for explaining the projects. Could I switch gears for a moment and ask about how the program is structured?”

#### A3. Transitions are not always necessary

In ordinary conversation, you do not need to announce every small shift. A relevant follow-up can move into a related topic with no explicit marker. Overusing “speaking of...” or “that reminds me...” can make your speech sound scripted.

**Key principle:** Use transition language when it helps the other person see **why the subject is changing**.

### B. Connecting related topics

#### B1. “Speaking of...”

**Speaking of + noun / noun phrase** introduces something connected to the topic just mentioned.

- “Speaking of research collaborations, are students involved in joint projects?”
- “Speaking of conferences, are you attending any this year?”
- “Speaking of data quality, how do you usually deal with missing values?”

**Grammar:** *speaking of* is followed by a noun phrase, not a full finite clause.

Natural: “Speaking of **supervision**, how often do students meet their advisor?”  
Awkward: “Speaking of **how students meet their advisor is important**...”

To connect to a complete proposition, use “That connects to something I wanted to ask...” or “On that point...”.

#### B2. “That reminds me...”

**That reminds me of...** introduces an association prompted by the current conversation.

Professor: “We had trouble with participant recruitment.”  
Student: “That reminds me of an issue we encountered in an observational study. We had to reconsider how to define eligibility.”

This can be more personal or anecdotal than “speaking of.”

**Useful patterns:**

- “That reminds me of something I encountered during my internship.”
- “That reminds me—I wanted to ask about the timeline.”
- “That actually reminds me of a question I had.”

In “That reminds me—I wanted to ask...”, *reminds me* functions as an informal transition even when the connection is weak.

#### B3. “On that note...”

**On that note** often uses a point just made as a bridge into a related question, action, or conclusion.

> “You mentioned that the team meets weekly. **On that note**, how do students usually prepare for those meetings?”

> “We've agreed on the next steps. **On that note**, perhaps we should confirm the deadline.”

The expression can also accompany the end of a conversation (“On that note, I should let you get back to work”). Formal closing is covered systematically in Lesson 08.

#### B4. “That brings me to...”

This expression links an earlier point to a new question or argument.

> “The distinction between cohort and trial data is really useful. **That brings me to** something I wanted to ask about the kinds of datasets MSc students work with.”

It is especially useful when moving through prepared questions without sounding as though you're reading a checklist.

#### B5. “Along those lines...” and “Related to that...”

- “**Along those lines**, would students have opportunities to collaborate with clinicians?”
- “**Related to that**, how is methodological training usually arranged?”
- “**On a related note**, what types of data do students typically work with?”

These expressions frame the next contribution as connected, even if the connection is not identical.

### C. Introducing a new or only loosely related topic

#### C1. “By the way”

**By the way** is a common conversational marker for additional or incidental information, often less closely related to what came before.

- “By the way, will you be attending the conference next month?”
- “By the way, I meant to ask whether the seminar will be recorded.”

It works well in friendly conversation. In a structured academic meeting, a more explicit shift may be clearer if you're changing from research discussion to administrative questions.

#### C2. “Anyway”

**Anyway** often marks a return to a prior topic, a departure from a digression, or a move toward the main agenda.

Professor: “The weather here has been unpredictable lately. **Anyway**, shall we get started?”

Here *anyway* closes the small-talk sequence.

It can sometimes sound dismissive if used immediately after someone has shared something significant.

Professor: “The team faced a difficult setback this year.”  
Student: “Anyway, what projects are available?”

The topic change may sound brusque. Acknowledge the prior point before transitioning:

> “I'm sorry to hear that. I hope things improve. When you're ready, I'd also be interested in hearing about the projects you're currently working on.”

#### C3. “Switch gears”

**Switch gears** is a common American English idiom meaning to change focus or activity.

- “Could we **switch gears** for a moment and talk about the supervision structure?”
- “I'd like to **switch gears** slightly and ask about the application process.”
- “Before we switch gears, could I ask one more question about the study?”

**Register:** Conversational and professionally appropriate in many North American academic meetings.

It is especially helpful when the next subject is clearly different.

#### C4. “Moving on to...”

This is explicit **signposting（话语路标）**.

- “Moving on to the next question...”
- “Moving on to the data analysis...”
- “Could we move on to the project timeline?”

A speaker who leads or structures a meeting may say “Moving on to...” freely. A prospective student speaking to a professor might phrase a change as an invitation:

> “Would it be alright if we moved on to the potential thesis directions?”

#### C5. “There's something else I'd like to ask...”

This is a clear, neutral way to introduce a new question without pretending it connects closely to the current subject.

> “Thanks for explaining that. There's something else I'd like to ask about the application process.”

It can sound more natural than inventing a weak connection with “Speaking of...”

### D. Important differences between similar transitions

#### D1. By the way vs. speaking of vs. anyway

| Expression | Typical relationship to prior topic | Common purpose |
|---|---|---|
| **Speaking of X...** | Clear topical connection | Launch related question |
| **That reminds me...** | Association, sometimes personal | Introduce recalled example/question |
| **On that note...** | Builds on or closes a prior point | Related question/action/transition |
| **By the way...** | Additional or more incidental | Introduce a side topic |
| **Anyway...** | Move away from digression/return to agenda | Regain focus or wrap a detour |
| **Switching gears...** | Deliberately change focus | Announce a separate topic |
| **Moving on to...** | Structured sequence | Advance the agenda |

There is overlap, but the words are not interchangeable in all contexts.

#### D2. “As for...” vs. “Regarding...” vs. “When it comes to...”

These expressions introduce or highlight a particular topic, but their style differs.

**As for + noun phrase** — Turns attention to a specific item, often in contrast with one already mentioned:

> “The course requirements seem clear. **As for** thesis projects, how are those usually arranged?”

**Regarding + noun phrase** — More formal or administrative:

> “**Regarding** the project timeline, is there a provisional schedule?”

**When it comes to + noun / -ing** — Natural, explanatory, and conversational:

> “**When it comes to** statistical analysis, how much independence do MSc students usually have?”

**Grammar caution:** “Regarding to” is generally incorrect in this use. Say **regarding the application**, **with regard to the application**, or **in regard to the application**.

#### D3. “As for X” can imply contrast

Compare:

> “I feel comfortable working with cross-sectional data. **As for** longitudinal analysis, that's an area I'm still learning about.”

This introduces a contrast between the two topics. If no contrast is intended, “On the topic of...” or “Regarding...” may be smoother.

#### D4. “Anyway” vs. “In any case”

**Anyway** is common in conversation for moving on or returning to a prior point.

**In any case** often emphasizes a conclusion that holds regardless of details:

> “We haven't confirmed the recruitment timeline. **In any case**, we'll need to discuss feasibility before committing.”

It may also move a conversation forward, but it more strongly implies “regardless of what has just been considered.”

### E. Returning to an earlier topic

Sometimes the discussion moves away from an important question before you've obtained the information you need.

#### E1. Natural return expressions

- “**Coming back to** what you said earlier...”
- “**Going back to** your point about recruitment...”
- “**To return to** the question of supervision...”
- “**I wanted to revisit** something you mentioned earlier.”
- “**Before we leave that topic**, could I clarify one thing?”
- “**There was one earlier point** I wanted to follow up on.”

**Revisit** means return to something for further consideration.

> “I'd like to revisit the project timeline once we've discussed the data requirements.”

This is natural in professional American English.

#### E2. Return after the professor changes the topic

Professor: “We usually work on projects with existing datasets. By the way, have you taken any courses in statistical modeling?”  
Student: “Yes, I've studied epidemiological statistics and worked on analyses in R.”  
Professor: “Great.”  
Student: “Could I come back to the dataset question for a moment? I'm curious how students usually access the data for their projects.”

The student answers the new question first, then returns politely.

#### E3. Returning without suggesting the professor made a mistake

Compare:

> “You didn't answer my question about funding.”

This may sound accusatory even if the question was overlooked.

> “Could I come back to one question about funding? I wanted to make sure I understood the usual arrangements.”

The second is less confrontational and gives the professor room to clarify. Funding details should be verified with the relevant institution; this example teaches wording, not funding policy.

#### E4. “Where were we?”

**Where were we?** is a conversational way to resume after an interruption or digression.

Professor: “Sorry, I had to take that call. Where were we?”  
Student: “We were discussing how students develop thesis questions.”

A student may also say:

> “Right, we were talking about the project structure.”

This helps restore shared context.

### F. Moving between small talk and the academic agenda

#### F1. From greetings to substance

Professor: “How have things been in Hangzhou?”  
Student: “Pretty busy with my internship, but good overall. How about you?”  
Professor: “Busy as well. Anyway, let's get started.”  
Student: “Absolutely.”

The professor has signaled the transition; the student can simply follow.

#### F2. If you need to initiate the transition

Sometimes the opening small talk continues, and it may be useful to move toward the purpose of the meeting.

- “It's been great chatting. Would it be alright if we talked a little about your current projects?”
- “Actually, that connects to something I was hoping to discuss today.”
- “Speaking of research, I'd love to hear about what your group is working on.”
- “Maybe we could start with the research interests you mentioned in your email.”

The last example assumes such an email actually exists; adapt to the real context.

#### F3. When the professor sets the agenda

Professor: “I'd like to hear about your background first, then we can discuss possible projects.”  
Student: “That sounds good. I'd be happy to start with a brief overview.”

You don't need to impose a separate sequence. Following the professor's proposed agenda is a valid form of topic management.

#### F4. Signaling a change without sounding like a presentation

Compare:

> “Now, I will proceed to the second section of my prepared remarks.”

This sounds excessively scripted for ordinary one-on-one conversation.

> “There was one other thing I wanted to ask.”

Natural and efficient.

In seminar presentations, more structured signposting may be appropriate. In informal meetings, use conversational transitions.

### G. Redirecting the conversation diplomatically

A discussion may drift, become overly detailed, or move toward a topic you're not ready to address.

#### G1. Redirecting after a useful digression

> “That's really helpful. Could we come back to the main research question for a moment?”

> “I think that connects to the broader issue of feasibility. Could we return to that?”

> “That's something I'd like to explore further. For now, could we focus on the study design?”

The acknowledgment reduces abruptness; the request establishes the intended focus.

#### G2. If the professor asks about something unrelated to your planned answer

Professor: “I noticed you also have a public health internship. What have you been doing there?”  
Student: “It's given me a useful perspective on practice outside university. One thing it's made me think about is how research evidence informs public health decisions.”  
Professor: “Interesting. How does that relate to your research interests?”

This is an opportunity to form a natural bridge rather than forcing the meeting back onto a prepared script.

#### G3. When you want to postpone a topic

- “Could we come back to that after we discuss the project structure?”
- “I'd be happy to talk about that later in the meeting.”
- “That might be better to discuss once we know more about the timeline.”
- “Could we park that question for now and return to it later?”

**Park a question / park that for now** = temporarily set it aside for later discussion. Common in workplace meetings, including North American contexts. It can sound efficient; don't use it to dismiss something emotionally important.

#### G4. “Circle back”

**Circle back (to something)** = return to a topic later.

- “Could we circle back to the analysis plan later?”
- “Let's circle back to that after we've discussed the data.”

This is recognizable American workplace English. Some speakers find it cliché or corporate-sounding, but it remains widely understood. **Come back to** is a simpler alternative.

#### G5. When the topic is sensitive or premature

Professor: “Have you decided exactly what field you'll specialize in?”  
Student: “I'm still exploring different fields, so I haven't narrowed that down yet. I'd be interested in learning more about the research questions your group is working on.”

This answers truthfully and moves toward a useful area of discussion without inventing commitments.

### H. Unexpected situations: topic management under pressure

#### Scenario 1 — Professor changes the subject suddenly

Professor: “We have several observational studies. Anyway, tell me about your statistical experience.”  
Student: “Sure. Most of my experience has involved analyzing observational data in R.”

**Lesson:** Recognize the professor's clear topic switch; answer the new question.

#### Scenario 2 — You remembered a question from earlier

Professor: “That's roughly how supervision works in our group.”  
Student: “Thank you. Could I come back to your earlier point about project selection? I'm curious whether students usually choose from existing topics.”

**Lesson:** Label the earlier referent so the listener can follow.

#### Scenario 3 — An unrelated administrative question

Professor: “We use several different datasets.”  
Student: “I see. Could I switch gears briefly and ask about how applications are handled?”

**Lesson:** Announce a deliberate shift when the topical connection is weak.

#### Scenario 4 — The professor gives a long side story

Professor: “That reminds me of a conference I attended years ago...”  
*Later, after the story ends:*  
Student: “That's a fascinating example. Coming back to the project, how would a student typically contribute?”

**Lesson:** Wait for a natural completion and return with a specific question.

#### Scenario 5 — You start a topic that the professor cannot discuss

Student: “Could you tell me about the unpublished results?”  
Professor: “I'm afraid I can't share those details yet.”  
Student: “Of course, I understand. In that case, could I ask more generally about the type of research questions the project addresses?”

**Lesson:** Respect a boundary and move to a permitted level of discussion.

#### Scenario 6 — Two possible next topics

Professor: “We could discuss the project itself or how MSc supervision works.”  
Student: “Both would be useful. Perhaps we could start with the project and then move on to supervision, if time allows?”

**Lesson:** Propose an order and leave room for adjustment.

#### Scenario 7 — Topic drift in a group meeting

Researcher A: “And that reminds me of another study that...”  
Chair: “That's interesting. Could we park that discussion for now? We still need to agree on today's analysis plan.”

**Lesson:** A chair may redirect the meeting more directly due to responsibility for the agenda.

#### Scenario 8 — You want to ask one last question before a shift

Professor: “Let's move on to possible thesis projects.”  
Student: “Absolutely. Before we move on, could I clarify one thing about the data sources you mentioned?”

**Lesson:** The transition boundary creates an opportunity to finish an unresolved question.

#### Scenario 9 — You use “anyway” too abruptly

Colleague: “Recruitment has been really difficult, and the team has been under pressure.”  
Better response: “That sounds challenging. I hope things improve. When you have a moment, I'd also like to ask about the revised timeline.”

**Lesson:** Acknowledgment matters before redirecting away from something consequential.

### I. Extended dialogue: several connected and unrelated transitions

**Professor:** “How has your week been?”

**Student:** “Pretty busy. I've been balancing an internship with my research work.”

**Professor:** “Oh, what kind of internship?”

**Student:** “It's in public health. I've enjoyed seeing how research and practice connect.”

**Professor:** “That's useful experience.”

**Student:** “It really has been. **Speaking of research**, I'd love to hear a little about the projects your group is working on.”

**Professor:** “Sure. One project focuses on clinical trial methodology, while another uses data from a large observational study.”

**Student:** “That's interesting. **On that point**, how do students usually become involved in those different projects?”

**Professor:** “It depends on the student's interests and the data we have available.”

**Student:** “I see. **That brings me to** something else I wanted to ask: do students usually develop a research question together with the supervisor?”

**Professor:** “Yes, typically. Sometimes the question is part of an existing project.”

**Student:** “That makes sense.”

**Professor:** “Do you already know exactly what field you'd like to focus on?”

**Student:** “I have some current interests in clinical and population-based research, but I'm still exploring other fields. I'd prefer to learn more before narrowing my options too early.”

**Professor:** “Of course. We have students with different backgrounds.”

**Student:** “That's helpful to know. **Could I switch gears briefly** and ask how the overall MSc program is organized?”

**Professor:** “Sure. There's a coursework component and a research project, but the details depend on the program.”

**Student:** “Thanks. **Coming back to** the earlier point about thesis questions, would it be useful for a prospective student to prepare a research idea beforehand?”

**Professor:** “A broad idea is useful, but it doesn't have to be fully developed.”

**Student:** “Got it. That's reassuring.”

**Professor:** “Anyway, we should probably talk about the next steps.”

**Student:** “Absolutely.”

#### I1. Analysis of transitions

| Expression | What it does |
|---|---|
| “Speaking of research...” | Connects internship discussion to research projects |
| “On that point...” | Extends a related topic |
| “That brings me to...” | Links a question to a prior answer |
| “Could I switch gears briefly...?” | Announces a less-related administrative topic |
| “Coming back to...” | Reopens an earlier unresolved question |
| “Anyway, we should probably...” | Professor moves from discussion to next steps |
| “Absolutely.” | Student accepts the transition |

### J. Phrase bank, contrasts, and core principles

#### J1. Functional expressions

| Purpose | Expression |
|---|---|
| Connected subject | “Speaking of...” |
| Personal association | “That reminds me of...” |
| Related move | “On that note...” |
| Logical bridge | “That brings me to...” |
| Related point | “Along those lines...” |
| Incidental question | “By the way...” |
| Deliberate switch | “Could we switch gears for a moment?” |
| Agenda transition | “Moving on to...” |
| New question | “There's something else I'd like to ask.” |
| Earlier point | “Coming back to your earlier point...” |
| Reopen issue | “I'd like to revisit...” |
| Pause topic | “Could we park that for now?” |
| Return later | “Let's circle back to that.” |
| Regain focus | “Could we return to the main question?” |
| Clarify before leaving | “Before we move on...” |
| Resume after interruption | “Where were we?” |
| Relate to one area | “When it comes to...” |
| Contrast topics | “As for...” |
| Formal topic marker | “Regarding...” |

#### J2. Core principles

1. **Name the connection when helpful.** A good transition explains how a new question relates to the prior one.
2. **Use “speaking of” for related topics; “by the way” for an additional side issue; “anyway” often to leave a digression or return to the main purpose.**
3. **Don't force a fake link.** A clear “Could I switch gears?” is better than awkwardly stretching “Speaking of...”
4. **Give the professor's agenda priority when they establish one.** Topic management is collaborative.
5. **Return to an important unanswered point explicitly.** “Coming back to...” and “Could I revisit...” are useful.
6. **Acknowledge sensitive content before redirecting.** Abrupt transitions can appear dismissive.
7. **Treat parking a topic as a temporary agreement, not silent abandonment.** Return to it when relevant.
8. **Use natural speech rather than a scripted presentation.** A single appropriate transition phrase usually suffices.
