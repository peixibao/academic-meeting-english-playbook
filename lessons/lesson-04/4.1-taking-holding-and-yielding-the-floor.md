# Lesson 04 · Turn-Taking & Conversation Management

## 4.1 Taking, Holding, and Yielding the Floor

**Learning objectives:** Understand how English speakers coordinate turns; recognize possible turn endings; enter a discussion naturally; keep speaking while organizing an answer; and invite others to contribute.

Lesson 04 addresses the conversation itself: who speaks, when, how long, and how a discussion moves forward. This section introduces the mechanics of taking, holding, and yielding a turn. Further details follow in 4.2 (interruptions), 4.3 (topic changes) and 4.4 (pacing and group discussions).

### A. Understanding turn-taking in spoken English

#### A1. What is turn-taking?

**Turn-taking（话轮转换）** is the process by which speakers coordinate who speaks, when they speak, and when the conversation moves between them.

Professor: “What kind of research have you been involved in?”  
Student: “Most of my experience has been in observational epidemiology.”  
Professor: “Could you tell me a little more about one of those projects?”  
Student: “Of course. One project involved examining factors associated with sleep-related health outcomes.”

Each speaker contributes a **turn**. Actual speech includes pauses, hesitation, overlap, interruptions, and delayed answers; the turns are less tidy than in a transcript.

#### A2. Three fundamental actions

| Action | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Taking the floor** | Begin your contribution | “Could I jump in for a second?” |
| **Holding the floor** | Signal you're continuing | “There are two things I'd like to mention. First...” |
| **Yielding the floor** | Allow or invite the next contribution | “But I'd be interested in hearing your thoughts.” |

**The floor（发言权）** is the conversational opportunity to speak. “I'll hand the floor over to Dr. Chen” and “You have the floor” are conventional meeting expressions, somewhat formal in an organized discussion.

#### A3. How the next speaker is selected

Conversation analysis（会话分析）commonly distinguishes:

1. The current speaker selects someone else: “Peixi, what do you think?”
2. Another participant takes a turn at a possible completion point.
3. The current speaker continues if nobody else takes the floor.

**Selected participant**

Professor: “Peixi, what do you think about that approach?”  
Student: “It could be useful, particularly if we're interested in population-level outcomes.”

**Next speaker self-selects**

Professor: “That's one of the main challenges we encountered.”  
Student: “How did your team approach it?”

**Current speaker continues**

Professor: “There are several limitations to consider.”  
*Pause.*  
Professor: “The first is the relatively small sample size.”

A silence is not automatically an invitation to speak.

### B. Recognizing when someone has finished speaking

The most reliable judgment combines grammar, intonation and conversational context.

#### B1. Three kinds of signals

| Signal | What to notice |
|---|---|
| **Grammatical completion** | Sentence or clause seems complete |
| **Prosodic completion（韵律完成）** | Intonation and rhythm suggest an ending |
| **Interactional completion（互动完成）** | Speaker's intended action is complete or invites an answer |

#### B2. Grammatical completion

“The main reason we chose this approach was...” is grammatically unfinished.

“The main reason we chose this approach was its flexibility.” is complete, but the speaker might add:

> “And another consideration was the availability of data.”

Grammatical completion offers a *possible* entry point, not certain proof of a completed turn.

#### B3. Intonation and pitch

“There are three main reasons we chose this design...” often sounds as if a list will follow.

“So that's why we ultimately chose this design.” often suggests a conclusion.

Falling intonation can accompany completion, but it is not sufficient alone. Use grammar, voice and the communicative purpose together.

#### B4. Explicit invitations to speak

| Expression | Purpose |
|---|---|
| “What do you think?” | Invite an opinion |
| “Does that make sense?” | Check comprehension |
| “Any questions so far?” | Invite questions |
| “How about you?” | Ask for reciprocal input |
| “I'd be interested in your perspective.” | Invite a view |
| “What's been your experience?” | Ask for an example |
| “Does that answer your question?” | Check whether more explanation is needed |
| “Anything you'd like to add?” | Invite further information |

#### B5. “Does that make sense?”

This phrase is very common in American English.

Professor: “We adjusted for age and sex, but didn't include those variables in the interaction term. Does that make sense?”

**You understand:** “Yes, that makes sense. Thanks for explaining.”

**You mostly understand:** “Yes, I think so. Just to check, those variables were included as covariates, but not as interaction terms?”

**You need clarification:** “I think I follow the general idea, but could you clarify that last part?”

Section 05 will cover listening and comprehension repair more comprehensively.

### C. Knowing when to speak and when to keep listening

#### C1. Different types of pauses

| Pause | Possible meaning | Typical response |
|---|---|---|
| Thinking pause | Searching for words | Give time |
| Planning pause | Preparing the next part | Usually wait |
| Turn-completion pause | Thought has ended | You may contribute |
| Invitation pause | Awaiting your answer | Answer or ask |
| Technical delay | Audio/video lag | Check who is speaking |

Professor: “We were thinking about... um... possibly expanding the study to other sites.”  
A brief “Mm-hmm” can show attention without interrupting.

Professor: “So those are the main projects we're working on.”  
Student: “Thanks for the overview. Could I ask how MSc students typically get involved?”

#### C2. Unsure if someone has finished?

Tentative entries include:

- “Can I ask something about that?”
- “Could I jump in for a second?”
- “If I could just add something...”
- “There was one point I wanted to ask about.”

If the professor continues, pause and let them finish.

#### C3. Backchannels vs. full turns

As discussed in Lesson 02, **backchannels（倾听反馈语）** show attention without necessarily taking the floor.

Professor: “We've been working on this study for about two years.”  
Student: “Mm-hmm.”  
Professor: “One of the biggest challenges has been recruitment.”  
Student: “Right.”  
Professor: “We've revised our recruitment strategy several times.”  
Student: “I see.”

Compare an actual question:

Professor: “We've revised our recruitment strategy several times.”  
Student: “What prompted those changes?”

“Mm-hmm,” “right,” and “I see” may encourage continuation, while a substantive question normally seeks a response. Their function still depends on tone and context.

### D. Taking the floor naturally

#### D1. Enter with a question

- “Could I ask something about that?”
- “Can I ask a quick follow-up?”
- “Could I jump in with a question?”
- “There's something I'd like to clarify.”
- “I was curious about one thing you mentioned.”

Example:

Professor: “Our projects involve collaboration across several departments.”  
Student: “Could I ask something about that?”  
Professor: “Of course.”  
Student: “How are responsibilities usually divided across the departments?”

#### D2. Add a point

| Expression | Typical nuance |
|---|---|
| “I'd like to add something here.” | Clear, professional |
| “Can I just add something?” | Conversational |
| “If I could add one thing...” | Polite |
| “One thing I'd add is...” | Natural and direct |
| “Something else worth mentioning is...” | Additional consideration |
| “I'd also point out that...” | Substantive observation |
| “Just to build on that...” | Extend the previous point |

Professor: “Study feasibility depends partly on recruitment.”  
Student: “One thing I'd add is that data completeness can also affect feasibility, especially in observational studies.”

#### D3. “Jump in”

**Jump in** = start speaking or participating.

- “Can I jump in for a second?”
- “Feel free to jump in if you have questions.”
- “Sorry to jump in, but I had a quick question.”
- “Let me jump in here for a moment.”

**Register:** Friendly-professional, common in American workplaces and academic meetings.

**Jump in vs. interrupt**

“Can I jump in for a second?” usually sounds collaborative.

“Can I interrupt you for a second?” explicitly acknowledges the interruption.

“Sorry to interrupt” is useful if someone is clearly in the middle of speaking. Detailed interruption strategies come in Section 4.2.

#### D4. “Build on someone's point”

**Build on** = extend or develop a previous idea.

- “I'd like to build on your point about recruitment.”
- “Just to build on what you said...”
- “That's an interesting point, and I'd add that...”
- “Building on that, we could also consider...”

Professor: “Data comparability across study sites is a challenge.”  
Student: “Just to build on that, differences in variable measurement could also affect comparability.”  
Professor: “Exactly.”

#### D5. “Pick up on something”

**Pick up on** = notice or return to a point:

- “I'd like to pick up on something you mentioned earlier.”
- “Could I pick up on your earlier point about study design?”

**Build on** emphasizes extending the idea; **pick up on** emphasizes selecting a previous idea as the next focus.

#### D6. Softening an entry with “just”

- “Can I **just** add something?”
- “I **just** wanted to mention...”
- “Could I **just** clarify one thing?”
- “Let me **just** make one point.”

*Just* often presents the turn as brief or limited. Avoid stacking minimizers: “I just wanted to maybe quickly add just one little thing...” A simple “Can I add something?” is fine.

### E. Holding the floor while organizing an answer

#### E1. Organizational language

Previewing your structure lets listeners know your turn will continue:

> “There are two reasons I'm interested in this area. The first is my academic background, and the second is my research experience.”

Other possibilities:

- “There are a couple of things I'd like to mention.”
- “I'd highlight two main points.”
- “The first thing is...”
- “Another consideration is...”
- “And the final point is...”

These can occur in spontaneous conversations, not only formal presentations.

#### E2. “The first thing I'd say is...”

Professor: “What interests you about interdisciplinary research?”  
Student: “Well, the first thing I'd say is that my undergraduate training has covered several areas of medicine and public health.”  
Student: “And another reason is that I've found it useful to look at clinical questions from different perspectives.”

#### E3. Holding the floor while thinking

| Expression | Function |
|---|---|
| “Let me think for a second.” | Brief time to formulate a response |
| “Let me put it this way...” | Alternative explanation |
| “How should I put this?” | Search for wording |
| “What I'm trying to say is...” | Reformulation |
| “I guess what I mean is...” | Clarify intended meaning |
| “The way I see it...” | Introduce a viewpoint |
| “There are a few aspects to that.” | Signal a multipart answer |

Professor: “What kind of research environment would suit you best?”  
Student: “That's a good question. Let me think for a second.”  
*Brief pause.*  
Student: “I guess the main thing I'm looking for is an environment where I can explore different research questions and gradually become more independent.”

You can take a thinking pause without filling every second.

#### E4. “Let me put it this way”

Student: “I'm interested in research that involves multiple fields. Let me put it this way: I enjoy situations where clinical questions and public health perspectives inform each other.”

It announces reformulation. In some contexts it precedes a more candid statement: “Let me put it this way: the project would require more time than we currently have.”

#### E5. Signaling continuation

- “There's also another point.”
- “And that brings me to...”
- “The other thing is...”
- “One more thing I'd mention is...”
- “That leads to my second point.”

Student: “One main challenge was missing data.”  
*Short pause.*  
Student: “But there's another aspect worth mentioning.”

#### E6. Someone starts speaking before you've finished

Student: “One thing that interested me was the relationship between—”  
Professor: “The clinical and population-level aspects?”  
Student: “Exactly, yes. And what I was going to add is that I'd like to explore how those perspectives can be combined.”

Useful:

- “Exactly. And what I was going to add is...”
- “Right, and the other thing is...”
- “Yes, that's part of it. I was also thinking about...”

See 4.2 for a comprehensive treatment of overlap and interruption.

### F. Yielding the floor and inviting another person to speak

You can yield simply by finishing and pausing, or you can invite a response explicitly.

#### F1. Common expressions

| Expression | Effect |
|---|---|
| “What do you think?” | Invite an opinion |
| “I'd be interested in hearing your thoughts.” | Polite invitation |
| “How does that sound to you?” | Ask for evaluation of a plan |
| “What's your take on that?” | Conversational opinion request |
| “I'd love to hear your perspective.” | Warm professional invitation |
| “Does that align with your experience?” | Invite comparison |
| “Does that make sense?” | Check understanding |

#### F2. “What's your take on that?”

**Take** as a noun = an interpretation or opinion.

- “What's your take on the new approach?”
- “I'd be interested in your take on this.”
- “That's my take, anyway.”

This is common American conversational English, including many professional meetings. A more formal alternative: “I'd be interested in hearing your perspective.”

#### F3. “How does that sound?”

Student: “Perhaps I could start with a brief introduction, then we could discuss your current projects. How does that sound?”  
Professor: “That sounds good.”

Compare:

- “What do you think?” — Broad opinion.
- “How does that sound?” — Evaluation of a proposal.
- “Would that work for you?” — Practical acceptability or feasibility.

They overlap, but each emphasizes a different function.

#### F4. Yield without asking a question every time

Student: “My main experience has been in observational epidemiology, particularly data analysis and interpretation.”  
*Pause.*  
Professor: “I see. What would you like to explore next?”

A complete contribution plus pause is often sufficient. Ending every answer with “What do you think?” may be artificial.

#### F5. Handing over to a particular speaker

In group meetings:

- “Maybe Alex could speak to that.”
- “I'd be interested in hearing what Sarah thinks.”
- “Would you like to add anything?”
- “I'll let Michael take it from here.”
- “Over to you, Sarah.”

**Over to you** = handing over a meeting or presentation segment.

Team lead: “That's the overview of recruitment. Over to you, Sarah.”  
Sarah: “Thanks. I'll walk everyone through data collection.”

### G. Turn-taking across academic meeting types

#### G1. One-on-one supervisor meetings

- “Could I ask something about that?”
- “That's interesting. I was wondering...”
- “One thing I'd like to mention is...”
- “If I could add one point...”

Professor: “Most projects involve collaboration with clinicians.”  
Student: “That's something I find interesting about clinical research. Could I ask how students typically become involved in those collaborations?”  
Professor: “Of course. It depends on the project.”

The student connects to the content, asks a question, and yields.

#### G2. Research-group meetings

Researcher A: “We should consider adjusting for another variable.”  
Researcher B: “Could I add something here?”  
Researcher A: “Sure.”  
Researcher B: “I think we should first clarify whether it's a confounder in the proposed model.”  
Researcher C: “That's a useful point.”

Group conversations can require more explicit entry signals than a two-person discussion.

#### G3. Seminars and presentations

Seminar speakers may say:

- “Any questions so far?”
- “I'm happy to take questions.”
- “We have time for one or two questions.”
- “Let's open it up for discussion.”

**Open something up for discussion** = invite a group to discuss it.

The chair or moderator may select the next speaker.

#### G4. Video meetings

Video calls can have audio delay, accidental overlap, confusing gaze direction and muted participants.

- “I think we started talking at the same time.”
- “Sorry, go ahead.”
- “You go first.”
- “I was going to add something about the analysis.”
- “Could I come in on that point?”

Short explicit signals can help.

### H. Unexpected situations and floor management

#### Scenario 1 — Mid-explanation pause

Professor: “We initially considered a cross-sectional design, but...”  
*Pause.*  
Student: “Mm-hmm.”  
Professor: “...we realized a longitudinal approach would help us understand changes over time.”  
Student: “So you'd be able to examine changes within participants?”  
Professor: “Exactly.”

Use a minimal listener signal during a thinking pause, then a full question at possible completion.

#### Scenario 2 — One last point before moving on

Professor: “That covers the main findings. Shall we move on?”  
Student: “Actually, could I add one quick point before we move on?”  
Professor: “Of course.”  
Student: “There was one limitation worth discussing.”

#### Scenario 3 — Simultaneous speech

Professor: “I think we could—”  
Student: “One thing I—”  
*Both stop.*  
Student: “Oh, sorry. Go ahead.”  
Professor: “No, that's fine. What were you going to say?”  
Student: “I was going to ask whether the project would involve an existing dataset.”

Short acknowledgments restore conversational order.

#### Scenario 4 — Multipart answer

Professor: “What have you learned from research projects?”  
Student: “I'd highlight two main things. First, having a clear question before analysis is crucial.”  
Professor: “Mm-hmm.”  
Student: “Second, limitations in the data affect what conclusions we can reasonably draw.”  
Professor: “That's important.”

Announcing two points helps hold the floor appropriately.

#### Scenario 5 — Unexpected invitation for an opinion

Professor: “That's how we've approached the problem. What's your take?”  
Student: “My first thought is that feasibility is important, although I'd need to understand the data structure better.”

**My first thought is...** introduces a preliminary view without claiming exhaustive analysis.

#### Scenario 6 — Relevant point from another speaker

Researcher A: “We had some missing data.”  
Student: “Could I jump in with a quick point?”  
Researcher A: “Sure.”  
Student: “It might be useful to distinguish missing outcome data from missing covariate data.”  
Researcher A: “That's a good distinction.”

#### Scenario 7 — Keeping an introduction brief

Professor: “Could you briefly explain the project?”  
Student: “It examines a clinical question using observational data. My role involved analysis and interpretation.”  
*Pause.*  
Professor: “What analysis did you do?”  
Student: “Regression models to examine associations between exposures and outcomes.”

A concise answer lets the other person select the next area to explore.

#### Scenario 8 — Another participant is explicitly selected

Professor: “I'd like to hear what our statistician thinks.”  
Student: “Of course.”  
Statistician: “My concern is the outcome distribution.”  
Professor: “Could you explain how that would affect the model?”

Respect selected turns.

### I. Extended dialogue: Managing a spontaneous academic conversation

Professor: “Peixi, could you tell me a little about your research experience?”  
Student: “Of course. I'd highlight two main areas. Most of my experience has been in observational epidemiology, and I've been involved in different stages of the research process.”  
Professor: “What analyses have you worked on?”  
Student: “Mainly regression-based analyses. One thing I've learned is how important it is to understand the research question before choosing an approach.”  
Professor: “Yes, that's certainly important.”  
Student: “And the other thing I'd add is that working with real data has made me more aware of missing information and measurement limitations.”  
Professor: “Those can be major challenges.”  
*Brief pause.*  
Student: “Could I ask something related to that?”  
Professor: “Of course.”  
Student: “In your group, how are students involved in decisions about study design and analysis?”  
Professor: “Some students work with existing data, while others are involved in planning studies.”  
Student: “I see.”  
Professor: “We encourage them to take responsibility for one part of a project.”  
Student: “Could I follow up on that point?”  
Professor: “Sure.”  
Student: “What does taking responsibility typically involve in practice?”  
Professor: “A student might coordinate an analysis, develop a question, or prepare a manuscript.”  
Student: “So the level of responsibility depends on the project and role.”  
Professor: “Exactly. Does that sound like the kind of experience you're looking for?”  
Student: “Yes, developing independence is something I'd value. I'm still exploring different fields, so I'd also like to learn about the range of projects available.”  
Professor: “Let me tell you about our current projects.”  
Student: “Sure, I'd love to hear about them.”

#### I1. Function analysis

| Expression | Purpose |
|---|---|
| “I'd highlight two main areas.” | Organize and hold turn |
| “One thing I've learned is...” | Introduce a point |
| “And the other thing I'd add is...” | Continue |
| “Could I ask something related?” | Enter with question |
| “I see.” | Backchannel |
| “Could I follow up on that point?” | Request another turn |
| “So the level of responsibility depends on...” | Confirm understanding |
| “Does that sound like...” | Select next speaker |
| “Let me tell you about...” | Announce new contribution |
| “I'd love to hear about them.” | Yield the floor |

You don't need to request permission for every follow-up. The phrase is useful when it helps manage turns.

### J. Essential expressions and core principles

#### J1. Functional phrase bank

| Function | Expression | Register |
|---|---|---|
| Enter with a question | “Could I ask something about that?” | Professional |
| Enter briefly | “Can I jump in for a second?” | Friendly-professional |
| Add a point | “One thing I'd add is...” | Professional |
| Extend a point | “Just to build on that...” | Professional |
| Return to an earlier point | “I'd like to pick up on something you mentioned.” | Professional |
| Introduce a question | “I was curious about one thing.” | Friendly-professional |
| Hold the floor | “There are two things I'd like to mention.” | Professional |
| Organize an answer | “The first thing I'd say is...” | Conversational |
| Reformulate | “Let me put it this way...” | Conversational |
| Think | “Let me think for a second.” | Conversational |
| Continue after overlap | “And what I was going to add is...” | Professional |
| Add a final point | “There's one more thing I'd mention.” | Professional |
| Invite opinion | “What do you think?” | Neutral |
| Invite perspective | “I'd be interested in hearing your thoughts.” | Professional |
| Ask for a take | “What's your take on that?” | Friendly-professional |
| Evaluate plan | “How does that sound?” | Friendly-professional |
| Check feasibility | “Would that work for you?” | Neutral-professional |
| Hand over | “Over to you.” | Group meeting |
| Yield after overlap | “Sorry, go ahead.” | Neutral |
| Transition | “Before we move on...” | Neutral-professional |

#### J2. Key expressions

1. **Can I jump in?** — Friendly way to enter a conversation.
2. **I'd like to build on that.** — Develop someone else's point.
3. **Let me put it this way.** — Reformulate an explanation.
4. **What's your take on that?** — Ask for an opinion.
5. **Before we move on...** — Ask one last thing before changing topics.
6. **Over to you.** — Hand the floor to someone else.

#### J3. Common problems

| Problem | Better approach |
|---|---|
| Treat every pause as turn completion | Combine grammatical, vocal and interactional cues |
| Explain everything without pause | Organize the answer and invite follow-ups |
| Ask permission for every routine contribution | Enter directly when a turn is clearly available |
| Interrupt thinking pauses | Allow thinking time and use minimal listening signals |
| Stay silent when asked a question | Acknowledge and begin |
| Lose your place after overlap | “What I was going to add is...” |
| End every answer with “What do you think?” | A complete answer and pause can suffice |

#### J4. Core principles

1. Turn-taking is cooperative.
2. Grammatical completion can create a possible entry point.
3. Backchannels and full turns perform different functions.
4. Taking the floor can be explicit or implicit.
5. Previewing a multipart answer helps hold the floor.
6. Yielding can be a pause or an invitation.
7. Accidental overlap is normal and usually easily repaired.
8. One-on-one meetings, seminars, groups and video calls have different conventions.
9. Aim for clear, appropriately timed contributions.
