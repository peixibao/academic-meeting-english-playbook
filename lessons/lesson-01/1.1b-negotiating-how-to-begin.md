# Lesson 01 · 1.1b Negotiating How to Begin a Meeting

**Focus:** When a professor invites you to begin, how to choose your topic, appropriate level of detail, or who speaks first. The sample wording below is instructor-authored and transferable to any academic meeting.

## A. Identify the actual speech act

| Kind of invitation | What it means | What to do |
|---|---|---|
| **Floor allocation（分配话轮）** | “Would you like to start?” | Accept the first turn and propose where to begin. |
| **Topic selection（选择议题）** | “What would you like to discuss?” | Suggest one or two priorities, allow the professor to adjust. |
| **Content request（具体内容请求）** | “Tell me about your research experience.” | Answer the requested topic directly. |

Being offered the first turn doesn't obligate you to give a long presentation.

## B. Invitations you may hear

| Professor's wording | Likely meaning |
|---|---|
| “Would you like to start?” | General floor invitation |
| “Why don't you go ahead?” | Encouragement to begin |
| “Feel free to start whenever you're ready.” | Invitation without a strict start time |
| “Would you like to kick things off?” | Friendly, conversational opening |
| “I'll let you start.” | Explicit turn handover |
| “The floor is yours.” | Idiomatic handover |
| “Where would you like to begin?” | You may select the topic |
| “Why don't you tell me a little about yourself?” | Request for an introduction |
| “What were you hoping to get out of our discussion?” | Invitation to clarify expectations |
| “Was there anything in particular you wanted to cover?” | Ask for priorities |
| “Would you prefer to go first, or should I?” | Choice of speaker |
| “Should we start with your background or with my current projects?” | Proposed choice of topics |

**Idioms:** `kick things off` = start an activity; `take the lead` = guide the opening; `hand over the floor` = yield the turn.

## C. Three legitimate ways to respond to an open invitation

**You begin with background**

“Sure. Perhaps I could start with a brief overview of my academic background and research experience.”

**You begin with interests**

“I thought it might be helpful to start with my research interests and what I'm hoping to gain from the MSc.”

**You invite the professor to begin**

“I'd actually be very interested in hearing about your current projects first, if that works for you.”

**You offer both options**

“I'd be happy to give you a quick introduction, or we could start with your current work—whichever would be more useful.”

**You suggest a short sequence**

“Perhaps I could briefly introduce myself, and then we could discuss where our research interests might overlap.”

**Pragmatics（语用）:**
- “Perhaps I could...” suggests without directing.
- “I thought it might be helpful...” gives a reason.
- “Whichever would be more useful” offers flexibility.
- Once the professor chooses, follow their lead rather than repeatedly negotiating.

## D. Different questions require different answers

| Prompt | Primary focus | Useful initial length |
|---|---|---|
| “Tell me a little about yourself.” | Degree + research overview + motivation | 40–60 seconds |
| “Walk me through your research background.” | Previous work + methods + personal contribution | 60–90 seconds |
| “What are you currently working on?” | Present research activity | 30–60 seconds |
| “What are your research interests?” | Questions and fields you want to pursue | 30–60 seconds |
| “What would you like to work on during your MSc?” | Future potential thesis / training | 30–60 seconds |
| “Why my group?” | Research fit and a specific scholarly connection | 30–60 seconds |
| “What would you like to discuss today?” | Meeting priorities | 15–30 seconds |
| “Tell me more about your [specific project].” | Research question, design, your role, limitations | 60–90 seconds |

These are practical starting ranges, not mandatory time limits.

### D1. Tell me about yourself — Degree → Research → Interest → Goal

“I'm currently completing a degree in [FIELD] at [INSTITUTION]. My research experience has mainly involved [STUDY TYPE], particularly [TOPICS]. Those projects made me increasingly interested in [METHODOLOGICAL QUESTION], which I'd like to explore further through a research MSc.”

### D2. Tell me about your research experience — Research area → Projects → My role → Learning

“I've worked on studies involving [TOPIC A] and [TOPIC B]. My responsibilities have included [TRUE CONTRIBUTIONS]. In particular, working on [REAL CHALLENGE] made me more careful about [INSIGHT]. I'd be happy to discuss either project in more detail.”

### D3. What are your research interests? — Questions and future direction

“My main interests are in [AREA], especially how [METHOD / DESIGN] affects what we can conclude about [CLINICAL QUESTION]. Most of my experience so far is in [EXPERIENCE]. I'd like to build on that background while learning more about [NEW METHODS].”

### D4. What are you currently working on? — Specific, up-to-date work

“Recently, I've been working on [CURRENT PROJECT], focusing on [CURRENT TASK]. I've been particularly interested in [QUESTION / CHALLENGE].”

Do not present finished work as ongoing.

### D5. What were you hoping to discuss today? — Suggested agenda

“I was hoping we could explore where our interests overlap and whether there might be suitable research opportunities. I'd also love to learn about your current projects and how you typically work with graduate students.”

## E. Negotiating the structure — complete conversation branches

### Scenario 1: The professor offers a choice

**Professor:** “Would you like to tell me about your background, or should I introduce our current projects first?”

**Student, option A:** “I'd be happy to start with a brief introduction. That might help give some context before we discuss projects.”

**Student, option B:** “I'd love to hear about your current projects first. I think that would help me understand where my interests could fit.”

**Student, option C:** “Perhaps I could give you a quick overview of my background, and then I'd love to hear about your group's work.”

### Scenario 2: No agenda was set

**Professor:** “We haven't really set an agenda for today. Was there anything in particular you wanted to cover?”  
**Student:** “Yes. I was hoping we could explore where our interests overlap and discuss possible MSc projects. I'd also be interested in hearing about your approach to supervision.”  
**Professor:** “Sure. Tell me a little about your research interests first.”  
**Student:** “Absolutely. Broadly, I'm interested in [FIELD]...”

### Scenario 3: No specific thesis project is available yet

**Professor:** “I don't have a specific MSc topic lined up, but I'd be happy to hear your interests.”  
**Student:** “I have a few broad areas of interest, especially [AREA A] and [AREA B]. I'd be open to developing a feasible question around an existing project or dataset.”  
**Professor:** “Would working with clinical trial data interest you?”  
**Student:** “Definitely. I'm particularly interested in [ASPECT], although most of my direct experience has been with [CURRENT AREA].”

### Scenario 4: Very open invitation

**Professor:** “So, how can I help?”  
**Student:** “Thank you. I was hoping to learn more about your current research and explore whether there might be a good fit for a research-based MSc. I'd also like to understand what methodological skills I could develop through the project.”

### Scenario 5: Ambiguous “your work”

**Professor:** “Why don't you tell me a little about your work?”

You may give a concise overview immediately:  
“Sure. My research so far has mainly involved [AREA]. I've worked on [TWO TOPICS], and my role has focused on [SKILLS].”

Or ask to clarify *scope*:  
“Of course. Would you prefer a general overview, or would it be more useful if I focused on one project?”

**Professor:** “Either is fine.”  
**Student:** “Great. I'll start with a quick overview, and I'm happy to expand on anything that interests you.”

### Scenario 6: The professor must leave early

**Professor:** “I only have about fifteen minutes, I'm afraid.”  
**Student:** “Of course. I can keep my introduction brief. Perhaps we could focus on your current thesis opportunities and the possible research fit?”

## F. Calibrating answer length — progressive disclosure（渐进式展开）

**15–20 seconds — Elevator overview**  
“My background is in [FIELD], and most of my experience has been in [RESEARCH TYPE]. I'm particularly interested in [AREA] and [METHOD].”

**45–60 seconds — Standard overview**  
“My degree is in [FIELD]. I've worked on [TOPIC A] and [TOPIC B], with responsibilities including [ACTUAL CONTRIBUTIONS]. Through these projects, I've become interested in [METHODOLOGICAL ISSUE], and I'd like to pursue that through a research MSc.”

**90 seconds — One focused project**  
1. Research question — “We wanted to understand whether...”
2. Study design — “We used [DESIGN / DATA].”
3. Your role — “I was responsible for...”
4. Limitation — “A challenge was...”
5. Learning / future link — “That made me think about...”

Long answers are expansions of concise answers, not a requirement for every opening.

## G. Handing the conversation back naturally

| Purpose | Spoken language |
|---|---|
| Invite questions | “I'd be happy to go into more detail if that would be helpful.” |
| Offer an example | “I could talk a little more about that project if you're interested.” |
| Invite their perspective | “I'd be interested to hear how that connects with your group's work.” |
| Offer a methods focus | “I can also explain some of the methodological challenges we encountered.” |
| Propose next topic | “Perhaps we could talk about the kinds of projects your group is working on.” |
| Give the floor back | “But I'd love to hear more about your current research as well.” |

## H. Distinctions worth remembering

- **Research background:** what you've studied and done.
- **Current research projects:** what you're working on now.
- **Research interests:** questions and fields you care about.
- **Intended research direction:** where you want your work to develop.
- **Potential areas of overlap:** where both people's interests and expertise connect.

“An overview of my research background” is sufficient; *overview* already implies a general summary.

## I. Core takeaway

Before you answer, identify whether you're being asked to speak first, choose a topic, or provide specific information. Answer at the appropriate level of detail, pause, and let the professor's questions guide the expansion.
