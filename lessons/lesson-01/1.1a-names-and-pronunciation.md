# Lesson 01 · 1.1a Names, Pronunciation and Forms of Address

This is an additional **two-way conversational function**, not just a phrase to memorize. The aim is to handle uncertainty about either participant's name gracefully. Examples are instructor-written fictional dialogue.

## A. When you are unsure how to pronounce the professor's name

| Purpose | Natural expression | Nuance |
|---|---|---|
| Ask before attempting the name | “Before we get started, could I check how to pronounce your name?” | Careful and courteous |
| Ask about the surname | “Would you mind telling me how to pronounce your last name?” | Specific and polite |
| You have read, not heard, the name | “I've seen your name written down, but I'm not sure how it's pronounced.” | Gives context |
| Ask for a model | “Could you say your name for me?” | Direct and friendly |
| Invite a correction | “Please feel free to correct me if I get your name wrong.” | Reduces awkwardness |

**Distinguish pronunciation from form of address:**
- “How do you pronounce your name?” — sounds
- “How should I address you?” — title / surname / first name
- “What would you prefer I call you?” — person's preference

## B. When you have already attempted their name

| Purpose | Expression |
|---|---|
| Ask for confirmation | “Did I pronounce your name correctly?” |
| Soft check | “I hope I pronounced your name correctly.” |
| Acknowledge doubt | “I'm not sure I got the pronunciation quite right.” |
| Conversational check | “Did I get your name right?” |
| Explicit invitation | “Please let me know if I'm mispronouncing your name.” |

**Grammar note:** `I hope I pronounced...` refers to a pronunciation you just made. `I hope I'm pronouncing...` refers to how you are currently saying it.

## C. Responding when the professor confirms or corrects YOU

**Correct first attempt**

You: “Did I pronounce your surname correctly?”  
Professor: “Yes, you did.”  
You: “Great, thanks for confirming!”

**Incorrect first attempt**

You: “Did I get that right?”  
Professor: “Actually, it's pronounced [NAME].”  
You: “Ah, thanks for correcting me. [Repeat NAME]. Have I got it right now?”  
Professor: “Exactly.”  
You: “Perfect, thank you.”

**Still unsure**

You: “Sorry, could you say that one more time, a little more slowly?”  
Professor: “[Repeats NAME].”  
You: “I think I've got it now. Thank you.”

Brief acknowledgment is usually enough. There's no need to make repeated apologies.

## D. When the professor asks about YOUR name

**They ask directly**

Professor: “How do you pronounce your given name?”  
You: “Of course. It's Peixi—Pei-xi.”  
You can repeat it at normal speed after saying it slowly.

**They pronounce it correctly**

- “Yes, that's right!”
- “Exactly—you've got it.”
- “Yes, that's how I pronounce it.”
- “You pronounced it perfectly.”

**They are close**

- “That's very close! It's actually Pei-xi.”
- “Almost—there's just a slight difference in the second syllable.”
- “You're really close. The way I pronounce it is Pei-xi.”

**They pronounce it incorrectly**

- “Actually, it's pronounced Pei-xi.”
- “It's Pei-xi, but no worries at all.”
- “The pronunciation is a little different. It's Pei-xi.”

A model is more useful than prolonged explanation: say it slowly once, then naturally.

**They ask which name is which**

Professor: “Is Bao your first name or your family name?”  
You: “Bao is my family name, and Peixi is my given name. You can call me Peixi.”

`Given name` is especially precise when discussing naming conventions across languages.

## E. Adapting to what someone prefers to be called

Professor: “Please, just call me Sameer.”  
You: “Of course, Sameer. Thanks for letting me know.”

`Dr. [Surname]` and `Professor [Surname]` are both appropriate in an initial university meeting when the titles apply. A person's explicit naming preference is useful information.

## F. Complete branching exchanges

**Branch 1 — Both pronounce correctly**

You: “Hello, Professor [Surname]. I hope I pronounced your name correctly.”  
Professor: “You did, thank you. Did I say your name right—Peixi?”  
You: “Yes, exactly. You've got it!”  
Professor: “Wonderful. Shall we get started?”  
You: “Absolutely.”

**Branch 2 — You need correction, then they need correction**

You: “Could I check how you pronounce your surname?”  
Professor: “[NAME].”  
You: “Thanks. [Repeat]. Is that right?”  
Professor: “Exactly. And is your given name pronounced [attempt]?”  
You: “Almost. It's Pei-xi.”  
Professor: “Peixi?”  
You: “Yes, that's right. Thanks for checking!”

**Branch 3 — You are unsure whether to use a title or first name**

You: “Would you prefer Professor [Surname], or should I use your first name?”  
Professor: “Just [First Name] is fine.”  
You: “Of course, [First Name]. It's great to meet you.”

## G. Transferable interaction pattern

**Ask → listen → repeat or confirm → accept correction → continue.**

Names are a cooperative interaction between two speakers; the goal is to get it right respectfully and move on, without turning the greeting into an extended pronunciation test.
