# Lesson 01 · Joining & Greeting

## 1.1 The Opening Exchange: Your First 30–90 Seconds

### A. Understanding the structure of an opening exchange

An opening exchange is the first interaction after two people connect to a meeting.

In an online academic meeting, the opening may involve several **communicative moves（交际动作）**:

1. **Establishing contact** — confirming that both participants can communicate.
2. **Greeting and recognition** — acknowledging one another, particularly when meeting for the first time.
3. **Responding to circumstances** — handling delays, scheduling constraints, or technical difficulties.
4. **Expressing appreciation** — recognizing the other person's time and willingness to meet.
5. **Handing over the floor** — allowing the other person to introduce the next topic.

These moves are flexible. Their order depends on what happens during the call.

An important concept is **adjacency pairs（相邻话轮）**: certain conversational actions naturally invite particular responses.

For example:

| Speaker A | Speaker B |
|---|---|
| “Can you hear me okay?” | “Yes, loud and clear.” |
| “It's great to finally meet you.” | “It's great to meet you too.” |
| “Sorry to keep you waiting.” | “No worries at all.” |
| “Shall we get started?” | “Absolutely. I'm ready whenever you are.” |

A professor may perform several of these actions in a single turn.

**Example:**

**Professor:**

> “Hi, Peixi! Sorry I'm running a little late. My previous meeting ran over. Can you hear me alright? It's great to finally meet you!”

**You:**

> “Hi, Professor Parpia! Yes, loud and clear. Can you hear me okay? And no worries at all about the delay. It's great to finally meet you too. Thanks for making time to speak with me.”

Your response addresses the audio check, acknowledges the delay, reciprocates the greeting, and expresses appreciation.

You can then pause and let the professor continue.

The practical rule is:

**Respond to what's relevant → acknowledge the person → leave room for the next turn.**

### B. Establishing contact: Useful expressions and differences

#### 1. Checking your own audio

| Expression | Usage and nuance |
|---|---|
| Can you hear me okay? | The most versatile option for video calls. |
| Can you hear me alright? | Equally natural; slightly more conversational. |
| Am I coming through clearly? | Focuses specifically on whether your audio is being transmitted properly. |
| How's my audio on your end? | Useful when checking microphone quality or troubleshooting. |
| Is the sound okay on your end? | A natural way to ask whether the connection sounds acceptable. |

Two particularly useful expressions:

##### Come through

In online communication, *come through* refers to the successful transmission of audio or video.

> “My voice seems to be coming through clearly now.”

##### On my end / on your end

These expressions refer to each participant's side of a remote connection.

> “Everything sounds fine on my end. How about yours?”

They also work outside video calls:

> “Everything looks good on my end.”

> “I've completed the analysis on my end.”

#### 2. Confirming that you can hear the other person

| Expression | Nuance |
|---|---|
| Yes, loud and clear. | Concise, idiomatic, and friendly. |
| Yep, I can hear you perfectly. | Relaxed North American spoken English. |
| Yes, you're coming through clearly. | Particularly appropriate for remote communication. |
| I can hear you just fine. | Reassuring and conversational. |
| Everything sounds good on my end. | Confirms that you aren't experiencing any audio issues. |

You don't need to use a complete sentence every time. Short responses are a normal part of spoken professional English.

### C. Greeting someone you have never met

The most appropriate opening depends on how much previous contact you've had.

#### Situation 1: First contact, with no previous correspondence

> “Good morning, Professor [Surname]. It's a pleasure to meet you.”

> “Hello, Dr. [Surname]. Thank you for meeting with me today.”

These work well for formal academic introductions.

#### Situation 2: You've previously exchanged emails

> “Hi, Professor [Surname]. It's great to finally meet you.”

> “It's nice to put a face to the name!”

> “I've been looking forward to speaking with you.”

Two useful expressions deserve closer attention.

##### Finally meet

*Finally* can convey a positive sense of anticipation when two people have already communicated.

> “It's great to finally meet you.”

It implies that the meeting is a welcome continuation of an existing connection.

##### Put a face to the name

This idiom describes meeting someone whose name you already recognize from emails or other communication.

> “It's lovely to finally put a face to the name.”

Its tone is friendly and slightly informal. It is particularly natural after substantial previous correspondence.

#### Situation 3: You have met the person before

> “It's good to see you again.”

> “Great to see you! How have you been?”

> “I'm glad we could catch up.”

*Catch up* is useful when reconnecting after some time. It generally suggests an existing relationship.

#### Forms of address

For an academic you have never met, both **Dr. [Surname]** and **Professor [Surname]** are appropriate when the relevant qualification or title is confirmed.

For Sameer Parpia, either works.

If he says, “Please, just call me Sameer,” you can naturally switch to his first name.

The important distinction is between **professional respect** and **social distance**. Using someone's first name after being invited to do so does not automatically make the conversation less professional.

### D. Expressing appreciation and setting a positive tone

A short expression of appreciation is appropriate in an introductory meeting, especially when the professor has arranged time to speak with you.

| Expression | Particular emphasis |
|---|---|
| Thank you for meeting with me today. | Acknowledges the meeting itself. |
| Thank you for taking the time to speak with me. | Recognizes the time devoted to the conversation. |
| I really appreciate you making time for this. | Recognizes that the other person has made room in their schedule. |
| Thank you for fitting me into your schedule. | Particularly useful when the meeting was arranged at short notice. |
| I'm really glad we could find a time that works for both of us. | Acknowledges scheduling coordination. |
| I've been looking forward to our conversation. | Expresses anticipation and interest. |

#### Making time vs. taking the time

*Make time* emphasizes creating space in an otherwise busy schedule.

> “I really appreciate you making time for this meeting.”

*Take the time* emphasizes the act of devoting time and attention to something.

> “Thank you for taking the time to discuss this with me.”

Both are appropriate in academic settings.

#### A note on register（语域）

“Thanks for taking the time to chat with me” is friendly-professional.

“Thank you for taking the time to speak with me” is neutral-professional.

Both work in an informal faculty meeting. The second is a particularly safe default when meeting someone for the first time.

### E. Managing an Unexpected Opening

Real conversations rarely follow a perfect script. The following scenarios are useful across academic meetings, professional interviews, research collaborations, and online discussions.

#### Scenario 1: The professor joins late and apologizes

**Professor:**

> “I'm so sorry to keep you waiting. My previous meeting went a little longer than expected.”

Possible responses:

- “No worries at all. Thanks for letting me know.”
- “That's perfectly fine. I understand you've got a busy schedule.”
- “No problem at all. I'm glad we could still connect.”
- “Not a problem. I hope the rest of your day is going well.”

Useful terminology:

**Run over** — to exceed the scheduled time.

> “My previous meeting ran over by about ten minutes.”

**Keep someone waiting** — to cause someone to wait.

> “Sorry to keep you waiting.”

#### Scenario 2: You're running late because of a technical issue

**You:**

> “I'm sorry for joining a few minutes late. I was having some trouble connecting, but everything seems to be working now.”

Other natural variations:

- “Apologies for the delay. My internet connection dropped unexpectedly.”
- “Sorry I'm a little late. I had to restart the meeting application.”
- “Thanks for waiting. I was having a problem with my microphone, but I've managed to sort it out.”

*Sort something out* means to resolve a problem.

> “I think I've sorted out the audio issue.”

This expression is common in both British and North American English.

#### Scenario 3: The professor mentions having very limited time

**Professor:**

> “I'm afraid I've only got about fifteen minutes before my next meeting.”

**You:**

> “Of course, thanks for letting me know. I'm happy to keep things brief.”

Other useful responses:

- “Absolutely. We can focus on the main points.”
- “That works for me. I appreciate you fitting this into your schedule.”
- “Of course. Perhaps we could start with the most important questions.”

Two transferable expressions:

**Keep things brief** — communicate concisely.

**Focus on the main points** — prioritize essential topics.

These are useful when presenting, reporting research progress, or answering questions under time constraints.

#### Scenario 4: The professor's microphone is muted

> “I think you might be on mute.”

> “Sorry, I can't hear you. Your microphone may still be muted.”

> “I can see you, but I'm not getting any audio.”

*On mute* is especially common in online meetings.

#### Scenario 5: The professor's voice keeps breaking up

> “Sorry, you're breaking up a little. Could you repeat the last part?”

> “I'm having a bit of trouble hearing you. The audio seems to be cutting in and out.”

> “Sorry, I caught the beginning, but I lost you for a moment.”

Useful distinction:

**Breaking up** — audio is intermittently distorted or interrupted.

**Cutting in and out** — audio repeatedly disappears and returns.

**I lost you for a moment** — you temporarily stopped hearing the speaker.

#### Scenario 6: The connection briefly freezes

> “Sorry, I think the connection froze for a second.”

> “Your video seems to have frozen. Can you still hear me?”

> “I think we had a brief connection issue. Are we good now?”

*Are we good now?* is a conversational way of asking whether the problem has been resolved.

For a slightly more formal option:

> “Does everything seem to be working now?”

#### Scenario 7: The professor joins but doesn't immediately speak

You can initiate the conversation without making the silence awkward.

> “Hi, Professor [Surname]! Can you hear me okay?”

Once the audio is confirmed:

> “It's great to meet you. Thank you for making time to speak with me.”

Then pause.

You have established contact and opened the conversation. There is no need to fill every moment of silence.

#### Scenario 8: The professor immediately begins discussing research

**Professor:**

> “Hi, Peixi. I've had a look at your CV. Could you tell me a little more about your previous research?”

**You:**

> “Absolutely, I'd be happy to. Thanks again for taking the time to meet with me.”

You can then answer the question.

Here, **the professor has already initiated the transition into the main discussion**. Follow that lead.

#### Scenario 9: The professor asks whether you'd like to start

**Professor:**

> “Would you like to start, or shall I give you a quick overview of what we're working on?”

Possible responses:

- “I'd be happy to start with a brief introduction, if that works for you.”
- “I'd actually be very interested in hearing about your current projects first, if you don't mind.”
- “Perhaps I could briefly introduce my background, and then I'd love to hear more about your research.”

These responses show how to **negotiate conversational structure（协商对话结构）** without sounding controlling.

#### Scenario 10: You're asked to wait while the professor finishes setting up

**Professor:**

> “Give me just a moment. I'm trying to get my second screen working.”

**You:**

> “Of course, take your time.”

> “No problem at all.”

> “Sure, no rush.”

*Take your time* is a natural expression that gives the other person permission to proceed without feeling hurried.

### F. Putting it together: Three complete opening exchanges

#### Example 1 — Neutral professional

**Professor:** “Hi, Peixi. Can you hear me okay?”

**You:** “Yes, loud and clear. Can you hear me alright?”

**Professor:** “Perfect. Nice to finally meet you.”

**You:** “It's great to meet you too, Professor Parpia. Thank you for taking the time to speak with me.”

**Professor:** “Of course. Shall we get started?”

**You:** “Absolutely. I'm ready whenever you are.”

#### Example 2 — Warm and conversational

**Professor:** “Hi, Peixi! Sorry I'm a couple of minutes late. Things have been a little hectic today.”

**You:** “Hi, Dr. Parpia! No worries at all. I completely understand.”

**Professor:** “Thanks! Anyway, it's great to finally meet you.”

**You:** “Likewise! I'm really glad we could arrange this. I've been looking forward to our conversation.”

**Professor:** “Me too. So, where would you like to start?”

**You:** “I'd be happy to give you a quick overview of my background, if that would be helpful.”

#### Example 3 — Unexpected time constraint

**Professor:** “Good morning, Peixi. I'm sorry, but I'm going to have to keep this fairly short today.”

**You:** “Good morning, Professor Parpia. Of course, that's absolutely fine. I appreciate you making time for the meeting.”

**Professor:** “Thanks for understanding. Why don't we get straight into it?”

**You:** “Absolutely. Would it be helpful if I started with a brief overview of my research background?”

**Professor:** “Yes, that would be great.”

**You:** “Sure. So, my background is mainly in clinical and population-based research...”

The third example illustrates a useful principle: **acknowledge the constraint, cooperate, and move the conversation forward**.

### G. Key learning points from Section 1.1

For your notes, the most transferable language concepts are:

- **Adjacency pairs（相邻话轮）:** Respond appropriately to a greeting, apology, question, or invitation.
- **Turn-taking（话轮转换）:** Recognize when to speak, pause, or hand the floor back.
- **Register（语域）:** Adjust the level of formality to the relationship and situation.
- **Conversational reciprocity（对等回应）:** Acknowledge the other person's contribution instead of treating the conversation as a prepared speech.
- **Pragmatic competence（语用能力）:** Choose an expression that suits the social purpose and circumstances.
- **Managing contingencies（应对突发情况）:** Handle delays, time constraints, connection issues, and changes in conversational direction.
