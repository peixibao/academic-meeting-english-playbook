# Lesson 01 · Joining & Greeting

## 1.2 Meeting Types, Interview Formats, and Preparation

**Placement:** After 1.1b *Negotiating How to Begin* and before Lesson 02.  
**Status:** Curriculum-audit supplement for learner review; archived on GitHub at the learner's request.  
**Purpose:** Choose an appropriate response strategy before an academic conversation starts. This section does not replace the greeting and agenda-management language already taught in 1.1–1.1b.

### A. Three meetings that may sound similar but serve different purposes

#### A1. Exploratory meeting with a prospective supervisor

An **exploratory meeting**（探索性导师交流）is a preliminary conversation about interests, potential research directions, and whether further contact makes sense. It may be informal, but can still influence the professor's view of your preparation.

- “I'd be interested in learning more about your current projects.”
- “I have a few research interests I'd like to explore rather than a finalized proposal.”
- “Would it be useful if I briefly introduced my research background?”

A professor asking *“What would you like to talk about?”* is inviting agenda-setting, not necessarily starting a formal examination.

#### A2. Formal admissions interview

A **formal admissions interview**（正式录取面试）may involve an admissions panel（面试委员会）, a prescribed time, academic and technical questions, a written proposal, or a short presentation. Format varies widely by program and institution; never assume a panel or a presentation is required.

- “Could you walk us through one of your research projects?”
- “How has your previous training prepared you for this program?”
- “What would you do if your proposed analysis were not feasible?”

Here, evidence-backed answers and readiness to defend your reasoning often matter more than a conversational opening.

#### A3. Scholarship or funding interview

A **funding interview**（奖学金／经费面试）may assess your capacity to explain a project to an interdisciplinary committee, your research potential, and the feasibility and wider relevance of your proposed work. Some funding interviews are conducted separately from admission; others are not. Check the actual invitation.

- “Why does this research matter beyond your immediate field?”
- “What makes you well placed to carry out the project?”
- “What would you prioritize if resources were limited?”

**Useful distinction:** The goal, audience, and evaluation criteria may differ even when all three meetings take place on Zoom and involve a professor.

### B. Read the invitation carefully

#### B1. Clues in the wording

| Invitation wording | Reasonable working interpretation | What you still need to verify |
|---|---|---|
| “Let's have a chat about your interests.” | Probably exploratory | Duration, participants, agenda |
| “We'd like to invite you to interview.” | Formal or selection-related | Format, evaluation, required materials |
| “You will meet the selection panel.” | Multiple interviewers likely | Panel size, presentation, schedule |
| “Please prepare a five-minute presentation.” | A presentation is explicitly required | Topic, sharing format, questions |
| “We'd like to discuss your proposal.” | Proposal-focused discussion | Which version and any submission instructions |
| “Funding interview” | Resource/award selection | Audience, criteria, technical depth |

**Caution:** *Interview* does not always mean a standardized, highly formal question sequence. *Chat* does not guarantee a meeting has no evaluative component.

#### B2. Ask only for genuinely missing information

> “Thank you for the invitation. Could I check whether you'd like me to prepare anything specific for our meeting?”

> “Would a short presentation be helpful, or would you prefer an informal discussion?”

> “Could you let me know whether there will be any other members of the team joining us?”

> “Is there a particular aspect of my research experience you'd like me to focus on?”

These are practical questions, not attempts to demand disclosure of confidential selection criteria.

#### B3. Should you say “Is this an interview?”

You *can*, but it may sound unnecessarily blunt when the invitation already names the meeting. Prefer asking about the **format and preparation**:

> “Just so I can prepare appropriately, could you tell me a little about the format of the meeting?”

If you truly need a procedural distinction:

> “Could I clarify whether this conversation is part of the formal admissions process or an initial discussion about research interests?”

The latter is precise but best used when the distinction changes a concrete action.

### C. Preparing what to say rather than memorizing a speech

#### C1. A small set of reusable building blocks

Prepare adaptable answers to these functions:

1. **Academic orientation:** degree, current stage, broad research experience.
2. **Contribution:** one concrete research task you performed yourself.
3. **Research thinking:** one question, design choice, finding and limitation you can explain.
4. **Motivation:** why the degree, specific program, institution and supervisor (Lesson 06.5).
5. **Discussion:** one paper-specific observation and an informed question (Lesson 06.3).
6. **Reciprocal inquiry:** what you need to learn from the prospective supervisor (Lesson 07).

This list is a preparation map, not a script to recite in this order.

#### C2. Prepare answer lengths

| Invitation from professor | Suitable initial scale | Example opening |
|---|---|---|
| “Tell me a little about yourself.” | 20–45 seconds, then pause | “I'm in the final stage of my undergraduate degree in...” |
| “Could you summarize your experience?” | Roughly 30–60 seconds | “Most of my research experience has involved...” |
| “Walk us through that project.” | Often 60–90 seconds before technical follow-ups | “The question we were trying to address was...” |
| “Please give a five-minute presentation.” | Prepare to the **specified** time | “I'll briefly cover the question, approach, findings and limitations.” |

These are conversational planning ranges, not university rules or fixed speaking-speed requirements.

#### C3. Different audiences, different depth

**Prospective PI:** may appreciate methods detail relevant to their field.  
**Admissions panel:** may combine technical and general questions.  
**Funding panel:** may include researchers from other fields.

Example of one idea at three levels:

- **Plain:** “We wanted to understand whether a sleep-related health measure tended to vary with a clinical characteristic.”
- **Research-aware:** “We examined an observational association and considered several potential confounders.”
- **Technical:** “We assessed the association with a regression model and evaluated the outcome definition and covariate selection.”

No version should claim causality just because the audience is less technical.

### D. Materials, logistics, and responsible preparation

#### D1. Possible preparation materials

Depending on the invitation, prepare access to:

- Your latest **CV** and the version actually submitted.
- A reliable description of projects, contribution boundaries and manuscript status.
- Your **transcript**, if relevant and requested.
- The program description and relevant entry requirements.
- A recent paper and notes on why it interests you.
- A **research proposal** or slides *if the invitation requires them*.
- Two or three genuine questions you would like answered.
- A notes page with the meeting time, time zone and contact details.

Do not send unpublished data, identifiable participant details or confidential project documents without authorization.

#### D2. Practical language for formats

- “Will I be expected to share my screen?”
- “Would you like me to send the slides in advance?”
- “Is there a preferred format for the proposal?”
- “How much time should I allow for questions?”
- “Would it be helpful for me to bring a copy of my transcript?”

**Expect to** means anticipate or be required by context; **be expected to** emphasizes an expectation placed on you.

#### D3. Confirming dates and time zones

> “Just to confirm, the meeting is scheduled for October 28 at 2 p.m. UK time, correct?”

Use the date and the **actual** named time zone; do not assume that “UK time” or an abbreviation always has the same UTC offset year-round. Ask if an invitation is ambiguous.

### E. Important verbs and distinctions

#### E1. Apply to / apply for / interview for

- “I'm **applying to** the University of X.” — Institution.
- “I'm **applying for** an MPhil place.” — Position or opportunity.
- “I'm **interviewing for** a research studentship.” — Selection opportunity.
- “I'm **meeting with** a potential supervisor.” — Describes a meeting without assuming a selection interview.

#### E2. Admission, acceptance, offer, position, and funding

- **Admission:** Formal entry into a program.
- **Offer:** A formal or informal proposal; clarify which one. *An offer of admission* is more specific.
- **Supervisor interest:** Willingness to talk or potentially supervise; it may not confer admission.
- **Funding:** Financial support, often governed by separate decisions or conditions.
- **Studentship:** A funded student position in some systems; details vary.

Avoid: “The professor invited me to talk, so I got the offer.”

More precise: “The professor has invited me to an initial discussion.”

#### E3. Prepare / prepare for / prepare something

- “I'm **preparing for** the interview.” — Getting ready.
- “I've **prepared** a short overview.” — Created material.
- “Should I **prepare a presentation**?” — Make one.
- “Should I **prepare for questions about** the proposal?” — Anticipate discussion.

#### E4. Audience-aware phrasing

- “Could I give a quick overview before going into the details?”
- “Would it be helpful if I explained the background first?”
- “I can focus on the methodological side if that's more useful.”
- “In less technical terms, the main question is...”

Avoid speaking to an entire panel as though every person already knows your methods.

### F. Anticipating multiple interviewers

#### F1. Addressing the panel

When one person asks a question, usually answer that person while including the others conversationally. Do not force eye contact patterns in an online meeting.

- “Thank you. That's an important question.”
- “I'd separate that into two issues.”
- “I can start with the study design and then explain the main limitation.”

#### F2. When two interviewers ask different questions

Professor A: “Could you explain the design?”  
Professor B: “And why that design rather than a cohort study?”  
Student: “Sure. I'll start with what the study was designed to answer, and then address the alternative design.”

A structure announcement helps everyone follow your response.

#### F3. When you do not know who is in the meeting

> “Thanks for joining. Before we begin, could I quickly check everyone's names and roles?”

In a formal meeting, introductions are often handled by the chair. Ask only when necessary; you can also say “It's nice to meet everyone.”

#### F4. When the chair imposes a time limit

- “I'll keep the overview brief.”
- “I can give you the short version.”
- “We can come back to the technical details if time allows.”

Do not make a promised two-minute answer six minutes long.

### G. Handling preparation uncertainty responsibly

#### G1. The invitation provides no agenda

> “Thank you for arranging the conversation. I'm looking forward to learning more about the group's work. Please let me know if there are any materials you'd like me to review beforehand.”

If no additional instruction arrives, prepare flexible background and research talking points rather than assuming you must give slides.

#### G2. Your available proposal is preliminary

> “I have an initial idea rather than a fully developed proposal. I'd be happy to explain the question I'm considering and the main uncertainties.”

This is appropriate in an exploratory setting; a formal interview with an explicitly required proposal needs different preparation.

#### G3. An unfamiliar specialist joins

> “I can give a brief overview of the clinical context first, then explain the study design.”

#### G4. You are asked something beyond the agenda

> “I hadn't prepared a formal presentation on that topic, but I can explain my current thinking.”

Be willing to answer while keeping factual limits clear.

### H. Eight branching scenarios

#### Scenario 1 — A friendly email turns into a panel discussion

**Chair:** “Two of my colleagues are joining us today.”  
**Student:** “Of course. It's nice to meet everyone. Would it be helpful if I began with a short introduction?”  
**Chair:** “Yes, please.”

**Lesson:** Adjust to new participants; no need to act surprised or defensive.

#### Scenario 2 — You expected an informal chat, but there is a technical question

**Professor:** “How did you address potential confounding?”  
**Student:** “I can explain the adjustment strategy we used. I'd also want to distinguish what I was personally responsible for from earlier design decisions.”

**Lesson:** The label *chat* doesn't exempt you from technical questions.

#### Scenario 3 — No one sent presentation instructions

**Professor:** “Have you prepared slides?”  
**Student:** “I prepared a short overview and notes, but I hadn't understood that slides were required. I'm happy to talk through the material, and I can share slides afterward if that would help.”

**Lesson:** Do not falsely claim you were told to prepare something.

#### Scenario 4 — An interviewer asks about your transcript

**Interviewer:** “I noticed some variation in your grades. Could you tell us about it?”  
**Student:** “Yes. The transcript shows that [TRUE FACT]. The part of my preparation most relevant to this program is [VERIFIABLE EXPERIENCE]. I'm also continuing to develop [SPECIFIC SKILL].”

**Lesson:** Acknowledge the evidence; do not invent explanations.

#### Scenario 5 — A scholarship panelist is unfamiliar with your subject

**Panelist:** “Could you explain that without specialist terminology?”  
**Student:** “Certainly. In plain language, we're asking whether [SIMPLE QUESTION], and why that matters for [REAL-WORLD RELEVANCE].”

**Lesson:** Audience adaptation is a communicative skill, not a reduction in accuracy.

#### Scenario 6 — Meeting length suddenly changes

**Professor:** “I'm afraid I only have fifteen minutes today.”  
**Student:** “No problem. I can keep my introduction short, and perhaps focus on the projects you're currently recruiting for.”

**Lesson:** Suggest priorities without taking control away from the host.

#### Scenario 7 — You're unsure who can make an admissions decision

**Student:** “Could I clarify how the formal admission decision is made for this program?”  
**Professor:** “The graduate committee reviews applications.”  
**Student:** “That's helpful to know. Thank you.”

**Lesson:** Don't equate potential supervisor support with a formal decision.

#### Scenario 8 — You are asked to prepare something for another meeting

**Professor:** “Could you send a two-page proposal before our next conversation?”  
**Student:** “Certainly. Would you prefer a broad research outline or a proposal focused on one specific question?”

**Lesson:** Clarify the scope of an actual request.

### I. Full dialogue: an uncertain invitation becomes a structured discussion

**Student (pre-meeting email):** “Thank you for the invitation. Would you like me to prepare a short presentation, or would you prefer an informal discussion?”  
**Professor:** “No presentation needed. We'll have a conversation about your interests, and a colleague may join us.”

**At the meeting:**

**Professor:** “Good afternoon. This is Dr. Morris, who works with us on the clinical side.”  
**Student:** “It's nice to meet you both. Thank you for taking the time to speak with me.”  
**Professor:** “Could you tell us a little about your experience?”  
**Student:** “Certainly. I've worked on observational research projects involving data analysis and interpretation. I can briefly describe one project if that would be useful.”  
**Dr. Morris:** “Please do.”  
**Student:** “The main question was whether [EXPOSURE] was associated with [OUTCOME]. We used [ACTUAL DESIGN], and I was responsible for [ACTUAL TASK].”  
**Professor:** “What would you identify as the principal limitation?”  
**Student:** “One limitation was [TRUE LIMITATION]. It affects how confidently we can interpret [SPECIFIC FINDING].”  
**Dr. Morris:** “What would be the next step?”  
**Student:** “I'd first want to understand what additional data were available. If follow-up information exists, that might help answer a related longitudinal question.”  
**Professor:** “Good. Do you have questions for us?”  
**Student:** “Yes. Could I ask what the immediate research priorities are for the group, and how students usually get involved?”  
**Professor:** “We're currently developing two projects...”

**Debrief:** The student did not know exactly how technical the meeting would be. Preparation focused on adaptable truthful building blocks, and the student adjusted naturally to a second interviewer.

### J. Functional phrase bank and final preparation checklist

#### J1. Phrases worth retaining

| Function | Phrase |
|---|---|
| Ask about preparation | “Is there anything specific you'd like me to prepare?” |
| Clarify meeting format | “Could you tell me a little about the format?” |
| Ask about slides | “Would a short presentation be helpful?” |
| Ask about attendees | “Will anyone else be joining us?” |
| Confirm expected depth | “Would you prefer a broad overview or a technical explanation?” |
| Organize panel answer | “I'll start with the design and then address the limitation.” |
| Explain without jargon | “In plain language, the question is...” |
| Qualify proposal status | “It's an initial idea rather than a finalized proposal.” |
| Avoid assumption of offer | “Could you explain the formal admissions process?” |
| Respond to time limit | “I can keep my introduction brief.” |

#### J2. Preparation checklist

- [ ] Identify whether the invitation is exploratory, formal admissions-related, funding-related, or unclear.
- [ ] Confirm time, time zone, platform and named attendees where necessary.
- [ ] Check whether slides, a proposal or other documents are explicitly requested.
- [ ] Prepare truthful research summaries at more than one level of detail.
- [ ] Prepare one paper discussion and some genuine questions.
- [ ] Be ready to distinguish a potential supervisor's interest from a formal offer.
- [ ] Rehearse a change of format, time limit or audience.
