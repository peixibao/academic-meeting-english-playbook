# Lesson 03 · Politeness & Academic Etiquette

## 3.1 Understanding Politeness, Register, and Professional Relationships

**Status:** Taught and reviewed in conversation. Incorporates the learner's corrections to E2 and subsequent discussions of professional idioms and accepting compliments. This reference records covered material; it is not an assessed speaking result.

**Learning objectives:** Understand how politeness functions in English-speaking academic and professional settings; choose appropriate formality; manage titles, requests and tentative statements; express academic interests and openness accurately; respond to praise or unfamiliar project suggestions without unwanted commitments.

**Scope:** This is a reusable English-language lesson. Research scenarios are *illustrative*, not statements of the learner's permanent interests or commitments.

### A. The foundations of politeness in professional English

#### A1. Politeness as a pragmatic skill

**Pragmatics（语用学）** studies meaning and language use in context. Compare three ways to request information:

- “Could you explain how the program works?”
- “Would you mind explaining how the program works?”
- “I was wondering if you could explain a little more about how the program works.”

All request information. They differ in directness, formality, and how much room they leave the listener to respond.

Three helpful contextual factors:

| Factor | Meaning | Illustration |
|---|---|---|
| Social distance（社会距离） | How well the speakers know one another | New professor vs. long-standing collaborator |
| Relative authority（相对权威） | Differences in role and responsibility | Applicant vs. potential supervisor |
| Degree of imposition（请求负担） | The effort or inconvenience involved | Brief clarification vs. manuscript review |

A respectful routine question usually needs much less softening than a request requiring hours of someone's time. Appropriate politeness is contextual, not automatically maximal.

#### A2. Positive and negative politeness

These are traditional pragmatics terms; neither means that a speaker is a positive or negative person.

**Positive politeness（积极礼貌策略）** builds rapport and shows appreciation or shared interest:

- “It's great that we have some overlapping interests.”
- “I'd love to hear more about what you're working on.”
- “That's an interesting point.”
- “I'm glad we had a chance to discuss this.”

**Negative politeness（消极礼貌策略）** respects the listener's autonomy and time:

- “Would you have a few minutes to discuss this?”
- “If you have time, I'd appreciate your perspective.”
- “Would it be possible to schedule another conversation?”
- “I understand if your schedule doesn't allow it.”

Both can be combined: “I really enjoyed hearing about your research. If you have time, I'd love to ask a couple more questions.”

### B. Understanding levels of formality

#### B1. Three practical spoken registers

**Register（语域）** concerns language appropriate to a setting or relationship. In North American academic conversations, neutral-professional and friendly-professional registers are often effective.

| Purpose | More formal | Neutral professional | Friendly professional |
|---|---|---|---|
| Interest | “I would be very interested in learning more about your work.” | “I'd be interested in hearing more about your work.” | “I'd love to hear more about what you're working on.” |
| Clarify | “Would you mind elaborating on that point?” | “Could you tell me a little more about that?” | “Oh, how does that work?” |
| Appreciate | “I sincerely appreciate your time and consideration.” | “I really appreciate you taking the time to meet.” | “Thanks so much for making time to chat!” |
| Permission | “Would it be alright if I asked a question?” | “Could I ask you something about the project?” | “Can I ask you something?” |
| Agenda | “Perhaps we could begin by discussing research opportunities.” | “Maybe we could start with your current projects.” | “How about we start with what you're working on?” |
| Preference | “I would be particularly interested in pursuing...” | “I'd be especially interested in working on...” | “I'd really like to work on...” |
| Acknowledgment | “I appreciate your perspective.” | “I see your point.” | “Right, I see what you mean.” |

These are tendencies rather than rigid categories; tone, delivery and context matter.

#### B2. Warmth and formality

Both formal and informal professional speech can be warm and respectful. Ordinary contractions such as *I'd*, *I'm*, *don't* and *that's* are completely normal in spoken academic English.

- Natural: “I'm delighted we were able to arrange this meeting.”
- Unusually ceremonial for an ordinary introductory call: “I am profoundly honored to have been granted the privilege of speaking with you.”

Use the amount of ceremony the occasion warrants.

#### B3. Spoken vs. written academic language

| Written | Spoken equivalent |
|---|---|
| “I would appreciate the opportunity to discuss...” | “I'd love to discuss...” |
| “I am writing to inquire about...” | “I wanted to ask about...” |
| “I would be grateful for your feedback.” | “I'd really appreciate your feedback.” |
| “Please do not hesitate to contact me.” | “Feel free to reach out.” |
| “I look forward to hearing from you.” | “I'd be happy to hear your thoughts.” |

These pairs illustrate shifts of register, not exact substitutions in every email or speech situation.

### C. Academic titles, social distance, and forms of address

#### C1. Common forms of address

| Form | Usage |
|---|---|
| “Professor [Surname]” | Respectful initial academic address |
| “Dr. [Surname]” | Appropriate for someone with a doctorate |
| First name | Common after an invitation or once an established relationship permits |
| “Professor” alone | Possible in direct address, but title plus surname is often safer initially |

One can generally begin with “Professor [Surname]” in an initial meeting, then adjust when invited.

#### C2. When invited to use the first name

Professor: “Please, just call me Alex.”  
Student: “Of course, Alex. Thanks for letting me know.”  
Professor: “So what are your interests?”  
Student: “My interests currently lie in clinical and population-based research.”

Using a first name need not change the substance or professionalism of the discussion.

#### C3. Does an email sign-off establish the address?

A signature such as “Best, Alex” offers a stylistic clue, but does not always establish a person's preferred verbal address. A direct invitation provides clearer guidance. Avoid inferring a fixed preference from one cue.

### D. Indirectness: Softening requests and proposals

**Indirectness（间接表达）** allows someone to make a request while reducing the sense of demand.

#### D1. Direct and indirect requests

- “How do you usually supervise MSc students?” — straightforward, professional information question.
- “Could you tell me a little about how you supervise MSc students?” — polite request frame.
- “Would you mind explaining your supervision approach?” — more deferential.
- “I was wondering if you could tell me how supervision typically works.” — tentative and indirect.

None is automatically superior. A routine information question need not sound apologetic.

#### D2. Could, would, and might

**Could**: Requests and possibilities:
- “Could you tell me a little more about that?”
- “Could we discuss some possible research directions?”
- “Could I ask a quick follow-up question?”

**Would**: Hypothetical situations, willingness and preferences:
- “Would you be open to discussing possible thesis topics?”
- “Would it be possible to schedule another meeting?”
- “I'd be interested in hearing your thoughts.”

**Might**: Possibility or a tentative proposal:
- “That might be worth exploring.”
- “We might consider a different angle.”
- “I thought it might be helpful to start with the study design.”

The modals may signal interpersonal softening, genuine uncertainty, or both.

#### D3. The polite past: “I was wondering...” / “I was hoping...”

- “I was wondering if you could tell me a little more about the program.”
- “I was wondering whether we might discuss potential project directions.”
- “I was hoping we could discuss how thesis topics are typically developed.”

The past form can create distance from an immediate demand; this is sometimes described as **modal remoteness（情态距离）**.

**If vs. whether:** In an indirect yes/no question, either may work: “I was wondering if/whether you have any openings.” *Whether* is often slightly more formal and more explicit about alternatives. Some structures require *whether*: “I don't know whether to apply”; “It depends on whether the project is funded.” *If* is the usual conditional conjunction: “If I have time, I'll read it.”

**Different intentions:**

| Expression | Function |
|---|---|
| “I'd like to discuss potential projects.” | Clear current preference |
| “I was hoping we could discuss potential projects.” | Polite proposal |
| “I'd be interested in discussing potential projects.” | Interest without a definite commitment |
| “I was wondering whether we could discuss potential projects.” | Indirect request |

### E. Hedging: Expressing ideas without overstating certainty

**Hedging（审慎措辞／模糊限制表达）** moderates the certainty or force of a statement. It can help communicate the limits of evidence, the tentativeness of a proposal, or the current state of one's interests.

This section distinguishes **epistemic hedging** (certainty about a proposition) from **interpersonal mitigation** (softening social force). Expressions like “I'm keeping my options open” and “I'd need to learn more” are relevant communicative strategies, though they are not necessarily hedges in the narrow linguistic sense.

#### E1. Common expressions and functions

| Expression | Typical function |
|---|---|
| “I think...” | Personal judgment, potentially tentative |
| “It seems that...” | Inference |
| “It looks like...” | Impression based on information |
| “It might be...” | Possibility |
| “It could be...” | Possible explanation or option |
| “I'm inclined to think...” | Considered but nonabsolute opinion |
| “As far as I understand...” | Knowledge limitation |
| “From my perspective...” | Point of view |
| “To some extent...” | Partial applicability |

The same phrase may do different things depending on context.

#### E2. Hedging when expressing an academic interest — **corrected and reviewed**

Distinguish FOUR different topics; do not substitute one for another:

1. **Current research interests**: What attracts the student now.
2. **Future research fields**: Other disciplines or areas the student may still wish to explore.
3. **Specific research questions**: The concrete questions within or across fields that the student may investigate.
4. **Research methods vs. methodological research**: Applying epidemiological/statistical methods to substantive clinical questions is different from studying or developing methods as the primary subject. Do not infer an interest in *methodological research* merely from interest in using these methods.

**Example 1 — Clear present interest without permanence**

“My research interests **currently lie** at the intersection of clinical medicine and public health, particularly clinical and population-based epidemiology.”

*Currently* means the position is true at the present stage, leaving room for development. It is a temporal qualification, not a confession of uncertainty about everything.

**Example 2 — Exploring different RESEARCH FIELDS, not just questions within one field**

“I'm still exploring **different research fields**, and I'd like to keep an open mind about where my interests might lead me.”

This expresses broad intellectual openness. “I'm still exploring the specific questions I'd like to pursue” has a narrower meaning and should **not** be used as a replacement when the speaker means *different fields*.

**Example 3 — Tentative interest in a proposal outside current experience**

“That's somewhat outside my current research experience, but I'd be open to learning more about it. Could you tell me what the project would involve?”

This signals willingness to hear more without claiming that the offered project is already an established interest.

**Example 4 — A genuine boundary, alongside exploration**

“I'm open to exploring different research areas. That said, I'm currently more drawn to clinical or population-based projects where I can **apply** epidemiological and statistical methods.”

*Apply methods* describes tools used to answer research questions; do not silently rewrite this as wanting to do methodological research.

**Example 5 — Reserve a decision**

“I'd be interested in hearing more before deciding whether it might be a good fit.”

This expresses curiosity and noncommitment. Whether it is appropriate depends on whether the student sincerely wants to explore the project.

| Expression | What it communicates |
|---|---|
| “I'm interested in X.” | Existing interest |
| “I'm still exploring different fields.” | Continuing exploration across areas |
| “I'd be open to exploring X.” | Willingness to consider X |
| “I'd be interested in learning more about X.” | Interest in obtaining information about X |
| “I'd need to learn more before deciding.” | No decision or commitment yet |
| “I'm leaning toward X.” | Current preference, not final |
| “I wouldn't rule X out.” | X remains a possibility |

**Do not automatically treat “I'd be open to exploring related methodological projects” as a generic academic-interest example.** It explicitly suggests willingness to consider research **about methodology**, which may misrepresent a speaker's goals.

##### Practical dialogue: professor proposes an unfamiliar project

Professor: “Would you be interested in a laboratory-based project?”  
Student: “I appreciate you bringing that up. My interests currently lie mainly in clinical and population-based research, but I'm still exploring different fields and keeping my options open. Could you tell me more about the project's main research questions?”  
Professor: “It would primarily involve laboratory assays.”  
Student: “Thanks for explaining. I'd prefer a project centered on clinical or population-based research, though I'm open to hearing about other directions that connect with those interests.”

The student's preference is a hypothetical example, not an obligation. Speakers can be open to *fields* while maintaining boundaries around a particular type of work.

##### Idioms and conversational expressions: nuance and professional suitability

These are mainly **idioms**, not necessarily slang.

| Expression | Meaning | Context |
|---|---|---|
| “I'm keeping my options open.” | Keeping multiple possibilities available | Natural professional speech |
| “I'm on the fence about X.” | Undecided about a particular choice | Conversational; specify X |
| “I'm leaning toward X.” | A provisional preference for X | Natural professional speech |
| “I wouldn't rule it out.” | Not excluding an option | Natural professional speech |
| “I'm not set on one field yet.” | No final commitment to one field | Friendly-professional |
| “It's a little outside my wheelhouse.” | Beyond one's experience or expertise | Casual professional; can sound informal |
| “I'm drawn to X.” | X feels intellectually appealing | Natural professional speech |

**Correction:** The idiom is **on the fence**, not *in the fence*. “I'm on the fence” usually means indecision about a **specific** option; “I'm keeping my options open” is better for wider exploration. “I'm open to welcoming any possibility” is awkward and overbroad; try “I'm open to considering different research directions.” “Keen on” is correct English but relatively associated with British usage; “particularly interested in” or “drawn to” often sounds more neutral in American professional speech.

#### E3. Hedging when interpreting research findings

Compare:

- “The exposure causes the disease.”
- “The exposure is associated with the disease.”
- “Our findings suggest a possible association between the exposure and the disease.”

These convey different scientific claims. Observational associations generally cannot establish causality without supporting assumptions and an appropriate design. **Scientific qualification** must reflect the evidence; it should not be added or removed merely to sound polite.

#### E4. Avoid excessive hedging

Hedging is appropriate where uncertainty is genuine. Do not downplay verified experience for politeness.

Appropriate if true: “I conducted the regression analyses for the project.”

Needlessly self-minimizing: “I suppose I might have been somewhat involved in the statistical analysis.”

Separate **what you know**, **what you prefer**, **what you're willing to consider**, and **what you have yet to decide**.

### F. Showing respect without excessive deference

**Deference（礼让／表示敬重）** recognizes another person's role or expertise while preserving one's own voice.

#### F1. Respecting expertise

- “I'd be interested in hearing your perspective on that.”
- “Given your experience in this area, I'd really value your thoughts.”
- “That's an interesting approach. I'd like to understand the reasoning behind it.”
- “I appreciate you explaining that.”
- “Thank you for sharing your perspective.”

#### F2. Respecting time

- “I appreciate you making time for this.”
- “I know you have a busy schedule, so I'll keep this brief.”
- “If we're running short on time, I'm happy to focus on the main questions.”
- “I'm happy to follow up by email if that's more convenient.”

#### F3. Expressing a preference clearly

- “I'd prefer to focus on research-oriented training.”
- “I'm especially interested in projects that connect clinical questions with population-level evidence.”
- “Ideally, I'd like to gain more experience in study design.”
- “I'm open to considering other directions as well.”

Preferences should reflect the actual speaker, not an invented permanent academic identity.

### G. Unexpected differences in formality: seven scenarios

#### Scenario 1: The professor is informal

Professor: “Hey! Good to meet you. Just call me Alex.”  
Student: “Of course, Alex. Great to meet you too.”  
Professor: “So what got you interested in epidemiology?”  
Student: “My training has covered different areas of medicine and public health, and that breadth got me interested in connections between them.”

#### Scenario 2: The professor is formal

Professor: “Good morning. Thank you for joining the meeting.”  
Student: “Good morning, Professor [Surname]. Thank you for taking the time to speak with me.”  
Professor: “Let's begin with your academic background.”  
Student: “Certainly. I'd be happy to give you a brief overview.”

#### Scenario 3: The professor offers help

Professor: “I could send you a couple of papers if that would be helpful.”  
Student: “That would be really helpful, thank you. I'd appreciate the chance to read them.”  
Professor: “Sure, I'll send them after the meeting.”  
Student: “Wonderful, thanks!”

#### Scenario 4: The professor is uncertain about opportunities

Professor: “I'm not sure we'll have a suitable project at the moment.”  
Student: “I understand. I appreciate you being upfront about that.”  
Professor: “Something may come up later.”  
Student: “I'd be interested in hearing more if an opportunity arises.”

**Be upfront** = be honest and direct about something important, even when potentially disappointing.

#### Scenario 5: The professor suggests a different project direction

Professor: “Would you be interested in a laboratory-based project?”  
Student: “Thanks for suggesting that. At the moment, I'm leaning toward clinical and population-based research, but I'm still exploring different fields. Could you tell me a little more about the research question and what the day-to-day work would involve?”  
Professor: “It would involve experiments at the bench.”  
Student: “Thanks for clarifying. That's outside the kind of work I'm currently hoping to pursue. I'd still be interested in hearing about other projects where epidemiological methods could be useful.”

This student expresses openness to exploring *research fields* while stating a *work-type preference*. The distinction prevents false promises.

#### Scenario 6: The professor compliments your work

Professor: “You've gained quite a lot of research experience as an undergraduate.”

Possible student responses:

- “Thank you! I've learned a great deal from the projects I've worked on.”
- “Thanks! I really appreciate that.”
- “That's very kind of you to say.”
- “Oh, thank you. I'm flattered!”
- “That means a lot coming from you.”

**“I'm flattered”** is grammatically natural when a compliment feels particularly significant or honoring. A routine remark (“Your slides were clear”) usually invites a simpler “Thank you—I'm glad they were clear.” *Flattered* can also occur in polite refusals (“I'm flattered, but I can't accept”), so context matters. It can be used professionally without a romantic implication.

**“That means a lot coming from you”** expresses that the opinion of this particular person carries weight. Best when sincere and not overused.

Accept praise graciously; do not reflexively deny a real achievement.

#### Scenario 7: The professor gives advice

Professor: “You might benefit from more statistical-methods coursework.”  
Student: “That makes sense. It's an area I'd be interested in developing further.”  
Professor: “You could look into regression and trial-methodology courses.”  
Student: “That's helpful—I'll look into those options.”

This acknowledges the advice without guaranteeing any particular future course or path.

### H. Extended model dialogue: Adjusting politeness through a meeting

Professor: “Hi! It's nice to finally meet you.”  
Student: “Great to meet you too, Professor [Surname]. Thanks for making time to speak with me.”  
Professor: “Please, just call me Alex.”  
Student: “Sure, Alex. Thanks for letting me know.”  
Professor: “What were you hoping to get out of our conversation?”  
Student: “I was hoping we could explore where our research interests might overlap and whether a suitable MSc research direction might emerge.”  
Professor: “What research are you interested in?”  
Student: “At the moment, I'm drawn to research connecting clinical questions with population-level evidence. I'm also keeping an open mind about other fields—I don't want to narrow my focus before I've had a chance to learn more.”  
Professor: “Most of our work is in clinical trial methodology. Would that interest you?”  
Student: “It's outside most of my previous research experience, but I'd be interested in hearing about the types of questions your group is working on.”  
Professor: “We compare methods for analysing randomized trials.”  
Student: “I see. What aspects of the work would an MSc student typically contribute to?”  
Professor: “I can send you a few papers to give you a better idea.”  
Student: “That would be really helpful. Thank you!”

**Functions used:** professional greeting; adaptation to preferred address; polite proposal; accurate present interest; willingness to explore across fields; no premature commitment; clarification; appreciation.

### I. Essential distinctions

| Concept | Meaning | Useful language |
|---|---|---|
| Register | Appropriate formality | Neutral / friendly professional |
| Social distance | Degree of familiarity | Titles / first names |
| Positive politeness | Connection and rapport | “I'd love to hear more.” |
| Negative politeness | Respect for autonomy/time | “If you have time...” |
| Indirectness | Softer social force | “I was wondering if...” |
| Modal verbs | Requests, willingness, possibility | Could / would / might |
| Epistemic hedging | Uncertainty about a proposition | “It might be...” |
| Interpersonal mitigation | Softening a request or opinion | “I was hoping...” |
| Exploratory interest | Willingness to consider other fields | “I'm keeping my options open.” |
| Noncommitment | No decision yet | “I'd need to learn more.” |
| Deference | Respect for position and expertise | “I'd value your perspective.” |
| Receiving a compliment | Thanking or acknowledging praise | “I appreciate that.” / “I'm flattered.” |

### J. Core principles

Professional communication balances clear information, appropriate respect, intellectual independence, and curiosity. In particular:

1. Choose register based on the situation and the other speaker's preferences.
2. Use indirectness where it serves the interaction; routine questions may be straightforward.
3. Hedge evidence-based conclusions only as warranted by the evidence.
4. Separate **current interests**, **future exploration across fields**, **specific research questions**, and **research methods**.
5. Use phrases such as “on the fence,” “keeping my options open,” and “I'm flattered” according to their actual pragmatic meanings.
6. Be open without promising to accept a proposed project; be clear about genuine preferences.
7. Do not minimize accurate accomplishments merely to sound polite.
