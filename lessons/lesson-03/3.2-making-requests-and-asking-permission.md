# Lesson 03 · Politeness & Academic Etiquette

## 3.2 Making Requests and Asking Permission

**Learning objectives:** Learn how to ask for information, permission, time, help, and professional favors. Understand the differences between similar grammatical structures, choose language appropriate to the size of a request, and respond naturally when someone accepts, declines, or hesitates.

In Section 3.1, we examined the principles behind professional politeness. Now we'll apply them to one of the most common functions in academic English: **getting something done through conversation**.

Consider four different statements:

- “Could I ask you a question?”
- “Could you answer a question for me?”
- “Would you be able to review my draft?”
- “Would you mind if I shared my screen?”

All four are polite, but they involve different relationships between the speaker, the listener, and the requested action.

Understanding those distinctions is the foundation of this section.

### A. Four types of requests

A useful first distinction is identifying **what you're asking the other person to do or allow**.

| Type | Communicative purpose | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Information request** | Ask someone to provide information | “Could you tell me how the project is organized?” |
| **Permission request** | Ask whether you're allowed to do something | “Would it be alright if I shared my screen?” |
| **Action request** | Ask someone to perform an action | “Could you send me the paper?” |
| **Favor / commitment request** | Ask for meaningful time, effort, or responsibility | “Would you be willing to provide feedback on my proposal?” |

The wording should reflect the burden of the request.

For example, asking a professor to explain a term during a conversation usually requires less elaborate politeness than asking them to read and comment on a lengthy manuscript.

#### A1. Information requests

You can use direct questions naturally:

- “How does your research group usually organize projects?”
- “What would an MSc student's responsibilities involve?”
- “How are thesis topics normally developed?”
- “What kind of methodological training does the program provide?”

You can also add a polite request frame:

- “Could you tell me how your research group usually organizes projects?”
- “I'd be interested in hearing how thesis topics are developed.”
- “I was wondering if you could explain how the program is structured.”

All are appropriate.

A straightforward question is often the most efficient choice when someone has already invited you to ask questions.

#### A2. Permission requests

These concern **your own actions**.

- “Could I ask a quick follow-up question?”
- “Would it be okay if I took a few notes?”
- “Do you mind if I share my screen?”
- “Would you mind if I briefly walked you through the figure?”

You are asking whether the listener is comfortable with something **you** intend to do.

#### A3. Action requests

These concern **the other person's actions**.

- “Could you share the paper with me?”
- “Would you be able to send me the project description?”
- “Could you recommend a few introductory readings?”
- “Would it be possible for you to clarify the next steps?”

The requested action may require the other person's time or effort.

#### A4. Favors and larger commitments

These often benefit from acknowledging the listener's schedule or ability to decline.

> “If you have time, I'd really appreciate your feedback on the proposal.”

> “Would you be open to discussing whether there might be an opportunity to collaborate?”

> “I understand you may have other commitments, but I was wondering whether you'd be willing to provide some feedback on my research plan.”

Be careful with the scale of the request. A request for extensive supervision or substantial manuscript feedback is a meaningful commitment.

The phrasing can be polite, but the listener still needs a genuine opportunity to decline.

### B. Can I, Could I, May I, and Would it be okay if...

These are common ways of asking permission. Their differences are largely pragmatic.

#### B1. Comparing permission structures

| Expression | Typical effect | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Can I...?** | Direct, natural, everyday | “Can I ask you something?” |
| **Could I...?** | Slightly softer, professional | “Could I ask you a follow-up question?” |
| **May I...?** | More formal or ceremonious | “May I ask a question?” |
| **Would it be okay if...?** | Explicitly checks the listener's comfort | “Would it be okay if I shared my screen?” |
| **Would it be alright if...?** | Similar to *okay*, slightly more polished in some contexts | “Would it be alright if I took notes?” |
| **Do you mind if...?** | Checks whether the action would bother the listener | “Do you mind if I take notes?” |

**Can I** is perfectly normal in spoken American professional English. There is no need to replace every *can* with *may*.

**Could I** is a particularly versatile choice when speaking to a professor you have just met.

#### B2. “Could I...?” vs. “Could you...?”

This distinction is easy to overlook.

**“Could I share my screen?”**

You're asking for permission to share your own screen.

**“Could you share your screen?”**

You're asking the professor to share theirs.

Likewise:

- “Could I ask another question?” — Your action.
- “Could you ask another question?” — The listener's action.
- “Could I explain my analysis?” — Your action.
- “Could you explain your analysis?” — The listener's action.

The subject pronoun changes the entire request.

#### B3. “May I...?” and American academic speech

“May I ask a question?” is correct and polite.

It can sound more formal than “Could I ask a question?”, depending on the situation and delivery.

At a formal seminar, “May I ask a question about your methodology?” may fit very naturally.

In a relaxed one-on-one meeting, “Could I ask something about the project?” may sound more conversational.

Neither construction is inherently disrespectful or unprofessional.

### C. Would you mind...? — Grammar, meaning, and responses

This construction deserves special attention because its grammar and responses can be confusing.

#### C1. Would you mind + -ing?

Use this structure when asking **the other person to perform an action**.

**Structure:**

`Would you mind + verb-ing ...?`

Examples:

- “Would you mind explaining that part again?”
- “Would you mind sharing the link?”
- “Would you mind sending me a copy of the paper?”
- “Would you mind going over that figure once more?”

**Incorrect:** “Would you mind to explain that?”

**Correct:** “Would you mind explaining that?”

The verb after *mind* takes the **-ing** form.

#### C2. Would you mind if I...?

This structure asks permission for **your own action**.

> “Would you mind if I shared my screen?”

> “Would you mind if I asked another question?”

> “Would you mind if I took a few notes?”

Notice the common use of the past tense: *shared, asked, took*.

This is a hypothetical or distancing use of the past tense. It refers to something you're considering doing now or in the near future.

Compare:

- “Do you mind if I **share** my screen?”
- “Would you mind if I **shared** my screen?”

Both are natural.

The second is generally more tentative.

In contemporary English, “Would you mind if I share...” also occurs, but “Would you mind if I shared...” is a well-established and useful pattern.

#### C3. Why the answers can be confusing

Consider:

**You:** “Would you mind if I took a few notes?”

**Professor:** “No, not at all. Go ahead.”

This means **permission granted**.

Why?

Because the literal question is whether the professor would *mind* — whether taking notes would bother them.

“No, not at all” means it would not bother them.

| Response | Likely meaning |
|---|---|
| “No, not at all.” | Permission granted |
| “Not at all. Go ahead.” | Permission granted |
| “Of course not.” | Permission granted |
| “Sure, go ahead.” | Permission granted, despite not literally answering the negative question |
| “Actually, I'd prefer you didn't.” | Permission declined |
| “I'd rather keep this off the record.” | Permission declined in context |

A bare **“Yes”** can be ambiguous, since speakers sometimes answer the literal question and sometimes the implied request.

In actual conversation, additional words such as *go ahead*, *that's fine*, or *I'd rather you didn't* clarify the intended meaning.

#### C4. Practical dialogue

**Student:** “Would you mind if I took a few notes during our discussion?”

**Professor:** “No, of course not.”

**Student:** “Great, thank you.”

Or:

**Student:** “Would you mind sending me that paper afterward?”

**Professor:** “Not at all. I'll email it to you.”

**Student:** “I really appreciate it.”

These are different grammatical structures, even though both begin with *Would you mind*.

### D. Could you, Would you, and Would you be able to...

Now let's focus on asking another person to do something.

#### D1. “Could you...?”

This is one of the most broadly useful professional request structures.

- “Could you explain how recruitment works?”
- “Could you recommend some relevant papers?”
- “Could you tell me what a typical MSc project involves?”

It is direct enough to be clear and polite enough for ordinary academic situations.

#### D2. “Would you...?”

This often focuses more explicitly on willingness.

Compare:

**“Could you review this draft?”**

A conventional polite request, framed around ability or possibility.

**“Would you review this draft?”**

A request framed around willingness, though it may sound more direct without additional context.

**“Would you be willing to review this draft?”**

Explicitly asks whether the person is willing to undertake the task.

These constructions overlap in everyday use, so avoid treating their meanings as completely separate.

#### D3. “Would you be able to...?”

This often works well when availability, workload, or practical feasibility matters.

> “Would you be able to meet sometime next week?”

> “Would you be able to share the relevant reading materials?”

> “Would you be able to provide feedback on the proposal before the submission deadline?”

The construction leaves room for the answer to depend on circumstances.

In North American academic settings, it is a very useful way to request meaningful assistance.

#### D4. “Would it be possible to...?”

This is another indirect option.

> “Would it be possible to schedule a follow-up conversation?”

> “Would it be possible to discuss the project's expected timeline?”

> “Would it be possible for me to speak with one of your current graduate students?”

Notice the difference between:

**“Would it be possible to arrange a meeting?”**

This leaves the person responsible for arranging it somewhat unspecified.

**“Would it be possible for you to arrange a meeting?”**

This explicitly asks the listener to take responsibility for arranging it.

The second request therefore imposes a more specific action.

### E. Asking for time, attention, and availability

Professional requests frequently involve someone's time.

#### E1. Common expressions

| Expression | Typical situation |
|---|---|
| “Do you have a minute?” | Brief, relatively informal interruption |
| “Do you have a moment?” | Similar, slightly more neutral |
| “Could I take a minute of your time?” | Brief question with polite acknowledgment |
| “Do you have a few minutes to discuss something?” | Short discussion |
| “Would you have time to look at this?” | Request involving some effort |
| “Would you be available sometime next week?” | Scheduling |
| “When would be a good time to discuss this?” | Letting the listener suggest a time |

The literal phrase *a minute* is often idiomatic. It doesn't guarantee that the conversation will last exactly sixty seconds.

Still, avoid asking for “just a minute” if you already know you need half an hour.

#### E2. “When you get a chance”

This is a particularly useful conversational expression.

> “Could you take a look at this when you get a chance?”

Meaning: Whenever you have a suitable opportunity.

It signals that the task doesn't need immediate attention.

**Important:** If there is an actual deadline, communicate it clearly.

Compare:

- “Could you look at this when you get a chance?” — No specified deadline.
- “Would you be able to review this by Friday?” — Specific deadline.
- “If possible, could you review this by Friday? I understand if that's too short a turnaround.” — Deadline plus acknowledgment of the burden.

#### E3. “At your convenience”

This is a more formal expression, especially common in written communication.

> “Please let me know a time that would be convenient for you.”

Natural and professional.

> “Please respond at your earliest convenience.”

This is common in business writing but can sound somewhat formal, and sometimes carries an implicit expectation of promptness.

For ordinary spoken academic conversation, “Whenever works best for you” or “Whenever you have time” may sound warmer.

#### E4. Asking to reschedule

**You:** “Would it be possible to move our meeting to later in the week?”

**Professor:** “Possibly. Would Thursday work?”

**You:** “Thursday would work well for me. Thank you for being flexible.”

A rescheduling request is clearer when you state the proposed change and provide practical alternatives.

### F. Making requests without unnecessary apologies

Many learners use *sorry* every time they ask a question.

In professional English, apologies are useful when you've genuinely interrupted, inconvenienced, or caused a problem. They aren't required for every normal conversational request.

Compare:

> “I'm so sorry to bother you, but could I possibly ask a question, if that's okay?”

For a routine question in a scheduled meeting, this may be unnecessarily elaborate.

A simpler alternative:

> “Could I ask a quick question about the study design?”

If the professor is already explaining a project, you can often ask directly:

> “How did you decide on that approach?”

#### F1. “Sorry to bother you”

This is appropriate when there is a meaningful interruption.

For example:

**Professor:** “I'm about to head into another meeting.”

**Student:** “Sorry to bother you. Would now be a bad time to ask a quick question?”

However, a scheduled meeting is already an agreed opportunity to talk.

You don't need to apologize for participating.

#### F2. “I just wanted to ask...”

This is common in spoken American English.

> “I just wanted to ask about the project timeline.”

Here, *just* functions as a **minimizer（弱化语）**. It presents the action as relatively small or modest.

It can make a request sound casual and approachable.

But repeated use may weaken the clarity of your speech.

For example:

“I just wanted to ask if maybe you could possibly...”

You can often simply say:

“Could you tell me...?”

#### F3. “Would you happen to know...?”

This is a polite, slightly tentative way to ask whether someone knows something.

> “Would you happen to know when applications open?”

> “Do you happen to know who coordinates the program?”

It often works well when the listener may not have the information.

The phrase avoids presuming that they definitely know the answer.

### G. Important grammar distinctions

#### G1. Ask, ask for, and request

**Ask someone something**

> “Could I ask you a question?”

**Ask someone for something**

> “Could I ask you for some advice?”

**Ask someone to do something**

> “Could I ask you to review this section?”

**Request something**

> “I'd like to request an extension.”

*Request* can sound more formal or administrative than *ask*.

“I'm requesting clarification” is grammatical, but “Could I ask for some clarification?” is often more natural in a spoken meeting.

#### G2. Embedded question word order

This is an important grammar point.

**Direct question:**

> “What is the application deadline?”

**Indirect question:**

> “Could you tell me what the application deadline **is**?”

After *Could you tell me*, the embedded question generally uses statement word order.

More examples:

| Direct question | Embedded question |
|---|---|
| “How does the project work?” | “Could you explain how the project works?” |
| “What are the requirements?” | “Do you know what the requirements are?” |
| “When does recruitment begin?” | “I was wondering when recruitment begins.” |
| “Who supervises MSc students?” | “Could you tell me who supervises MSc students?” |

In the last pair, *who* is already the subject, so there is no visible inversion to undo.

#### G3. Would you mind doing vs. Would you mind if I did

| Structure | Requested action |
|---|---|
| “Would you mind **sending** the paper?” | The listener sends it |
| “Would you mind **if I sent** the paper?” | You send it |
| “Would you mind **sharing** your screen?” | The listener shares |
| “Would you mind **if I shared** my screen?” | You share |

This distinction is particularly useful in video meetings.

#### G4. Ask permission vs. make a polite suggestion

**Permission:**

“Could I briefly explain my previous work?”

**Suggestion:**

“Perhaps we could start with a brief overview of my previous work.”

**Preference:**

“I'd be happy to start by discussing my previous research experience.”

A permission request asks whether *you may do something*. A suggestion proposes a shared course of action. A preference expresses what you would be comfortable doing. These may all appear at the start of a meeting, but they do different conversational work.

### H. Realistic conversations: Requests, permission, and responses

#### H1. Asking to share your screen

**Student:** “Would it be okay if I shared my screen? I have a figure that might help explain the project.”

**Professor:** “Of course. Go ahead.”

**Student:** “Thanks. Can you see it clearly?”

**Professor:** “Yes, I can.”

**Student:** “Great. This shows the general structure of the analysis.”

**Why it works:** The student asks permission before taking an action, confirms the technical result, and briefly explains why the screen share is useful.

#### H2. Requesting a paper or reading recommendation

**Student:** “You mentioned a review on this topic earlier. Would you happen to remember the title?”

**Professor:** “I don't remember the exact title, but I can email it to you.”

**Student:** “That would be really helpful. Thank you.”

**Professor:** “No problem.”

**Why it works:** The question allows for uncertainty; the student accepts a voluntary offer without treating it as an obligation.

#### H3. Requesting feedback that requires effort

**Student:** “Would you be willing to give me some brief feedback on my project outline, if your schedule allows?”

**Professor:** “Potentially. How long is it?”

**Student:** “About two pages. I'm mainly hoping to get your perspective on whether the research question is focused enough.”

**Professor:** “All right. Send it over, and I'll see what I can do.”

**Student:** “I appreciate that. Thank you.”

**Why it works:** The student specifies the scope and lets the professor judge whether the request is manageable. “I'll see what I can do” is a tentative response, not a guarantee of detailed feedback.

#### H4. The professor cannot help at the moment

**Student:** “Would you have time to discuss the analysis this week?”

**Professor:** “I'm afraid this week is completely packed.”

**Student:** “No problem at all. Would next week be more convenient, or would you prefer I send my questions by email?”

**Professor:** “Email would probably be easier.”

**Student:** “Sounds good. I'll send a concise summary.”

**Why it works:** The response accepts the constraint, offers concrete alternatives, and preserves the other person's choice.

#### H5. Requesting clarification without over-apologizing

**Professor:** “We used a different definition for the primary outcome in the sensitivity analysis.”

**Student:** “Could I clarify one point? Did you change the outcome threshold, or did you also change how the outcome was measured?”

**Professor:** “Only the threshold.”

**Student:** “Got it. Thanks for clarifying.”

**Why it works:** The student identifies precisely what needs clarification instead of using an extended apology or a vague request to repeat everything.

#### H6. Asking permission when the answer is no

**Student:** “Would you mind if I recorded the meeting so I can revisit your comments afterward?”

**Professor:** “I'd prefer not to record it.”

**Student:** “Of course, I understand. I'll take notes instead.”

**Professor:** “That's fine.”

**Why it works:** Recording requires clear permission. Once the professor declines, the student accepts the boundary and offers a simple alternative.

### I. Functional phrase bank

| Function | Useful expression | Register / nuance |
|---|---|---|
| Ask an information question | “Could you tell me how that works?” | Neutral professional |
| Ask to speak | “Could I ask a quick follow-up question?” | Neutral professional |
| Ask permission | “Would it be okay if I shared my screen?” | Professional |
| Ask permission more formally | “Would it be alright if I took a few notes?” | Professional |
| Ask someone to act | “Could you send me the paper?” | Direct, polite |
| Ask about ability or availability | “Would you be able to review the outline?” | Acknowledges practical constraints |
| Ask about willingness | “Would you be willing to provide feedback?” | Meaningful favor |
| Offer the other person choice | “Would email be easier, or would you prefer a meeting?” | Collaborative |
| Make a low-urgency request | “When you get a chance, could you take a look?” | No explicit deadline |
| State a deadline politely | “If possible, could you review it by Friday?” | Specific timeline |
| Ask if someone knows | “Would you happen to know who coordinates the program?” | Tentative knowledge question |
| Ask about timing | “When would be a good time to discuss this?” | Flexible scheduling |
| Accept permission | “Great, thank you. I'll share my screen now.” | Clear next action |
| Accept a limitation | “Of course, I understand.” | Respectful acknowledgment |
| Respond to an offer | “That would be really helpful. I appreciate it.” | Appreciation |
| Make a suggestion | “Perhaps we could start with the project overview.” | Shared agenda |
| Express a preference | “I'd be happy to begin with my research background.” | Shows willingness |

### J. Core principles

1. Identify what you actually need: **information, permission, action, or a favor**.
2. Distinguish **your own action** (“Could I...?”) from **the listener's action** (“Could you...?”).
3. Use *Would you mind + -ing?* when asking the listener to act, and *Would you mind if I + past tense...?* when asking permission for your own action.
4. Remember that “No, not at all” usually grants permission in response to “Would you mind...?” Listen for the full response rather than relying on *yes* or *no* alone.
5. Use indirect questions with **statement word order**: “Could you tell me how the project works?”
6. Match the politeness to the **size, timing, and burden of the request**. Routine questions need not be excessively softened.
7. Make meaningful favors specific and leave room for the other person to decline.
8. In a meeting, respond to acceptance, hesitation, and refusal naturally. The conversation continues even when the answer is no.
