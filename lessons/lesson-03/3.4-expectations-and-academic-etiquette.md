# Lesson 03 · Politeness & Academic Etiquette

## 3.4 Communicating Expectations and Maintaining Academic Etiquette

**Learning objectives:** Express expectations and preferences; ask about professional norms; clarify responsibilities; check assumptions; communicate boundaries without premature commitment; and manage academic meetings and collaborations respectfully.

### A. Understanding expectations in academic communication

People enter meetings with different assumptions. An applicant may want to explore research opportunities, while a professor may want to evaluate research fit. Both may wish to learn more without having decided on collaboration.

Good communication expresses **what you hope for** while leaving room for **what the other person expects**.

#### A1. Five ways of expressing expectations

| Expression | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **I hope to...** | Desired outcome | “I hope to gain more research experience during my master's.” |
| **I'm hoping to...** | Current aspiration | “I'm hoping to explore a few different research directions.” |
| **I'd like to...** | Preference or intention | “I'd like to develop a stronger foundation in epidemiological research.” |
| **I expect to...** | Anticipated outcome | “I expect to complete my degree next year.” |
| **I'm planning to...** | Intended course of action | “I'm planning to apply for research-based master's programs.” |

#### A2. Hope vs. expect

“I **hope** to work on an epidemiology project” = desire, with uncertain outcome.

“I **expect** to work on an epidemiology project” = some basis for anticipating it.

In an exploratory meeting, “I'm hoping there might be an opportunity to contribute to one of your projects” expresses interest appropriately.

“I expect to join one of your projects” may sound presumptive without an offer or agreement.

#### A3. Expect vs. look forward to

“I expect to hear from you next week” communicates anticipated contact and sometimes sounds demanding.

“I look forward to hearing from you” expresses positive anticipation.

**Look forward to + noun or verb-ing:**

- “I look forward to **meeting** you.”
- “I look forward to **hearing** your thoughts.”
- “I'm looking forward to our conversation.”

Incorrect: “I look forward to meet you.” The *to* here is a preposition followed by a gerund, not an infinitive marker.

### B. Asking about expectations without assuming anything

In a potential supervision meeting, learn about normal practices, not just the professor's promises.

#### B1. Useful questions

- “What would you typically expect from an MSc student?”
- “What would a student's responsibilities usually involve?”
- “How much independence do students generally have?”
- “What does the supervision process usually look like?”
- “How do students typically get involved in ongoing projects?”
- “What would successful progress look like in the first few months?”

#### B2. What do you expect? vs. What would you expect?

- “What do you expect from your students?” — Direct general question about current expectations.
- “What **would** you expect from an MSc student working on this project?” — Hypothetical expectations for a possible position.

*Would* is especially useful when no position has been agreed upon.

#### B3. Would be expected to... — Passive structure

**Pattern:** subject + be expected to + base verb.

- “Students are expected to attend weekly meetings.”
- “Researchers are expected to document their analyses.”
- “An MSc student might be expected to contribute to data collection.”
- “Would I be expected to develop my own research question?”

Compare:

“Would **you expect me** to write the manuscript?” — Asks what the professor personally expects.

“Would **I be expected** to contribute to manuscript writing?” — Focuses on the normal duties of the role.

#### B4. Typically, usually, generally

- “How are projects **typically** assigned?” — Characteristic practice.
- “How often do students **usually** meet their supervisors?” — What happens most of the time.
- “What responsibilities do students **generally** have?” — Broad pattern allowing exceptions.

Their meanings overlap; they help avoid assuming that every project follows the same practice.

### C. Expressing your own expectations and preferences

#### C1. Four levels of preference

| Expression | Communicative force |
|---|---|
| “I'm interested in...” | General interest |
| “I'd prefer...” | Preference among options |
| “Ideally, I'd like...” | Desired arrangement |
| “It's important to me that...” | Stronger priority |

Examples:

- “I'm interested in research that connects clinical questions with population-level evidence.”
- “I'd prefer a project that involves epidemiological research.”
- “Ideally, I'd like to gain experience with different parts of the research process.”
- “It's important to me that I have opportunities to develop independent research skills.”

These are illustrations; use preferences that are actually true for you.

#### C2. Ideally

**Ideally** = under the best or most desirable circumstances:

- “Ideally, I'd like to participate in both study design and data analysis.”
- “Ideally, I'd like a project with a clear clinical or public health application.”
- “Ideally, I'd like opportunities to collaborate across disciplines.”

The word allows an aspiration without representing it as guaranteed.

#### C3. I'm looking for... vs. I'd be interested in...

“I'm **looking for** a research environment where I can develop independence” communicates a relatively clear objective.

“I'd **be interested in** a research environment that encourages independent work” expresses positive interest in that characteristic.

#### C4. Openness across research fields

- “I'm still exploring different research fields, so I'm keeping an open mind about possible directions.”
- “My interests currently lie in clinical and population-based research, but I'm open to learning about other areas.”
- “I'd be interested in understanding the different projects available before narrowing my focus.”

Distinguish **current field interests**, **possible future fields**, **specific research questions**, and **using methods vs. researching methods**; see Section 3.1 E2. Openness to hearing about a project is not a commitment to accept it.

### D. Clarifying responsibilities and working arrangements

#### D1. Questions about roles

- “What would my role involve?”
- “What would the main responsibilities be?”
- “How is the work typically divided among team members?”
- “Would the project involve independent work or close collaboration?”
- “Who would normally be responsible for data management?”
- “Would there be opportunities to contribute to the analysis?”

#### D2. Be responsible for vs. be involved in

**Responsible for** conveys accountability or ownership:

> “I was responsible for preparing the statistical analysis.”

**Involved in** conveys participation, not necessarily primary responsibility:

> “I was involved in preparing the statistical analysis.”

Other important contrasts:

- “I **coordinated** data collection.”
- “I **contributed to** data collection.”
- “I **assisted with** data collection.”

Choose the phrase that accurately reflects what happened.

#### D3. Take the lead on

**Take the lead on** = assume primary responsibility for organizing or directing a task.

- “She took the lead on drafting the manuscript.”
- “I took the lead on the statistical analysis.”
- “Would the student be expected to take the lead on a specific part of the project?”

Natural in American professional English.

#### D4. Be in charge of

**Be in charge of** generally conveys authority or responsibility for a task.

- “Who's in charge of coordinating the interviews?”
- “She's in charge of data management.”

It may imply more control than *be involved in*, so be accurate.

### E. Checking assumptions and preventing misunderstandings

#### E1. My understanding is that...

> “My understanding is that the project would primarily involve secondary data analysis. Is that correct?”

Other options:

- “As I understand it...”
- “From what you've described...”
- “If I've understood correctly...”
- “Just to make sure we're on the same page...”

This makes your interpretation visible and invites correction.

#### E2. We're on the same page

**Be on the same page** = share an understanding of a situation, plan, or expectation.

- “I want to make sure we're on the same page about the timeline.”
- “Could we make sure we're on the same page about responsibilities?”
- “Great, it sounds like we're on the same page.”

Common in American academic and workplace conversation.

#### E3. I was under the impression that...

> “I was under the impression that the project would involve existing data.”

This describes an earlier belief, and can imply that earlier information led you to that conclusion.

Compare:

- “I was under the impression that the deadline was Friday.” — Previous understanding, possibly caused by prior communication.
- “I thought the deadline was Friday.” — More neutral statement of previous belief.
- “Just to clarify, is the deadline Thursday or Friday?” — Direct clarification without emphasizing why you were confused.

#### E4. As far as I know vs. as far as I understand

**As far as I know** limits a factual claim to your knowledge:

> “As far as I know, the application deadline hasn't changed.”

**As far as I understand** limits a claim to your interpretation:

> “As far as I understand, MSc students usually work on a defined research project.”

Both limit certainty appropriately.

### F. Professional boundaries and avoiding premature commitments

A potential opportunity may sound interesting without being suitable or settled.

#### F1. Provisional interest

- “That sounds like an interesting possibility.”
- “I'd be interested in hearing more.”
- “That could be worth exploring.”
- “I'd be open to considering it.”
- “I'd like to understand the project better before deciding.”

#### F2. Interest vs. commitment

Compare:

- “I'd be interested in **exploring** that possibility.” — Further consideration.
- “I'd be happy to **take on** that project.” — Sounds closer to agreement.
- “I can **take responsibility for** the analysis.” — Relatively definite commitment.

The differences matter when discussing tasks, roles, funding, or project fit.

#### F3. I wouldn't want to commit to...

> “I'd be interested in learning more, but I wouldn't want to commit to a specific direction before understanding the project.”

Or:

> “I'd need more information before I could make that decision.”

Both are reasonable when a choice is genuinely undecided.

#### F4. I don't want to overpromise

**Overpromise** = promise more than you can realistically deliver.

- “I don't want to overpromise, so I'd need to check my availability first.”
- “I'd be happy to help, but I don't want to promise a deadline I can't meet.”
- “I want to make sure I can give the project the time it requires.”

#### F5. State experience limits accurately

Professor: “Have you used this particular analytical approach?”

Possible replies:

- “I haven't used that method directly, but I'm familiar with the broader research context.”
- “That's an area where I'd need additional training.”
- “My experience so far has mainly involved observational research, so I'd be interested in understanding what additional skills this project would require.”

Being accurate about limitations allows a constructive conversation about training without unwarranted self-deprecation.

### G. Maintaining academic etiquette in everyday interactions

**Academic etiquette（学术交流礼仪）** refers to interactional conventions that support respectful collaboration. Conventions vary by university, culture and research group; these are general good practices, not promises about any institution's policies.

#### G1. Time and scheduling

- “I know we're coming up on time.”
- “I want to be mindful of your schedule.”
- “We can leave that for another conversation if we're short on time.”
- “I'm happy to follow up by email.”

**Be mindful of** = be aware of and considerate toward.

> “I want to be mindful of your time.”

#### G2. Preparing without dominating

Bring a concise background overview, one or two relevant research examples, some questions, and notes. Adapt to the other person's agenda.

- “I have a few questions prepared, but I'm happy to follow your agenda.”
- “There are a couple of things I'd love to learn more about, if we have time.”

#### G3. Taking notes

- “Would you mind if I took a few notes?”
- “I'd like to jot down a couple of points, if that's alright.”
- “Let me just jot that down.”

**Jot something down** = write it quickly, usually a short note.

Recording a meeting is different from taking notes; it may require explicit consent and compliance with policies.

#### G4. Unpublished research and confidentiality

- “Is that work publicly available yet?”
- “Has the paper been published, or is it still under review?”
- “Would you be comfortable with me sharing this information?”
- “Is this something you'd prefer to keep within the group for now?”

Access to unpublished results does not automatically grant permission to disclose them.

#### G5. Accurate academic credit

- “I conducted the statistical analyses, while other team members were responsible for data collection.”
- “I contributed to the analysis and interpretation of results.”
- “My primary responsibility was preparing the analysis.”

Distinguish your tasks from those of collaborators.

#### G6. Institutional practices vs. individual decisions

- “Could you tell me how the process usually works in your department?”
- “Would that be determined by the department or the supervisor?”
- “Is there an official resource you would recommend checking?”

Policies vary, and an individual professor may not control admissions or funding decisions.

### H. Unexpected situations and professional responses

#### Scenario 1 — What do you hope to gain?

Professor: “What are you hoping to get out of a master's program?”  
Student: “I'd like more substantial research experience and greater independence. I'm also hoping to explore different areas before settling on a more specific long-term direction.”  
Professor: “So you're still considering different possibilities?”  
Student: “Exactly. There are areas I'm currently drawn to, but I'd like to keep an open mind.”

#### Scenario 2 — High independence expectations

Professor: “Our students are generally expected to work quite independently.”  
Student: “Could you tell me what that looks like in practice?”  
Professor: “They manage their daily progress and bring questions to our meetings.”  
Student: “So they take responsibility for their progress, with guidance during regular discussions?”  
Professor: “Exactly.”

#### Scenario 3 — Professor assumes skills you don't have

Professor: “Since you've worked with observational data, I assume you've also done lots of longitudinal analysis?”  
Student: “Most of my experience has actually been with cross-sectional data. I've studied related concepts, but I haven't had much direct longitudinal-analysis experience yet.”  
Professor: “I see.”  
Student: “It's something I'd be interested in learning more about.”

#### Scenario 4 — A different research field

Professor: “Would you be willing to take on a project involving another area?”  
Student: “I'd be open to learning more. I'm still exploring different fields, and I'd be interested in understanding the main research questions and the day-to-day work.”  
Professor: “Let me explain what we're planning.”

#### Scenario 5 — Unclear preparation for next meeting

Professor: “Maybe we could meet again to discuss a project idea.”  
Student: “That sounds great. Is there anything in particular you'd like me to prepare beforehand?”  
Professor: “Perhaps read a few recent papers.”  
Student: “Absolutely. Would you have any particular papers in mind?”

#### Scenario 6 — Pressure to decide immediately

Professor: “Does this sound like something you'd pursue?”  
Student: “It certainly sounds worth exploring. I'd like to read more about the question and methods before deciding whether it's the right direction for me.”  
Professor: “That's reasonable.”

#### Scenario 7 — Misunderstood responsibility

Professor: “I'd expect the student to take the lead on the literature review.”  
Student: “Ah, I initially thought it would be shared across the team. Thanks for clarifying.”  
Professor: “They would still get feedback from others.”  
Student: “So the student would coordinate the review, with input from team members?”  
Professor: “Exactly.”

#### Scenario 8 — Meeting time limit

Student: “I know we're coming up on time, so I don't want to keep you longer than planned.”  
Professor: “That's alright. I have a few more minutes.”  
Student: “Thank you. Could I ask one last question about the project?”

#### Scenario 9 — Long-term career plan

Professor: “Do you see yourself pursuing a PhD eventually?”  
Student: “It's something I'm considering, although I haven't made a final decision. I'd like more research experience and a clearer sense of the direction I want to pursue.”  
Professor: “That makes sense.”

### I. Extended prospective-supervisor meeting dialogue

Professor: “What are you hoping to gain from graduate study?”  
Student: “I'm hoping to develop greater independence as a researcher and gain experience across the research process. I'm also interested in exploring different fields before narrowing my long-term direction.”  
Professor: “What projects have you worked on?”  
Student: “Most of my experience has been in observational epidemiology, particularly clinical or population-based questions.”  
Professor: “Our group mainly works in clinical trial methodology. Would that interest you?”  
Student: “I'd be interested in learning more. It's somewhat outside my previous experience, but I'm keeping an open mind.”  
Professor: “Our MSc students usually work on a defined project within ongoing studies.”  
Student: “What would a student's responsibilities typically involve?”  
Professor: “Students may contribute to analysis planning, data analysis, and manuscript preparation.”  
Student: “Would the student be expected to develop an independent question, or would that be determined with the supervisor?”  
Professor: “Usually we develop it together.”  
Student: “That sounds valuable. I'd like the opportunity to contribute to shaping a question.”  
Professor: “We also expect students to work independently.”  
Student: “What does that look like in practice? How often do students meet with a supervisor?”  
Professor: “We usually meet weekly; students manage their work between meetings.”  
Student: “Got it. So students manage their day-to-day progress, with regular discussion and feedback.”  
Professor: “Exactly. Do you think the environment might suit you?”  
Student: “It sounds like an interesting possibility. I'd like to learn more about specific projects before making a decision, but I appreciate hearing how the group works.”  
Professor: “I can send you a few papers.”  
Student: “That would be really helpful. Thank you.”

#### I1. Language functions in the dialogue

| Expression | Function |
|---|---|
| “I'm hoping to...” | Aspiration |
| “Keeping an open mind” | Openness |
| “I'd be interested in learning more” | Tentative interest |
| “What would responsibilities involve?” | Hypothetical role clarification |
| “Would the student be expected to...?” | Norms without presumed offer |
| “What does that look like in practice?” | Ask for concrete detail |
| “So students manage...” | Check interpretation |
| “I'd like to learn more before deciding.” | Avoid early commitment |
| “I appreciate hearing...” | Recognize useful explanation |

#### J1. Seven expressions to remember

1. **“What would that look like in practice?”** — Ask what a general statement means concretely.
2. **“I just want to make sure we're on the same page.”** — Confirm shared expectations.
3. **“I'm keeping an open mind.”** — Indicate openness to possibilities.
4. **“I'd need to learn more before making a decision.”** — Remain noncommittal.
5. **“I don't want to overpromise.”** — Avoid promising more than you can deliver.
6. **“I want to be mindful of your time.”** — Respect someone's schedule.
7. **“I was under the impression that...”** — State a previous understanding, while aware it may suggest earlier communication led you to it.

Also: **“Let me jot that down.”** = Let me write a quick note.

#### J2. Core principles

1. Distinguish hope, plans, and reasonable expectations.
2. Ask about standard norms without presuming you have an offer.
3. Express preferences and priorities clearly.
4. Clarify the difference between participation and primary responsibility.
5. Check assumptions before they develop into misunderstandings.
6. Openness, tentative interest, and acceptance are distinct.
7. Respect time, confidentiality, institutional processes, and accurate attribution.
8. State experience and limitations truthfully.
9. Academic etiquette varies with the setting.
10. Treat expectations as something to explore collaboratively.
