# Lesson 08 · Wrapping Up & Follow-up

## 8.1 Summarizing Decisions and Closing the Meeting

**Learning objectives:** Recognize when a meeting is ending; summarize what was actually discussed; distinguish proposals, possibilities, requests and confirmed actions; confirm who will do what and by when; thank people specifically; and leave a video meeting naturally, including awkward or ambiguous endings.

**Where this fits:** Lesson 04 taught turn-taking and transitions; Lesson 07 taught how to explore expectations and negotiate. This section turns a live discussion into an accurate, mutually understood next step. A friendly conversation is not automatically an offer, an agreement or a promise of funding.

### A. The three jobs at the end of a meeting

A professional close often contains three different speech acts（言语行为）:

1. **Recap:** What was discussed or learned?
2. **Confirm:** Is there an agreed next action, owner or timeframe?
3. **Close:** Express appreciation and end the interaction.

These can be very brief:

> “That was really helpful. Just to confirm, I'll send you my CV and a short summary of my research experience. Thanks again for taking the time to meet with me.”

If no action was agreed, do **not** invent one. A perfectly good close is:

> “Thank you. I really appreciated learning more about the group and the program.”

#### A1. A discussion is not always a decision

| What the professor said | What you may accurately say | What you should avoid assuming |
|---|---|---|
| “You might look at this paper.” | “I'll take a look at that paper.” | “You assigned me a research project.” |
| “We sometimes take MPhil students.” | “It was helpful to learn that the group sometimes works with MPhil students.” | “You've offered me an MPhil place.” |
| “Feel free to send your CV.” | “I'll send my CV.” | “My application has been approved.” |
| “Let's keep in touch.” | “I'd be happy to stay in touch.” | “We have an ongoing commitment.” |
| “I can introduce you to a colleague.” | “Thank you for offering to make an introduction.” | “The introduction has already happened.” |

**Principle:** Report the **strength of the commitment** that was actually made.

### B. How to recognize that the conversation is wrapping up

Common closing signals:

- “I think that covers most of what I wanted to discuss.”
- “We're coming up on time.”
- “We're almost at the end of our time.”
- “I have to jump to another meeting.”
- “Is there anything else you wanted to ask?”
- “What would be the best next step?”
- “Well, it was great talking with you.”
- “I should probably let you go.”

**We're coming up on time** = the meeting is approaching its scheduled end. **We're out of time** = the available time is over.

#### B1. The professor asks, “Anything else?”

If you have an important question:

> “I have one last question, if there's time. What would you recommend as the next step for someone interested in applying?”

If you have no further questions:

> “I think you've answered my main questions. Thank you—that was very helpful.”

If the professor clearly has a hard stop:

> “I know you need to go, so I won't keep you. I can send my remaining question by email.”

Don't turn a closing invitation into a ten-minute interview.

#### B2. Closing cues are social, not just grammatical

“Great speaking with you” often announces the end. Respond with a closing move rather than restarting the research discussion:

> “Likewise. Thank you again for your time.”

### C. Summarizing accurately without sounding like a meeting secretary

#### C1. Four useful recap frames

| Expression | Function | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **“Just to recap...”** | Summarize key points | “Just to recap, the project is still in its planning stage.” |
| **“So, if I've understood correctly...”** | Invite correction | “So, if I've understood correctly, students can develop a question within an existing dataset.” |
| **“My understanding is that...”** | State tentative interpretation | “My understanding is that eligibility is assessed by the graduate office.” |
| **“The main takeaway for me is...”** | State personal learning | “The main takeaway for me is that the project involves more data work than data collection.” |

Use **the main takeaway** for what *you learned*. Use **we agreed** only when an agreement was explicitly made.

#### C2. Summary language for three different states

**Information received:**

> “It was helpful to understand how students typically work with the research team.”

**Tentative possibility:**

> “It sounds as though there may be a project worth exploring further.”

**Confirmed action:**

> “We agreed that I'd send a brief summary of my research background by Friday.”

Those states are not interchangeable.

#### C3. “So” is a conversational tool

In speech, beginning with **So, ...** can naturally frame a synthesis:

> “So, the next step would be for me to contact the program administrator—is that right?”

It does not automatically sound informal or careless. Tone depends on the whole sentence.

### D. Confirming the next step: action, owner and timing

A reliable structure is:

**Action → person responsible → timeframe, if known → confirmation.**

> “Just to confirm, I should send you the CV and the one-page project summary, and then check the formal application requirements with the department. Is that right?”

#### D1. Clarify who does what

- “Would you prefer that I send the document to you directly?”
- “Should I contact the graduate office, or would you recommend someone else?”
- “Would you like me to follow up after I've submitted the application?”
- “Is there anything else you'd like me to prepare?”
- “Will you be able to share the paper, or should I look for it through the university library?”

Avoid assigning an action to the professor that they have not accepted.

#### D2. Clarify timing without demanding a deadline

- “Is there a timeframe you'd recommend?”
- “Would it be helpful to send that over this week?”
- “Is there a particular date I should keep in mind?”
- “Would it make sense for me to get back in touch after the application deadline?”

**By Friday** = no later than Friday. **On Friday** = on that day. **Around Friday** = approximately then. Use only the degree of precision actually established.

#### D3. “Should I...?” vs. “Would you like me to...?”

- **Should I send a summary?** asks whether that would be the right next step.
- **Would you like me to send a summary?** offers a specific action based on their preference.
- **I'll send a summary.** commits to doing it. Say this only when you can and intend to follow through.

#### D4. Confirming without overconfirming

One focused check is enough for a simple meeting:

> “Great. I'll send that over tomorrow. Thanks again!”

For a complicated discussion, a brief recap plus a follow-up email is more reliable than repeating the same point several times on the call.

### E. Gratitude, appreciation and parting language

#### E1. Making thanks specific

- “Thank you for taking the time to speak with me.”
- “I really appreciate you explaining how the project is structured.”
- “It was helpful to hear more about the group's approach to supervision.”
- “I enjoyed hearing about the research you're working on.”
- “Thank you for being so open about the project's current limitations.”

Use the past tense when referring to the meeting that just occurred: **“It was great talking with you.”**

#### E2. “It was nice meeting you” vs. “It was nice talking with you”

- **Nice meeting you:** Especially appropriate when it is your first encounter.
- **Nice talking with you:** Works both for first and subsequent meetings.
- **Nice to see you again:** Appropriate for someone you have already met.

A natural exchange:

> Professor: “It was nice meeting you.”  
> You: “Likewise. Thank you for your time.”

**Likewise** means the feeling is mutual; it can sound concise and professional.

#### E3. “Have a good one” and “Take care”

- “Have a great rest of your day.” — warm, broadly professional.
- “Enjoy the rest of your week.” — appropriate if timing fits.
- “Take care.” — friendly closing, often fine when the meeting has become warm.
- “Have a good one.” — casual; best when the other person is already informal.

Don't turn a closing into a memorized parade of idioms. “Thanks again. Have a great day!” is enough.

### F. The final five seconds of a video meeting

Video calls sometimes end with both people waiting for the other to disconnect.

#### F1. A normal ending

> Professor: “All right. Well, it was nice speaking with you.”  
> Student: “Likewise. Thank you again. Have a great day!”  
> Professor: “You too. Bye!”  
> Student: “Bye!”

Then leave the call. You do not need to wait for the professor to click “Leave” first.

#### F2. Cross-time-zone closings

If it is morning for you and evening for them:

> “Thanks again, and enjoy the rest of your evening.”

If unsure of their local time:

> “Thank you again. Have a good rest of your day.”

No need to guess the clock time.

#### F3. The professor keeps talking after the goodbye

> Professor: “Oh, one other thing—please check the eligibility requirements.”  
> Student: “Absolutely. I'll check those before applying. Thanks for mentioning that.”

Wait until the final thought is complete, then close again naturally.

### G. When closing becomes difficult

#### G1. No clear next step emerged

> “Thank you. This gave me a much clearer picture of the group's work. Would it be appropriate to get in touch again if I have a specific question after reviewing the program details?”

If the answer is vague, accept it as vague.

#### G2. You're interested, but need to investigate further

> “I'd be interested in exploring the possibility further. I'd also like to review the program requirements before deciding on my next steps.”

This expresses interest without promising to apply or accept a place.

#### G3. The professor offers to help, but it's unclear how

> “That's very kind of you. Would it be useful for me to send a brief email with the question and relevant details?”

A concrete request is often easier to act on than “Please help me.”

#### G4. You are invited to commit immediately

> “Thank you for discussing that possibility. I'd appreciate a little time to review the details before giving you a definite answer.”

Lesson 07.4 covers the negotiation; Lesson 08 focuses on accurately leaving the matter open.

#### G5. You don't want to ask more questions

> “I think you've covered everything I wanted to ask today. Thank you—that was really helpful.”

It is entirely legitimate to finish without inventing another question.

### H. Unexpected situations: branching endings

#### Scenario 1 — Time is almost up

Professor: “I'm afraid I have to go in two minutes.”  
Student: “Of course. Before we wrap up, could I quickly confirm whether you recommended contacting the admissions office first?”  
Professor: “Yes, that's the best place to start.”  
Student: “Great. I'll do that. Thanks again.”

**Language function:** Prioritize one essential clarification and exit.

#### Scenario 2 — Professor requests a CV

Professor: “Feel free to send me your CV.”  
Student: “I'd be happy to. Would you like a brief research summary as well?”  
Professor: “Just the CV is fine.”  
Student: “Perfect. I'll send that over. Thanks for meeting with me.”

**Lesson:** Don't over-attach documents when the requested scope is narrow.

#### Scenario 3 — Professor offers a possible introduction

Professor: “I may be able to put you in touch with a colleague.”  
Student: “That would be very helpful. Would it be useful for me to send a brief note about my interests?”  
Professor: “Yes, please.”  
Student: “Of course. I'll email it to you.”

**Lesson:** *May be able* is conditional. An introduction has not yet occurred.

#### Scenario 4 — No openings

Professor: “I'm not taking students this cycle.”  
Student: “Thank you for letting me know. I appreciate your time and the chance to learn more about your research.”

**Optional, if appropriate:** “Would you happen to know of other groups working on related questions?” Do not demand referrals.

#### Scenario 5 — Ambiguous interest

Professor: “This sounds interesting. Let's stay in touch.”  
Student: “I'd be happy to. Would it make sense to contact you again once I've reviewed the program requirements?”  
Professor: “Sure.”  
Student: “Great. Thank you again.”

**Lesson:** Interest does not by itself establish availability or admission.

#### Scenario 6 — The professor asks for a deliverable you need to check

Professor: “Could you send me a project summary by tomorrow?”  
Student: “I can send a short overview tomorrow, though I'd need a little longer for a detailed summary. Would the shorter version be useful?”  
Professor: “Yes, a short overview is fine.”

**Lesson:** Make an achievable commitment and confirm revised scope.

#### Scenario 7 — Several possible next steps

Professor: “You could contact the office, read the paper, and perhaps send a short idea.”  
Student: “That gives me several useful directions. To make sure I prioritize correctly, would you recommend that I check formal eligibility first?”  
Professor: “Yes, definitely.”

**Lesson:** Identify priority; don't interpret a list of ideas as compulsory assignments.

#### Scenario 8 — Unexpected final question

Professor: “One last thing—where do you see yourself after the degree?”  
Student: “I'm considering further research training, potentially including doctoral study. I'd like to gain more experience before narrowing down my longer-term direction.”  
Professor: “That sounds reasonable.”  
Student: “Thank you. It was great talking with you.”

**Lesson:** A last-minute substantive question is still a question; answer it, then return to closing.

### I. Extended conversation: from research discussion to a clean close

*Illustrative first meeting with a potential supervisor; no admission or funding decision is implied.*

**Professor:** “I think we've covered the main projects we have at the moment. Is there anything else you'd like to ask?”  
**Student:** “You've answered most of my questions. I do have one practical question, if there's time.”  
**Professor:** “Sure, go ahead.”  
**Student:** “If I decide to apply, would you recommend contacting the program office first about eligibility, or submitting the application and then reaching out?”  
**Professor:** “I'd check with the program office first. Their requirements change from time to time.”  
**Student:** “That makes sense. I'll check the current requirements rather than assume they haven't changed.”  
**Professor:** “Yes. And feel free to send your CV if you'd like me to have a look at your background.”  
**Student:** “Thank you, I'd appreciate that. Just to confirm, I'll contact the program office about eligibility and send you my CV. Is there anything particular you'd like included?”  
**Professor:** “No, a regular academic CV is fine.”  
**Student:** “Great. This was really helpful. I appreciated hearing about the research questions your group is working on.”  
**Professor:** “Likewise. Best of luck with the application process.”  
**Student:** “Thank you again. Have a great rest of your day!”  
**Professor:** “You too. Bye!”  
**Student:** “Bye!”

#### I1. What the dialogue accomplished

| Utterance | Function |
|---|---|
| “One practical question, if there's time” | Respects remaining time |
| “Would you recommend contacting...?” | Requests a prioritized process suggestion |
| “I'll check the current requirements” | Agrees to verify rather than speculate |
| “Feel free to send your CV” | Permission or invitation, not an offer |
| “Just to confirm...” | Checks exact actions |
| “Is there anything particular...?” | Checks document scope |
| “I appreciated hearing about...” | Specific thanks |
| “Best of luck...” | Warm parting, not an admission signal |

#### I2. If the professor changes the ending

**Alternative A — No time for your final question:**

Professor: “I'm sorry, I have to leave now.”  
Student: “Of course, I understand. Would it be okay if I sent that last question by email?”  
Professor: “Yes, that's fine.”

**Alternative B — No openings:**

Professor: “I'm afraid I can't take on another student.”  
Student: “Thank you for being clear. I appreciate the opportunity to learn about the group.”

**Alternative C — A concrete conditional suggestion:**

Professor: “If you're eligible, send me the confirmation and we can discuss possible projects.”  
Student: “Thank you. I'll confirm eligibility first and follow up with that information.”

### J. Functional phrase bank and closing checklist

#### J1. Essential expressions

| Function | Expression | Nuance |
|---|---|---|
| Signal closing | “Before we wrap up...” | Transition to final item |
| Check final questions | “Is there anything else I should know at this stage?” | Ask without demanding exhaustive information |
| Brief recap | “Just to recap...” | Summarize points |
| Verify interpretation | “So, if I've understood correctly...” | Invite correction |
| Confirm action | “Just to confirm, I'll...” | State your own action |
| Confirm another action | “Would you like me to...?” | Offer, not assume |
| Confirm timeframe | “Is there a timeframe you'd recommend?” | Request guidance |
| Thank | “Thank you for taking the time to meet with me.” | Professional |
| Express value | “I really appreciated learning about...” | Specific appreciation |
| Accept ending | “I won't keep you any longer.” | Suitable when they must leave |
| Natural goodbye | “It was great talking with you.” | Broadly useful |
| Natural goodbye | “Have a great rest of your day.” | Warm |
| Vague invitation | “Would it be appropriate to follow up after...?” | Seek a concrete next action |

#### J2. Important grammar and pragmatics

- **Wrap up** = bring an activity to an end; “wrap up the meeting” / “before we wrap up.”
- **Follow up on + topic:** “I'll follow up on the program requirements.”
- **Follow up with + person:** “I'll follow up with the coordinator.”
- **Follow up by + action:** “I'll follow up by email.”
- **As discussed** refers to matters actually discussed, not imagined agreements.
- **Agreed to + action:** “We agreed to exchange documents.” Use only when mutual agreement occurred.
- **Should I...?** asks for guidance. **I'll...** makes a commitment.

#### J3. Exit checklist

1. Did you accurately distinguish information, possibility and agreement?
2. Does each actual next step have a clear owner?
3. Is any deadline real and confirmed?
4. Did you avoid implying admission, supervision or funding was offered?
5. Is your final thanks specific and proportionate?
6. Can you end without a long scripted monologue?
