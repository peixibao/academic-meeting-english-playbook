# Lesson 08 · Wrapping Up & Follow-up

## 8.3 Handling Outcomes and Making Informed Next Moves

**Learning objectives:** Interpret outcomes of prospective-supervisor conversations with appropriate caution; respond to interest, uncertainty, unavailable positions, referrals, rejection, and silence; separate supervisor interest from an official offer; plan practical next actions; and maintain professional communication without chasing vague signals or making premature commitments.

**Where this fits:** Lesson 06 explored scientific/program fit; Lesson 07 explored working conditions, funding and boundaries; Sections 8.1–8.2 handled meeting closure and email follow-up. This final section considers what happens **after** the conversation—when the result is encouraging, unclear, negative, conditional or delayed.

**Important:** University admission, scholarships, supervisor allocation and recruitment depend on the actual program. These are illustrative conversations, not promises or policy descriptions for any particular institution.

### A. Understand the different possible outcomes

A meeting does not end with only “yes” or “no.” It can produce several kinds of information:

| Outcome | What you know | What you do **not** yet know |
|---|---|---|
| **Positive discussion** | Professor engaged with your work | Whether a place exists |
| **Invitation to send materials** | Professor is willing to review information | Whether your application will succeed |
| **Potential project fit** | A possible research direction exists | Whether it can be funded or supervised |
| **Conditional next step** | Something must be checked or completed | Whether the condition will lead to an offer |
| **Referral** | Another contact may be relevant | Whether that person has an opening |
| **No capacity** | This supervisor cannot take students now | Whether other routes are possible |
| **Formal rejection** | That decision has been made for the specified application | Whether unrelated future opportunities exist |
| **No response** | You have not received a reply | Why there was no reply |

#### A1. Think in terms of evidence, not emotional tone

A professor can be warm but have no available place. A professor can be brief but still offer useful procedural guidance. Look for **explicit, verifiable next steps** rather than the strength of friendly language.

### B. Interpreting encouragement and noncommittal phrases

#### B1. “Your background is interesting.”

This is positive feedback. It does **not** automatically mean:

- “You have been selected.”
- “I will supervise you.”
- “Funding is available.”
- “The graduate school has approved your application.”

Appropriate response:

> “Thank you. I'm glad that my experience seems relevant. I'd be interested in learning more about the next steps, if there might be an opportunity.”

#### B2. “Feel free to apply.”

The professor may be encouraging an application, perhaps without controlling the decision.

> “Thank you. I'll review the current requirements and application process.”

If essential to clarify:

> “Would it be helpful to mention our conversation in the application, or is there a particular process applicants should follow?”

Don't assume permission to apply means sponsorship, selection or nomination.

#### B3. “Let's stay in touch.”

This can mean a genuine invitation to future contact, a friendly conclusion, or an open-ended possibility. Ask a concrete but light question if there is a reason:

> “I'd be happy to. Would it make sense for me to follow up after I've checked the program requirements?”

If the response remains vague, leave it there and pursue your other options.

#### B4. “We should talk again sometime.”

“Sometime” is indefinite; it gives no date.

> “I'd be happy to arrange another conversation if that would be useful. Would you prefer I get back in touch once I have [SPECIFIC INFORMATION]?”

#### B5. “There may be an opportunity.”

**May** expresses possibility. Avoid upgrading it to certainty when summarizing or telling others.

> “That's encouraging. Would there be anything I should check before considering that possibility further?”

### C. When the professor genuinely appears interested

#### C1. Move from encouragement to a clear process

Professor: “I think your background could be relevant to one of our projects.”  
Student: “I'd be interested in learning more. Would the next step be a formal application, or is there some additional information you'd like me to send first?”

You are asking about **process**, not pressing for an informal admissions decision.

#### C2. Conditional language matters

- “If your application meets the requirements, we **could** discuss projects.”
- “We **might** have an opening next year.”
- “I'd be **happy to consider** your CV.”
- “Subject to funding, we **may** recruit a student.”

None equals “You're admitted” or “The scholarship is confirmed.”

#### C3. Written confirmation and scope

For a material opportunity, the relevant formal decision and conditions should be confirmed through the actual institutional process. Even a written supportive message from an individual professor might not constitute an official offer.

Useful phrasing:

> “Thank you for explaining. I'll check the formal program process so I understand which steps are still required.”

#### C4. If you are interested but haven't decided

> “I'm interested in exploring this further. I'd also like to understand the project expectations and formal conditions before making any commitment.”

This connects directly to Lesson 07. Do not say “I accept” unless you mean it and have the necessary information.

### D. When there is no vacancy or a negative outcome

#### D1. A professor has no capacity

Professor: “I'm afraid I'm not taking new students this year.”  
Student: “Thank you for letting me know. I appreciate you being clear about that.”

If appropriate:

> “Would you happen to know of colleagues working in a related area who may be accepting students?”

**Would you happen to know...** is an indirect, low-pressure request. A professor is not obliged to provide referrals.

#### D2. The project or academic interests don't align

Professor: “I don't think my current projects match your interests very closely.”  
Student: “Thank you for your honest assessment. That's helpful to know. I'll take a closer look at other groups working in related areas.”

If you genuinely want to explore the difference:

> “Could I ask which aspect of the project you see as the main mismatch?”

Do not automatically argue or overstate your interest in unfamiliar work to keep the door open.

#### D3. Formal application rejection

> “Thank you for informing me of the decision. I appreciate the time the committee took to consider my application.”

A feedback request, when appropriate and allowed:

> “If feedback is available to applicants, I'd be grateful for any general advice about areas I could strengthen.”

Avoid demanding an explanation from an individual faculty member who does not control the decision.

#### D4. “Not this year” vs. “not at all”

- “We're **not recruiting this year**.” — Time-limited statement.
- “We **don't supervise master's students**.” — Possible structural/program limitation.
- “I **don't have capacity right now**.” — Current workload/capacity.
- “Your background **isn't a suitable match**.” — Fit-related assessment.

These imply different next steps. Ask one targeted clarification only if it would affect your decision.

#### D5. If you are told to contact someone else

> “Thank you for the suggestion. Would it be appropriate for me to mention that you recommended I contact them?”

Permission to **mention a referral** differs from an actual introduction. Do not claim a recommendation letter or personal endorsement was given.

### E. When the response is unclear, delayed or absent

#### E1. Silence is an absence of data

A professor may be traveling, overloaded or uninterested; you usually cannot know which without a response. Avoid turning silence into a confident story about their intentions.

A reasonable approach:

1. Check whether there is an agreed timeframe or official deadline.
2. Send a concise, contextual follow-up if warranted.
3. Continue formal processes and other suitable contacts when appropriate.
4. Avoid repeated messages that contribute no new information.

#### E2. A gentle follow-up

> **Subject:** Re: Our discussion about [topic]  
>  
> Dear Professor [Surname],  
>  
> I hope you're doing well. I wanted to follow up on my message from [date] about [specific item]. I understand you may have a busy schedule.  
>  
> If you have an opportunity to share any guidance, I'd appreciate it.  
>  
> Best regards,  
> [Your name]

If the deadline is real and approaching, note it and contact the correct office rather than assuming the professor can alter institutional timelines.

#### E3. “I haven't heard back” vs. “You didn't reply”

- **“I haven't heard back yet.”** — neutral description from your perspective.
- **“You didn't reply to my email.”** — can sound accusatory.
- **“I wanted to follow up on...”** — usually the cleanest professional opening.

#### E4. When a reply finally comes

> “Thank you for getting back to me. I appreciate the clarification.”

You can respond to the content without asking why they took so long.

#### E5. When to close an inactive lead

There is no magic number of reminders. If an opportunity has no confirmed next step and multiple well-spaced requests have not produced an answer, focus on other viable paths. Keep your decision based on actual deadlines and information, not the possibility of a future reply.

### F. Comparing options without closing doors unnecessarily

#### F1. More than one ongoing conversation

You may be exploring several groups at the same time. You do not need to claim exclusive commitment during an initial conversation.

If asked:

> “I'm exploring several programs to understand the research opportunities and training environments available. I'm trying to identify the best match before making a decision.”

Honesty is important. There is no need to volunteer a list of every professor you've emailed in every conversation.

#### F2. Being asked for a decision before you have the facts

> “Thank you for the opportunity. Could I have a little time to review the details, including [SPECIFIC CONDITION], before giving you a definite answer?”

Ask about a real decision deadline when necessary:

> “Is there a date by which you would need a response?”

Do not invent an offer or negotiate terms that have not been placed on the table.

#### F3. “I'm considering” vs. “I've decided”

- **I'm considering applying.** — exploring the possibility.
- **I'm planning to apply.** — present intention, still future.
- **I've applied.** — application completed.
- **I've been admitted.** — formal admission occurred.
- **I've accepted the offer.** — acceptance completed.

These verbs encode different stages and should not be substituted for one another.

#### F4. Academic interest vs. financial viability

> “The research direction is appealing. Before deciding whether I can proceed, I would need to understand the funding and tuition arrangements.”

Having scientific interest does not create financial feasibility. Exact conditions should be verified through official documentation.

#### F5. Declining a real opportunity respectfully

> **Subject:** Thank you for the opportunity  
>  
> Dear Dr. [Surname],  
>  
> Thank you very much for discussing [SPECIFIC OPPORTUNITY] with me. After careful consideration, I've decided to pursue a different direction. I appreciate the time you spent explaining the project and answering my questions.  
>  
> Thank you again, and I wish you and your team all the best.  
>  
> Best regards,  
> [Your name]

Be clear once a decision is final; do not keep suggesting interest merely to avoid discomfort.

### G. Language for difficult outcomes: grammar and register

#### G1. “I'm afraid...”

Often introduces unwelcome news politely:

- “I'm afraid we don't have any funded positions this year.”
- “I'm afraid the deadline has passed.”

It does not necessarily mean the speaker is literally afraid. Your response can simply acknowledge the information:

> “I understand. Thank you for letting me know.”

#### G2. “Unfortunately...” vs. “I'm sorry to hear that”

**Unfortunately, ...** commonly signals regrettable circumstances: “Unfortunately, no places are available.”

**I'm sorry to hear that** is appropriate for bad news affecting the other person, but can be slightly overdramatic as a response to an ordinary admissions limitation. “I understand. Thank you for explaining” is often better.

#### G3. “Would you happen to know...?”

A polite indirect information request:

> “Would you happen to know whether there are related projects elsewhere in the department?”

Word order follows a **statement** inside the embedded question: “whether there **are** related projects,” not “whether **are there** related projects.”

#### G4. “At this stage,” “for now,” and “in the future”

- **At this stage** — refers to the present phase; often implies later reconsideration may be possible.
- **For now** — temporary present stance, without promising change.
- **In the future** — nonspecific later time; not a commitment.

Avoid turning “maybe in the future” into “there will be an opening next year.”

#### G5. “Subject to”

**Subject to + noun** expresses a condition:

- “Any offer would be **subject to** meeting the entry requirements.”
- “Recruitment is **subject to** funding availability.”

The condition matters; the conditional statement is not an offer by itself.

#### G6. “An offer” vs. “an invitation”

- **Invitation to apply** = permission or encouragement to submit an application.
- **Invitation to interview** = opportunity to participate in selection.
- **Offer of admission** = a formal decision, which may have conditions.
- **Offer of funding** = separate financial decision, unless clearly included by the authorized body.

Use institution-specific terminology and written documents for actual rights and obligations.

### H. Unexpected situations: realistic branching responses

#### Scenario 1 — Professor praises your work but has no opening

Professor: “You've clearly done a lot of thoughtful work, but I don't have capacity this year.”  
Student: “Thank you. I appreciate that feedback, and I understand the capacity constraint.”  
Professor: “You might contact our neighboring group.”  
Student: “That's helpful. Would it be all right to mention that you suggested I get in touch?”

#### Scenario 2 — Positive conversation, no concrete next step

Professor: “It was a really interesting discussion. Let's stay in touch.”  
Student: “Thank you. Would it be appropriate for me to follow up once I've reviewed the eligibility requirements?”  
Professor: “Sure, that would be fine.”

Record that contact was welcomed. Do not record an offer.

#### Scenario 3 — The professor is unsure about funding

Professor: “The project is promising, but I don't know whether funding will be available.”  
Student: “I understand. Would the department be the best place to check what funding routes are currently available?”  
Professor: “Yes, I'd start there.”

Funding uncertainty remains unresolved.

#### Scenario 4 — Application goes through a committee

Professor: “You can apply, but the committee makes the final decision.”  
Student: “Thank you for clarifying. I'll follow the formal application process.”

Distinguish supervisor interest from decision authority.

#### Scenario 5 — Asked whether you're considering other universities

Professor: “Are you looking at other programs as well?”  
Student: “Yes, I'm considering a few programs and comparing their research training and project opportunities. I'm particularly interested in understanding how your group works with students.”

Respond honestly without pretending to have chosen a first preference.

#### Scenario 6 — Professor suggests a research direction you don't want

Professor: “The available project would involve bench experiments.”  
Student: “Thank you for explaining. My experience and current interests are more aligned with clinical or population-based work. I'd need to consider whether the project's core activities would be a good fit.”

If it clearly cannot work, a respectful decline may be more useful than prolonging the discussion.

#### Scenario 7 — You're asked to accept an opportunity immediately

Professor: “Would you be willing to take this position?”  
Student: “I'm grateful that you're considering me. Before I give you a definite answer, could I clarify the expected responsibilities, funding arrangements and decision timeline?”

This is a genuine negotiation-stage question; confirm whether an actual position and formal offer exist.

#### Scenario 8 — Official rejection arrives

Admissions office: “We regret to inform you that your application was unsuccessful.”  
Student: “Thank you for informing me of the decision. If general feedback is available, I'd appreciate knowing whether there are areas I could strengthen in a future application.”

A feedback request may or may not be possible depending on policy.

#### Scenario 9 — A tentative referral

Professor: “I can suggest a colleague, though I don't know if they're recruiting.”  
Student: “Thank you for the suggestion. I'll check their research and current opportunities before reaching out.”

The existence of a referral does not establish recruitment capacity.

#### Scenario 10 — You have to move on without a response

Student (internal decision): “I've sent the requested materials and one contextual follow-up, but there is still no concrete opportunity or timeline. I'll proceed with other suitable applications and follow any official deadlines.”

There may be no spoken line to deliver here. Sometimes the appropriate skill is deciding to take action without another email.

### I. Extended dialogue: a promising meeting with an uncertain outcome

*Fictional example. The professor likes the student's background but cannot confirm an opening or funding.*

**Professor:** “Your experience with observational research could be relevant to the project we're considering.”  
**Student:** “Thank you. I'd be interested in learning more about it.”  
**Professor:** “We're still waiting to hear whether funding will be available.”  
**Student:** “I see. So the project is still a possibility, but the funding situation hasn't been confirmed.”  
**Professor:** “Exactly. And we'd have to check the program's admission requirements as well.”  
**Student:** “That makes sense. Would you recommend that I check eligibility with the graduate office now?”  
**Professor:** “Yes. That would be a good first step.”  
**Student:** “I'll do that. Would it be useful for me to send you my CV after the meeting?”  
**Professor:** “Yes, feel free to.”  
**Student:** “Thank you. Just to recap, I'll check eligibility and send the CV. The possibility of joining the project would still depend on funding and the formal application process.”  
**Professor:** “That's correct.”  
**Student:** “Great. I appreciate your clarity. It was very helpful to hear more about the project.”  
**Professor:** “Likewise. Best of luck.”  
**Student:** “Thank you again. Have a great day.”

#### I1. Accurate meeting notes afterward

| Confirmed | Still unresolved |
|---|---|
| Professor considers background potentially relevant | Whether recruitment will take place |
| Student may send a CV | Whether funding exists |
| Student should check eligibility | Whether the student would be admitted |
| The group is considering a project | Whether project details will remain the same |

#### I2. Follow-up email reflecting the actual outcome

> **Subject:** Thank you for our discussion  
>  
> Dear Professor [Surname],  
>  
> Thank you for taking the time to meet with me. I appreciated learning about the possible project on [ACTUAL TOPIC].  
>  
> As discussed, I've attached my academic CV and will confirm the current eligibility requirements with the graduate office. I understand that any potential opportunity would depend on the project's funding and the formal admissions process.  
>  
> Thank you again for your time and guidance.  
>  
> Best regards,  
> [Your name]

This remains accurate whether the project eventually opens or not.

#### I3. The alternative: a clear no

**Professor:** “I'm sorry, but I don't think I can supervise another student.”  
**Student:** “Thank you for being upfront about that. I appreciate the chance to learn about your work.”  
**Professor:** “You might want to look into the work of our epidemiology group.”  
**Student:** “Thank you for the suggestion. I'll look into it.”

A short, gracious response is complete. You do not need to persuade someone out of a capacity constraint.

### J. Outcome vocabulary, decision map and end-of-course synthesis

#### J1. Essential response bank

| Situation | Natural response |
|---|---|
| Encouragement | “Thank you. I'm glad my background seems relevant.” |
| Potential project | “I'd be interested in learning more about that possibility.” |
| Formal process | “I'll check the current requirements with the program office.” |
| Conditional funding | “I understand that funding hasn't been confirmed yet.” |
| No capacity | “Thank you for letting me know.” |
| No fit | “That's helpful to know as I consider other directions.” |
| Referral | “Would it be appropriate to mention that you suggested I contact them?” |
| Unclear next step | “Would it make sense to follow up after...?” |
| Delayed response | “I wanted to follow up on my earlier message about...” |
| Need time | “I'd appreciate a little time to review the details before deciding.” |
| Actual decline | “After careful consideration, I've decided to pursue a different direction.” |
| No guaranteed outcome | “I understand the formal decision will follow the program's usual process.” |

#### J2. A practical decision map

1. **Was there a confirmed action?** → Do it within the agreed scope.
2. **Was there a condition?** → Verify it with the responsible institution/person.
3. **Was there general interest only?** → Thank them, identify whether any meaningful next step exists.
4. **Was there a refusal or no vacancy?** → Acknowledge; optionally seek one relevant referral.
5. **Was there no reply?** → Make one contextual follow-up when reasonable, then continue other plans.
6. **Was there an actual offer?** → Read the official conditions and decide based on research fit, supervision, finances and deadlines.

#### J3. Three mistakes to avoid

- Turning **warmth** into **assumed selection**.
- Turning **silence** into a **certain explanation**.
- Turning **polite interest** into an **unwanted commitment**.

#### J4. Putting the eight units together

| Unit | What you can now practice |
|---|---|
| **01 Joining & Greeting** | Enter the meeting and negotiate the opening |
| **02 Small Talk & Building Rapport** | Respond naturally and sustain initial conversation |
| **03 Politeness & Academic Etiquette** | Ask, thank, clarify expectations and manage register |
| **04 Turn-Taking & Conversation Management** | Enter, hold, yield and redirect speaking turns |
| **05 Unexpected Situations & Recovery** | Handle missed words, confusion, thinking pauses and technical disruptions |
| **06 Topic-Specific Discussion** | Explain research, discuss papers, motivations, field and project fit |
| **07 Expressing Expectations & Negotiating** | Discuss supervision, roles, resources, funding and boundaries |
| **08 Wrapping Up & Follow-up** | Close accurately, follow up and respond to real outcomes |

**A complete realistic meeting** can draw from all eight units in a different order each time. Don't learn a single fixed script. Learn the speech function, recognize what was really said, and respond based on actual information.

#### J5. Final principles

1. Interpret statements at their actual level of certainty.
2. Ask clarifying questions when doing so changes your next action.
3. Verify institution-specific rules and formal conditions.
4. Follow through on commitments you genuinely made.
5. Remain courteous without manufacturing enthusiasm or consent.
6. Treat uncertainty as uncertainty and move forward with your own decisions.
