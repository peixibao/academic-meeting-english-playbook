# Lesson 08 · Wrapping Up & Follow-up

## 8.2 Follow-Up Emails, Documents, and Contact Cadence

**Learning objectives:** Write a concise, specific message after a supervisor meeting; distinguish thanks, document delivery, clarification, reminders and status inquiries; use clear subjects, requests and attachments; choose reasonable follow-up timing; communicate across time zones; and recover professionally when replies are delayed, ambiguous or absent.

**Where this fits:** Lesson 03 taught courtesy and requests; Lesson 07 addressed real conditions of supervision and admissions. Here we turn an actual discussion into accurate written communication. A friendly reply is not proof of an offer, and unanswered emails are not automatically rejections.

### A. Decide the purpose before writing the email

A follow-up message should make a recognizable conversational move:

| Message type | Main purpose | Typical trigger |
|---|---|---|
| **Thank-you note** | Appreciate time and briefly reference the discussion | After a substantive meeting |
| **Document delivery** | Send what was requested | Professor asked for a CV or summary |
| **Clarification** | Check a point that remained uncertain | Ambiguous action or responsibility |
| **Progress update** | Report completion of an agreed step | You checked eligibility or revised a document |
| **Gentle reminder** | Resurface an unanswered request | No reply after a reasonable interval |
| **Status inquiry** | Ask whether there is relevant new information | Application or opportunity remains unresolved |

Do not combine every possible purpose into a single long email. A brief thank-you can accompany a requested document; a later reminder may need a different emphasis.

#### A1. What a good follow-up actually proves

It shows that you listened, understood the agreed action and can communicate reliably. It does not manufacture research fit, an admissions decision or an endorsement.

#### A2. The four-part structure

A practical short email often follows:

**Subject → thanks/context → one specific substance or action → clear close.**

> **Subject:** Thank you for speaking with me  
>  
> Dear Dr. [Surname],  
>  
> Thank you for taking the time to meet with me today. I appreciated learning more about your group's work on [VERIFIED TOPIC], particularly your explanation of [ACTUAL POINT].  
>  
> As discussed, I've attached my academic CV. I'll also check the current program eligibility requirements with the graduate office.  
>  
> Thank you again for your time.  
>  
> Best regards,  
> [Your name]

Every bracketed detail must come from a real conversation or verifiable circumstance.

### B. Subject lines: clear and easy to recognize

| Subject | Use case |
|---|---|
| **Thank you for meeting with me** | Simple first-meeting thank-you |
| **Thank you for our discussion on [date]** | Useful if meetings are numerous |
| **Following up on our conversation — [topic]** | Topic-specific continuation |
| **Academic CV following our meeting** | Delivering the requested CV |
| **Follow-up: [program or project] discussion** | Related continuing exchange |
| **Question about [specific item] from our meeting** | One unresolved detail |
| **Re: [existing subject]** | Prefer replying within an active thread where appropriate |

#### B1. “Follow-up” vs. “follow up”

- **A follow-up email** — *follow-up* as an adjective.
- **I will follow up tomorrow.** — *follow up* as a verb.
- **I'm following up on our discussion.** — continuous verb phrase.

**Follow up on** a topic; **follow up with** a person; **follow up by** email.

#### B2. Avoid alarming or vague subjects

“URGENT!!!” is inappropriate unless there is a genuine urgent matter. “Hello professor” gives little context. “Reminder” alone can sound abrupt and unclear.

### C. Thanking the professor without sounding like a form letter

#### C1. Specificity beats exaggerated praise

**Generic:**

> “Thank you for your valuable and inspiring insights. It was an honor to learn from an internationally renowned scholar.”

**More useful:**

> “I particularly appreciated your explanation of how students become involved in developing research questions.”

The second identifies a real informational benefit; do not invent that point if it did not come up.

#### C2. “I enjoyed” vs. “I appreciated” vs. “It was helpful to...”

- **I enjoyed learning about...** — warm, genuine positive experience.
- **I appreciated your explanation of...** — explicit acknowledgment of time or effort.
- **It was helpful to understand...** — focuses on practical value.

These can coexist, but one is usually enough.

#### C3. Writing about tentative interest

- “Our discussion made me interested in learning more about [TOPIC].”
- “I'd be interested in exploring whether [PROJECT] could be a good fit.”
- “I plan to review the program information before deciding on the next step.”

Avoid “I look forward to joining your laboratory” if no place has been offered.

#### C4. “Thank you for your consideration”

Appropriate for an application or request under review; often unnecessary after a purely exploratory conversation.

**After a meeting:** “Thank you again for your time.”  
**After an application:** “Thank you for considering my application.”

### D. Sending documents safely and clearly

#### D1. State what is attached

- “I've attached my academic CV, as discussed.”
- “Please find my CV attached.” — correct, somewhat conventional.
- “I'm attaching the short project overview you requested.”
- “I've included a link to the publicly available paper below.”

**Attach + object:** “I've attached **my CV**.”  
**Attached to + noun:** “The CV is attached **to this email**.”

#### D2. Document names and scope

Good file names are informative and stable, for example:

- `FirstName_LastName_Academic_CV.pdf`
- `FirstName_LastName_Research_Summary.pdf`
- `Project_Overview_Public_Version.pdf`

Use accurate version labels. Do not send unpublished results, confidential protocols, patient-level data or restricted files without appropriate permission.

#### D3. A document email can be very short

> **Subject:** Academic CV following our meeting  
>  
> Dear Professor [Surname],  
>  
> Thank you again for speaking with me earlier today. As discussed, I've attached my academic CV for your reference.  
>  
> I appreciated hearing about [ACTUAL TOPIC]. Please let me know if there is any additional information I can provide.  
>  
> Best regards,  
> [Your name]

“**For your reference**” indicates the document is provided as background. It does not demand feedback.

#### D4. If an attachment is missing

Don't pretend the document was delivered. Reply promptly:

> “Apologies—I realized I forgot to attach the CV in my previous message. I've attached it here. Thank you for your patience.”

If you send the wrong version, name the correction explicitly:

> “I'm sorry; I attached an outdated version. Please use the updated CV attached to this message.”

#### D5. If the professor requests sensitive information

You can clarify the appropriate channel:

> “I'd be happy to provide the supporting material. Could you let me know whether there is a secure university system I should use for the document?”

### E. Timing and contact cadence（跟进节奏）

There is no universal number of days that guarantees the right result. A reasonable cadence depends on what the professor asked for, the deadline and the context.

#### E1. Common distinctions

- **Timely follow-up after a meeting:** Send an accurate thank-you or requested material while the discussion is still fresh.
- **Deadline-based communication:** If a date was agreed, honor that date. If you cannot, communicate before it expires.
- **No reply to a non-urgent email:** Allow a reasonable interval, factoring in weekends, holidays, conference travel and academic workload.
- **An approaching official deadline:** Explain the actual deadline briefly and contact the appropriate office when the issue concerns institutional rules.

A short thank-you the same day or next working day is common practice; it is a convention, not a requirement. For unanswered non-urgent messages, a polite follow-up after roughly a week or two can be reasonable, but never treat that interval as a guaranteed rule.

#### E2. “Just following up” and other openings

- “I wanted to follow up on my email from [date] about [topic].”
- “I was wondering whether you'd had a chance to review [DOCUMENT].”
- “I know this is a busy time, so I wanted to briefly follow up on [REQUEST].”
- “I'm following up because the [OFFICIAL DEADLINE] is approaching.”

“I just wanted to...” softens the email, but too many apologies and minimizers make the request harder to understand.

#### E3. Avoid claiming an unagreed deadline

**Unhelpful:** “As we agreed, I expect your reply by Monday.” (when nobody agreed)  
**Better:** “I understand that you may be busy. If you have a chance to respond before [ACTUAL DEADLINE], that would help me plan my next step.”

#### E4. Contact cadence and nonresponse

One thoughtful reminder is often useful. Repeated daily messages, escalating tone and sending the same email to many unrelated recipients are generally counterproductive. Formal application updates usually belong with admissions staff unless a supervisor has instructed otherwise.

### F. Polite reminders and requests for information

#### F1. Reminder when a requested action is unanswered

> **Subject:** Re: [Previous subject]  
>  
> Dear Dr. [Surname],  
>  
> I hope you're doing well. I wanted to follow up on my message from [date] regarding [specific request]. I understand you may have a busy schedule.  
>  
> If you're able to share any guidance when convenient, I'd appreciate it.  
>  
> Best regards,  
> [Your name]

**When convenient** is open-ended. If there is a real deadline, mention it plainly.

#### F2. Asking about a specific next step

> “You mentioned that eligibility should be confirmed with the graduate office. I've now received their response. Would it be useful for me to forward that information?”

This follows up with new information instead of resending the same request.

#### F3. Checking whether further contact is welcome

> “Would it be appropriate for me to contact you again once I've submitted the formal application?”

Respect a “no” or an ambiguous response; do not interpret silence as a standing invitation.

#### F4. Following up on an introduction

> “Thank you again for offering to introduce me to your colleague. Please let me know if a short summary of my interests would be helpful.”

Do not tell others an introduction has been arranged until it actually happens.

### G. Written grammar and pragmatic precision

#### G1. “I look forward to” + noun or -ing

Correct:

- “I look forward to **hearing** from you.”
- “I look forward to **our next conversation**.”

Incorrect:

- “I look forward to **hear** from you.”

The *to* in **look forward to** is a preposition, so a verb afterward takes the **-ing** form.

#### G2. “I would appreciate it if...”

> “I'd appreciate it if you could confirm whether the form is required.”

Useful for a real request. Compare:

- “I'd appreciate **your advice**.”
- “I'd appreciate **it if you could advise me**.”
- “I appreciate **you taking the time to explain**.”

#### G3. “If you have a chance” vs. “when you have a chance”

- **If you have a chance:** acknowledges it may not be possible.
- **When you have a chance:** assumes there will likely be an opportunity, while still not demanding an immediate response.

Both are courteous; choose based on the real expectation.

#### G4. “As discussed” vs. “As agreed”

> “As discussed, I've attached the CV.” — The topic was discussed; works if sending the document was mentioned.

> “As agreed, I've sent the revised summary.” — Implies explicit agreement about this action.

Do not say **as agreed** when the professor merely floated an idea.

#### G5. “Could you advise” and “Could you advise me”

Both can work, but “Could you advise **me on** the next step?” is explicit and natural. “Could you give me some advice about...?” is also fine.

#### G6. The double meaning of “application status”

“Could you update me on my application status?” may be appropriate to a graduate office handling admissions, but an individual professor may not control the portal or decision. Ask the person who actually has the information.

### H. Unexpected situations: ready-to-adapt emails

#### Scenario 1 — A polite thank-you after a useful meeting

> **Subject:** Thank you for today's conversation  
>  
> Dear Dr. [Surname],  
>  
> Thank you for taking the time to speak with me today. I especially appreciated your explanation of [SPECIFIC DISCUSSION POINT]. It helped me better understand [WHAT YOU ACTUALLY LEARNED].  
>  
> Thank you again for your time.  
>  
> Best regards,  
> [Your name]

#### Scenario 2 — You promised a CV and a short summary

> **Subject:** CV and research summary following our discussion  
>  
> Dear Professor [Surname],  
>  
> Thank you again for the meeting. As discussed, I've attached my CV and a one-page overview of [YOUR ACTUAL RESEARCH EXPERIENCE].  
>  
> Please let me know if you would prefer a different format or need any clarification.  
>  
> Best regards,  
> [Your name]

#### Scenario 3 — You need to correct a factual statement

> **Subject:** Quick clarification following our meeting  
>  
> Dear Dr. [Surname],  
>  
> Thank you again for our discussion. I wanted to correct one detail from my description of [PROJECT]. I said [INCORRECT STATEMENT]; the accurate information is [VERIFIED CORRECTION].  
>  
> I apologize for the confusion and wanted to make sure you had the correct information.  
>  
> Best regards,  
> [Your name]

Correct promptly. Do not quietly replace the claim without acknowledgment if it could affect their judgment.

#### Scenario 4 — The professor hasn't answered a document question

> “I wanted to follow up on whether a one-page research summary would be useful. I'm happy to send one if it would help, and I completely understand if no additional document is needed at this stage.”

If the topic is already settled, don't keep asking.

#### Scenario 5 — A deadline approaches

> “I'm following up because the formal application deadline is [VERIFIED DATE]. Would you recommend that I submit my application before arranging another conversation, or should I contact the program office about this?”

Check the deadline through the official program source; do not guess.

#### Scenario 6 — A professor replies “Sounds good”

Professor: “Sounds good. Best of luck!”

You can reply briefly if a response is useful:

> “Thank you! I appreciate your guidance.”

No need to extract an admission promise from routine politeness.

#### Scenario 7 — The professor says they're traveling

Professor: “I'm away at a conference this week. I'll get back to you when I return.”  
Student: “Of course. Thank you for letting me know, and I hope the conference goes well.”

Avoid an immediate additional reminder unless an actual deadline requires clarification.

#### Scenario 8 — Wrong recipient or accidental send

If the content was merely misaddressed and harmless, apologize and clarify. If sensitive data were sent to the wrong person, follow appropriate institutional security/reporting procedures promptly; a polite apology alone may not resolve the problem.

#### Scenario 9 — No response after a reminder

If a professor remains silent, consider whether you need to proceed with formal applications, contact program staff, or explore other supervisors. There is no language trick that forces a reply. Lesson 08.3 covers moving forward without overinterpreting silence.

### I. Extended email exchange: from meeting to next steps

*Fictional example: the professor provides guidance but does not make an offer.*

**Email 1 — Student (after the meeting)**

> **Subject:** Thank you for our discussion  
>  
> Dear Dr. Morgan,  
>  
> Thank you for speaking with me today. I appreciated learning about the group's ongoing population-health projects and how new students typically become involved.  
>  
> As discussed, I've attached my academic CV. I'll also check the graduate program's eligibility criteria with the admissions office.  
>  
> Best regards,  
> [Student name]

**Email 2 — Professor**

> Thanks for the CV. Yes, please check with admissions about eligibility. If you decide to apply, feel free to let me know.

**Email 3 — Student (after checking, only if relevant)**

> Dear Dr. Morgan,  
>  
> Thank you for your guidance. I've now checked the published eligibility requirements and received clarification from the graduate office about [ACTUAL POINT]. I'm planning to proceed with an application.  
>  
> I appreciated the opportunity to learn more about your work.  
>  
> Best regards,  
> [Student name]

**Possible alternative reply:**

> Professor: “Thanks for the update. The department will review applications through its usual process.”

**Interpretation:** The professor acknowledged the email and identified a process. That still does not mean the student was admitted.

#### I1. Why the exchange works

1. The first message thanks and follows through on a real request.
2. The professor's reply is interpreted literally, without implied commitments.
3. The second student message contains new information, not a generic “checking in.”
4. No publication, position or funding promise is invented.

### J. Phrase bank and final sending checklist

#### J1. Ready-to-use lines

| Function | Expression |
|---|---|
| Thank-you opener | “Thank you for taking the time to speak with me.” |
| Specific appreciation | “I particularly appreciated your explanation of...” |
| Attach document | “As discussed, I've attached...” |
| Offer additional detail | “Please let me know if any additional information would be useful.” |
| Clarify next step | “Would you recommend that I...?” |
| Gentle follow-up | “I wanted to follow up on my email from [date] about...” |
| Note deadline | “I'm following up because the formal deadline is approaching.” |
| Ask for correct contact | “Would the graduate office be the best place to ask about this?” |
| Acknowledge delay | “Thank you for getting back to me.” |
| Correct mistake | “I wanted to correct one detail from my earlier message.” |
| Keep interest provisional | “I'd be interested in exploring that possibility further.” |
| Close | “Best regards,” / “Kind regards,” |

#### J2. A compact quality checklist

Before sending, ask:

1. Is the recipient's name and title correct?
2. Does the subject identify the purpose?
3. Did I accurately describe what was discussed and agreed?
4. Is the actual request clear in one reading?
5. Does the email contain an invented offer, result, policy or deadline?
6. Are the promised attachments present, correctly named and appropriate to share?
7. Is the message concise enough for a busy reader?
8. Am I sending to the person who controls this decision or information?
9. If this is a reminder, is it justified by context and timing?

#### J3. Golden rule

**Write the shortest email that faithfully advances the conversation.** A useful follow-up is specific, accurate and easy to answer. It does not need excessive deference or theatrical enthusiasm.
