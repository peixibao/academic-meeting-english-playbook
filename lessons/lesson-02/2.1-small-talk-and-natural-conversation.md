# Lesson 02 · Small Talk & Building Rapport

## 2.1 From Greetings to Natural Conversation

**Focus:** Respond naturally, develop a topic without sounding scripted, and move from social conversation toward an academic discussion.

### A. The conversational function of small talk

In an academic or professional meeting, small talk can:

1. **Establish rapport（建立融洽关系）:** Help participants feel comfortable.
2. **Assess readiness:** Allow a little conversational settling-in before substantive work.
3. **Create a bridge:** Lead from greetings into the main discussion.

A key ability is **conversational expansion（对话延展）**: responding to the detail the other person has just introduced.

**Example**

Professor: “How are you doing today?”  
Student: “Pretty well, thanks! How about you?”  
Professor: “Not bad. It's been a busy morning.”  
Student: “Oh, have you had a lot of meetings today?”  
Professor: “Yes, quite a few, actually.”  
Student: “Sounds like a packed schedule. I appreciate you fitting me in.”

The student follows the professor's topic, rather than switching abruptly to a new one.

### B. Recognizing different small-talk openings

| Professor might say | Typical intention |
|---|---|
| “How are you?” | Conventional, often a brief greeting |
| “How are you doing?” | Conversational and slightly more personal |
| “How's it going?” | Casual, common in North American English |
| “How's everything going?” | Broad invitation to update |
| “How's your day going?” | Current day |
| “How's your week been?” | The week so far |
| “How have things been going?” | Recent developments leading to now |
| “What's keeping you busy these days?” | Current work and commitments |
| “How are things on your end?” | Often used across locations or online |

#### B1. Matching the other person's tone

**Neutral professional**

Professor: “Hi. How are you?”  
Student: “I'm doing well, thanks. How about you?”

**Friendly professional**

Professor: “Hey! How's it going?”  
Student: “Pretty good, thanks! How about you?”

**Style matching（交流风格适配）** means adjusting your conversational tone to the context. It doesn't mean imitating the person's personality, accent, or every word.

#### B2. Time perspective: “How are you?” and “How have you been?”

“How are you?” is a straightforward present-time greeting.

“How have you been?” uses the **present perfect（现在完成时）**, connecting a period before now to the present. It is particularly natural when reconnecting after some time.

“How have things been going on your end?” similarly invites an update on a recent period.

### C. A flexible three-part response

**Answer → Expand → Reciprocate**

These are optional moves; a short answer can be enough if the professor is ready to start.

**Short**

Professor: “How's your day going?”  
Student: “Pretty well, thanks! How about yours?”

**Expanded**

Professor: “How's your day going?”  
Student: “Pretty well, thanks! I spent most of the day working on research analyses. How about you?”

**Conversational**

Professor: “How's your week been?”  
Student: “It's been quite busy, actually. I've been balancing an internship with a couple of research projects, but things are going well overall. How about your week?”

#### C1. Describing your week

| Expression | Nuance |
|---|---|
| “Pretty good, thanks.” | Relaxed and friendly |
| “I'm doing well, thanks.” | Neutral professional |
| “Not too bad, actually.” | Mildly positive and casual |
| “Things have been going pretty smoothly.” | Progress with few problems |
| “It's been a busy week, but a productive one.” | Workload with positive result |
| “A little hectic, but in a good way.” | Very active but not necessarily negative |
| “I've had quite a lot on my plate lately.” | Multiple responsibilities |
| “I've been keeping busy with research and my internship.” | Current activities |
| “The past few weeks have been a bit of a whirlwind.” | Many developments in quick succession |

##### Have a lot on my plate

To have numerous responsibilities: “I've had a lot on my plate with research and applications.”

##### A bit of a whirlwind

A busy or fast-changing period: “It's been a bit of a whirlwind with deadlines lately.”

### D. Reciprocal questions and contingent follow-ups

**Reciprocity（互惠式回应）** helps keep an exchange two-sided.

| Expression | When appropriate |
|---|---|
| “How about you?” | Broad response about the person |
| “How about yours?” | A previously mentioned thing: day, week, etc. |
| “And yourself?” | Brief, slightly polished |
| “How's your day going?” | Current day |
| “How's your week been?” | Recent week |
| “How have things been on your end?” | Remote work or cross-location conversation |
| “Have you had a busy day so far?” | Acknowledging a busy schedule |

#### D1. Follow the detail you've just heard

Professor: “I've been preparing for a conference.”

Natural possible follow-ups:
- “Oh, which conference are you preparing for?”
- “Will you be presenting any of your recent work?”
- “Is it coming up soon?”
- “Have you been working on a presentation?”

This is **contingent questioning（基于对方回答的追问）**. The question grows from the other person's previous turn.

Not every response needs another question. Sometimes “That sounds exciting!” followed by a pause is enough.

### E. Common topics in international academic meetings

#### E1. Time zones and scheduling

Professor: “What time is it over there?”  
Student: “It's about ten in the evening here, actually. But I'm a bit of a night owl, so the timing works out well for me.”

| Phrase | Usage |
|---|---|
| “It's already quite late here.” | Local time |
| “The time difference can be a little tricky.” | Cross-time-zone coordination |
| “The timing works out well for me.” | Reassure the other person |
| “I'm used to staying up late.” | Explain a late call |
| “I appreciate you accommodating the time difference.” | Thank them for flexibility |

##### Work out well

To turn out conveniently or successfully: “That time works out well for me.”

##### Accommodate

To make arrangements that take someone's needs into account: “Thanks for accommodating the time difference.”

**Expanded exchange**

Professor: “Isn't it quite late where you are?”  
Student: “Yes, but no worries. I'm naturally a night owl.”  
Professor: “I'm usually an early riser.”  
Student: “That probably makes scheduling across time zones a little easier for us, then!”

#### E2. Current studies and internships

Professor: “Are you still studying, or have you already graduated?”  
Student: “I'm currently in my final year, and I'm also doing a public health internship.”  
Professor: “How's the internship going?”  
Student: “It's been valuable. I'm getting a closer look at how public health work is carried out in practice.”

Useful expressions:
- “I'm currently interning at [ORGANIZATION].”
- “I'm in the middle of my final-year internship.”
- “I've been gaining hands-on experience in [AREA].”
- “It's interesting to see how things work outside an academic setting.”
- “I've been juggling the internship with my research commitments.”

##### Hands-on experience

Direct practical experience, rather than learning solely through lectures or reading.

##### Juggle

Manage different commitments simultaneously: “I've been juggling research, coursework, and applications.”

#### E3. Their schedule or workload

Professor: “I've had back-to-back meetings all morning.”  
Student: “Sounds like a packed morning. Thanks again for fitting me in.”

Other responses:
- “Sounds like you've had a full schedule.”
- “I hope you get a bit of a break later.”
- “Are things particularly busy with teaching at the moment?”

##### Back-to-back

Consecutive, with little or no break between activities.

##### Fit someone in

Make time for someone within a busy schedule.

#### E4. Location, city, and weather

Professor: “Have you always lived in [CITY]?”  
Student: “I grew up in [PLACE], but moved to [CITY] for university.”  
Professor: “How do you like it?”  
Student: “I really like it. There's a good balance between city life and green spaces.”  
Student: “Have you been based in [THEIR CITY] for long?”

Related prompts:
- “What's [CITY] like?”
- “Is it getting cold there yet?”
- “Have you ever visited [COUNTRY]?”
- “How do you find living there?”

### F. Active listening and follow-up moves

**Backchannels（简短回应信号）** are short responses that show someone you're following their point.

| Expression | Function |
|---|---|
| “Right.” | Acknowledge |
| “I see.” | Indicate understanding |
| “Oh, really?” | Interest or mild surprise |
| “That's interesting.” | Encourage elaboration |
| “That makes sense.” | Acknowledge a reason or explanation |
| “Absolutely.” | Strong agreement |
| “I can imagine.” | Recognize a situation |
| “Sounds like a busy week!” | Respond to workload |
| “That must be exciting.” | Respond to positive news |
| “That's good to hear.” | React to good news |

“I see” acknowledges understanding; “That makes sense” recognizes the logic; “Absolutely” signals agreement.

#### F1. Questions that invite elaboration

| Expression | Focus |
|---|---|
| “How did that go?” | Outcome |
| “How has that been going?” | Ongoing progress |
| “What was that like?” | Experience |
| “How did you find that experience?” | Evaluation |
| “Was that something you expected?” | Reflection |
| “Has that been keeping you busy?” | Workload |
| “What happened next?” | Continuing a story |
| “How did you get involved in that?” | How a person entered the activity |

##### How did you find ... ?

Here, `find` means evaluate or experience, not locate: “How did you find the conference?” = “What did you think of it?” The latter is particularly common in North American speech.

### G. Managing unexpected small-talk turns

#### Scenario 1: The professor gives a short answer

Student: “How's your day going?”  
Professor: “Pretty good, thanks.”  
Student: “Glad to hear it! Thanks again for meeting with me.”

A short reply may signal readiness to begin. Don't force more small talk.

#### Scenario 2: The professor gives a detailed, busy-week answer

Professor: “We've had several deadlines and a major grant application to finish.”  
Student: “That sounds like a lot to manage. Has the grant application been taking up most of your time?”  
Professor: “Pretty much.”  
Student: “I can imagine. I hope the submission goes smoothly.”

#### Scenario 3: An unexpected personal-interest question

Professor: “What do you do outside research?”  
Student: “I spend quite a bit of my free time drawing. It helps me unwind after a long day.”  
Professor: “What kind of art?”  
Student: “Mostly character illustrations. I enjoy visual storytelling.”

##### Unwind

Relax after stress or work. A natural North American expression: “Drawing helps me unwind.”

#### Scenario 4: You prefer to keep a personal question professional

Professor: “What made you decide to study abroad?”  
Student: “I've been looking for more rigorous methodological training and a chance to experience a different academic environment. I'm particularly interested in developing as an independent researcher.”

This is an option, not a rule requiring you to conceal personal motivations. A more candid answer can also be appropriate when comfortable.

#### Scenario 5: You don't recognize an idiom

Professor: “It's been a bit of a zoo here.”  
Student: “Sorry, what do you mean by ‘a zoo’?”  
Professor: “I mean it's been chaotic.”  
Student: “Ah, got it. Sounds hectic!”

##### A bit of a zoo

Informal idiom for a chaotic environment.

#### Scenario 6: Silence or an unplanned pause

Refer to a relevant existing topic:  
“By the way, you mentioned a new project earlier. Is that something you're still involved in?”

Or transition:  
“I'd be interested in hearing a little more about the work your group is doing at the moment.”

### H. Complete example dialogues

#### H1. Brief and professional

Professor: “Hi! How are you doing?”  
Student: “Pretty well, thanks! How about you?”  
Professor: “Doing well. It's been a busy morning.”  
Student: “Sounds like a full schedule. I appreciate you making time for this.”  
Professor: “Of course. Shall we get started?”  
Student: “Absolutely.”

#### H2. Time zones developing into research

Professor: “What time is it over there?”  
Student: “It's around ten in the evening.”  
Professor: “That's late! Hope it isn't inconvenient.”  
Student: “Not at all—I'm a bit of a night owl, so it works out well.”  
Professor: “So, how have things been going?”  
Student: “Pretty well. I've been juggling my internship and a couple of research projects.”  
Professor: “What kind of internship?”  
Student: “One in public health practice. It's given me a chance to see the work outside university.”  
Professor: “And what kind of research?”  
Student: “Mainly clinical and population-based epidemiology.”

#### H3. Friendly opening developing into academic interests

Professor: “Hey! How's everything going?”  
Student: “Pretty good. It's been a busy few weeks, but things are going well. How about you?”  
Professor: “Busy! I'm teaching a course this term.”  
Student: “Oh, what course?”  
Professor: “Clinical trials methodology.”  
Student: “That sounds closely related to what I'm hoping to learn more about.”  
Professor: “Really? Tell me about your interests.”

### I. Transferable language functions

| Function | Flexible pattern |
|---|---|
| Brief answer | “Pretty well, thanks. How about you?” |
| Add detail | “I've been keeping busy with ...” |
| Multiple responsibilities | “I've been juggling X with Y.” |
| Acknowledge workload | “Sounds like you've had a packed schedule.” |
| Ask for progress | “How has that been going?” |
| Ask about an experience | “What was that like?” |
| Recognize an inconvenience | “I can imagine that must have been challenging.” |
| Move to work | “I'd love to hear more about your current projects.” |
| Start promptly | “Absolutely. I'm happy to get started.” |

### J. Responding versus developing

**Responding** acknowledges what the other speaker said: “That's interesting!”

**Developing** adds information or asks a relevant question: “How did you get involved in that?”

**Conversational flexibility（对话灵活性）** involves choosing when to acknowledge, elaborate, ask, change topics, or stop.
