# Lesson 02 · Small Talk & Building Rapport

## 2.2 Sustaining Conversations Through Follow-up Questions and Active Listening

**Learning objectives:** Develop conversations beyond a greeting, distinguish different kinds of follow-up questions, use active-listening signals appropriately, paraphrase meaning, connect your own experiences, and recognize when the other person wants to change topics.

This section extends 2.1. **Focus:** maintaining conversation without making it feel like an interview.

### A. The mechanics of conversational development

Three common conversational actions:

1. **Acknowledgment** — Show that you heard and understood.
2. **Elaboration** — Add a relevant observation, reaction, or personal connection.
3. **Follow-up** — Invite the speaker to expand on a specific detail.

You may use one or several; the appropriate combination depends on the exchange.

**Example**

Professor: “I've been working on a new project with colleagues in Europe.”  
Student: “That sounds interesting! What kind of project is it?”  
Professor: “It's a multicentre clinical trial involving several hospitals.”  
Student: “I see. So you're coordinating across multiple sites. That must involve quite a bit of planning.”  
Professor: “Exactly. Recruitment and data management can be complicated.”  
Student: “I can imagine. How do you usually handle data coordination across the sites?”

| Student move | Function |
|---|---|
| “That sounds interesting!” | Acknowledgment |
| “What kind of project is it?” | Request for basic information |
| “So you're coordinating across multiple sites.” | Paraphrasing（复述理解） |
| “That must involve quite a bit of planning.” | Inference（推断） |
| “How do you usually handle data coordination?” | Deeper follow-up |

**Topic development（话题深化）:** The next question becomes more specific as you learn more. Respond at the depth supported by what the speaker has revealed.

### B. The follow-up question ladder

| Level | Purpose | Example |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Clarification | Understand what is meant | “What kind of project is that?” |
| 2. Elaboration | Invite details | “Could you tell me a little more about that?” |
| 3. Explanation | Understand causes, methods, or challenges | “How did you approach that?” |
| 4. Connection | Relate this to prior knowledge or experience | “Does that connect with your earlier work on...?” |

This is an analytical tool, not a compulsory linear order.

#### B1. Clarification questions

| Expression | Focus |
|---|---|
| “What kind of project is that?” | General nature |
| “What's that about?” | Casual, broad question |
| “Which area does that fall under?” | Discipline |
| “Is that something you've been working on recently?” | Time frame |
| “When you say X, do you mean Y?” | Interpretation check |
| “What exactly does X involve?” | More precise meaning |

##### When you say X, do you mean Y?

Professor: “We're working on a methodological project.”  
Student: “When you say methodological, do you mean developing new statistical methods, or evaluating existing approaches?”  
Professor: “Mostly evaluating existing approaches.”  
Student: “Ah, I see. That sounds relevant to my interests.”

This checks what the speaker means while making your working interpretation visible.

#### B2. Elaboration questions

| Expression | Nuance |
|---|---|
| “Could you tell me a little more about that?” | Broad, polite |
| “I'd love to hear more about that.” | Warm interest |
| “Could you elaborate on that a little?” | Academic and specific |
| “What does that involve in practice?” | Implementation |
| “How has that been going so far?” | Progress |
| “What has your experience been like?” | Personal evaluation |

##### Elaborate on

`Elaborate on something` = expand with details: “Could you elaborate on how the project is organized?”

##### In practice

`In practice` = in actual implementation: “How does supervising an MSc student typically work in practice?”

#### B3. Explanation questions

| Expression | Focus |
|---|---|
| “How did you approach that?” | Strategy |
| “What led you to that decision?” | Reasoning |
| “What prompted you to explore that question?” | Original motivation |
| “What were the main challenges?” | Difficulties |
| “How did you deal with those challenges?” | Responses |
| “What did you find most interesting about that?” | Significance |
| “Was there anything that surprised you?” | Unexpected outcome |

##### What prompted you to ... ?

`Prompt` = motivate or trigger: “What prompted you to become interested in clinical trials?”

##### What led you to ... ?

Asks about conditions or reasons: “What led you to choose that particular design?”

#### B4. Connection questions

| Expression | Function |
|---|---|
| “Does that connect with your earlier work on X?” | Prior research connection |
| “Is that related to the project you mentioned earlier?” | Link to earlier turn |
| “Would a similar approach work in X?” | Explore transferability |
| “That reminds me of something I encountered in my own research.” | Introduce relevant experience |
| “I wonder whether that could also apply to X.” | Tentative connection |
| “How does that fit into your group's broader research direction?” | Move toward bigger picture |

Professor: “We've been looking at approaches for handling missing outcome data.”  
Student: “That's interesting. I've encountered missing-data issues in observational research. I wonder how much recommended approaches differ between observational studies and randomized trials.”

This is a natural way to connect different research experiences without pretending to have equivalent expertise.

### C. Active listening while the other person speaks

**Active listening（积极倾听）** includes short speech, timing, and attentive nonverbal behavior.

#### C1. Three kinds of listener responses

| Function | Examples | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| Continuers（鼓励继续说） | “Mm-hmm.” / “Right.” / “Uh-huh.” | I'm following; continue |
| Understanding signals | “I see.” / “Got it.” / “That makes sense.” | I understand |
| Evaluations | “That's interesting.” / “That's impressive.” / “That sounds challenging.” | A reaction to content |

**Encourage continuation**

Professor: “We started the project two years ago, and initially...”  
Student: “Mm-hmm.”  
Professor: “...we were mainly interested in feasibility, but the focus changed.”

**Confirm an explanation**

Professor: “We changed the analysis plan before we examined outcome data.”  
Student: “I see. That makes sense.”

**React to achievement**

Professor: “We managed to recruit patients from twelve hospitals.”  
Student: “Wow, that's impressive!”

#### C2. Calibrate your enthusiasm

| Mild | Moderate | Strong |
|---|---|---|
| “I see.” | “That's interesting.” | “That's fascinating!” |
| “Right.” | “That sounds useful.” | “That's really impressive!” |
| “I understand.” | “That sounds challenging.” | “That must have been incredibly difficult.” |

**Response calibration（回应强度调整）** means matching the emotional force of your reaction to the actual content.

If someone merely describes a routine administrative task, “That's fascinating!” may sound exaggerated. Strong praise can be appropriate for a significant achievement.

#### C3. Timing in video calls

Remote audio delay makes timing especially important. Short responses fit after completed ideas or at a natural pause. During complex explanations, a nod may be better than repeated spoken interjections.

Distinguish:

- **Thinking pause:** The speaker is gathering thoughts and may continue.
- **Turn-completion pause:** The speaker has completed a contribution and may expect an answer.

Consider sentence completion, intonation, eye contact and an explicit invitation to respond; no single cue is perfectly reliable.

### D. Paraphrasing and reflecting meaning

**Paraphrasing（用自己的话复述）** means restating the core message in your own words to check or demonstrate understanding.

#### D1. Useful structures

| Expression | Typical function |
|---|---|
| “So you're saying that...” | Restate an interpretation |
| “So, if I understand correctly...” | Explicitly check |
| “It sounds like...” | Offer an inference |
| “From what you're describing...” | Ground your interpretation |
| “So the main challenge was...” | Identify central issue |
| “It seems like the key issue is...” | Summarize a problem |
| “In other words...” | Reformulate |

The degree of certainty differs: `It sounds like` is an inference; `If I understand correctly` expressly invites correction.

#### D2. Checking your understanding

Professor: “We planned a simple analysis, but the data turned out more complicated than expected.”  
Student: “So the complexity meant you had to revise your original analytical approach?”  
Professor: “Exactly.”  
Student: “I see. What was the biggest challenge in making those adjustments?”

**Listen → Paraphrase → Confirm → Follow up.**

#### D3. Reflecting a negative experience

Professor: “We spent months preparing the proposal, but unfortunately it wasn't funded.”  
Student: “That's disappointing, especially after putting so much work into it.”  
Professor: “Yes, but we learned quite a lot from the process.”  
Student: “I'm glad something useful came out of it. Are you planning to revise the proposal?”

The response acknowledges the experience before asking about what happens next.

#### D4. Avoid assuming too much

Professor: “We've had difficulties with recruitment.”  
Student: “It sounds like recruitment has been a challenge. What have been the main obstacles?”

This allows the professor to elaborate without having their meaning exaggerated.

### E. Follow-up without interrogation: add a relevant connection

**Self-disclosure（适度自我分享）**: Briefly offer your own relevant thought or experience rather than asking question after question.

#### E1. Acknowledgment → Connection → Question

Professor: “I spend a lot of time supervising students.”  
Student: “That sounds rewarding. I've found that working closely with supervisors has been valuable in my own research experience. What do you enjoy most about mentoring students?”

#### E2. Expressions for linking to your experience

| Expression | Nuance |
|---|---|
| “That reminds me of...” | Related experience |
| “I've had a similar experience with...” | Similarity |
| “That's something I've been thinking about as well.” | Shared interest |
| “I've encountered something similar in my own work.” | Academic parallel |
| “That actually connects to one of my research interests.” | Academic bridge |
| “I've noticed something similar during my internship.” | Practical link |
| “That's a little different from what I've experienced.” | Diplomatic contrast |

##### Actually

“That actually reminds me of...” can signal that a connection has occurred spontaneously during the conversation.

##### Encounter / Run into

`Encounter` = come across an issue (slightly more academic). `Run into` = meet an issue, common conversationally.

“We ran into a similar problem in one of our projects.”

#### E3. Appropriate length for self-disclosure

For ordinary small talk, one sentence may suffice. For a substantive academic discussion, offer two or three sentences if the other person shows interest.

Professor: “I've been working with longitudinal data.”  
Student: “Oh, interesting. Most of my experience has been with cross-sectional data, so longitudinal analysis is something I'd like to learn more about. What do you find most challenging about longitudinal datasets?”

### F. Responding to different kinds of answers

#### Scenario 1: A lengthy explanation

Professor: “Each hospital has different procedures and data-recording practices.”  
Student: “I see. So maintaining consistency across sites is a major concern.”  
Professor: “Exactly.”  
Student: “How do you usually address those differences?”

Identify one major point; don't attempt to summarize every detail.

#### Scenario 2: A vague answer

Student: “How has the project been going?”  
Professor: “Interesting. A few challenges, but we're making progress.”  
Student: “That's good to hear. Have the challenges mainly involved the design or the practical side of running the project?”

An alternative question can encourage elaboration; remain open if the real issue is something else.

#### Scenario 3: The professor changes the topic

Student: “How's the conference preparation?”  
Professor: “Pretty well. Anyway, I had a chance to look at your CV.”  
Student: “Oh, great. Thanks for taking a look.”  
Professor: “I noticed your sleep epidemiology project.”  
Student: “Yes, that's been one of my main research experiences.”

`Anyway` often signals a transition. Follow the new conversational direction.

#### Scenario 4: The professor corrects you

Student: “So you're developing a new statistical method?”  
Professor: “Not exactly. We're comparing existing methods under different conditions.”  
Student: “Ah, I see. So the focus is evaluating existing approaches.”  
Professor: “That's right.”  
Student: “Got it. What criteria are you using to compare them?”

Useful acknowledgment after correction:
- “Thanks for clarifying.”
- “Ah, I see.”
- “So the distinction is between X and Y?”
- “That makes more sense now.”

#### Scenario 5: You lack the technical background

Professor: “We've been studying noninferiority trial analysis.”  
Student: “That's an area I'm still learning about. I'd be interested to know what drew you to that research question.”

Other useful follow-ups:
- “What makes that question important in practice?”
- “How does that connect to clinical decisions?”
- “What problems are you hoping to address?”

You can engage seriously without pretending specialist knowledge.

#### Scenario 6: The professor shares something negative

Professor: “It's been a difficult few months for the project.”  
Student: “I'm sorry to hear that. I hope things are starting to improve.”

Do not pressure someone into sharing uncomfortable personal or professional details.

#### Scenario 7: They invite you to expand

Student: “I've become interested in interdisciplinary research.”  
Professor: “What does interdisciplinary mean in your case?”  
Student: “For me, it's about connecting clinical questions with epidemiological methods and population-level evidence. My undergraduate training spans biomedical sciences, clinical medicine, and public health, so I'm interested in how those perspectives inform one another.”  
Professor: “Can you give an example?”  
Student: “Sure. We can study a clinical condition at the population level to understand its distribution and associated factors.”

### G. Reading signals: continue, yield, or transition

**Conversational sensitivity（对话情境敏感度）** means noticing whether the other person wants to continue a topic.

| Signal | Possible interpretation | Response |
|---|---|---|
| “Tell me more.” | Invitation to expand | Give relevant detail |
| “How did you become interested in that?” | Interest in your background | Explain and give an example |
| A related question | Engagement | Answer it |
| “Right, okay,” then a new topic | Transition | Follow the new topic |
| “Anyway...” | Often a topic change | Let the conversation shift |
| “We should probably get started.” | Move to agenda | Agree |
| Short answer, no follow-up | Limited time or interest, possibly | Acknowledge and move on |
| Pause | Ambiguous | Allow a beat and monitor cues |

A short response may reflect communication style, fatigue or connection lag; don't interpret it as a judgment.

### H. Three extended model conversations

#### H1. From internship small talk to a meaningful question

Professor: “How's your week been?”  
Student: “Pretty busy. I've been juggling my internship with research projects.”  
Professor: “You're doing an internship as well?”  
Student: “Yes, at a public health agency.”  
Professor: “What kind of work?”  
Student: “Mainly public health practice. It's valuable to see how things work outside university.”  
Professor: “That must give you a different perspective.”  
Student: “Definitely. It's made me think more about how research findings translate into public health practice.”  
Professor: “That's important.”  
Student: “Do your students often have opportunities to work with people outside academia?”

The sequence moves from an ordinary update to professional context, reflection, and a question about the research environment.

#### H2. Shared research challenges without overstating experience

Professor: “We're working on a clinical trial, but recruitment has been slower than expected.”  
Student: “I see. Has recruitment been the main challenge?”  
Professor: “Yes, especially because our patient population is quite specific.”  
Student: “That makes sense. Eligibility criteria can complicate recruitment.”  
Professor: “Exactly. We have to balance rigor with feasibility.”  
Student: “That's an interesting trade-off. I've encountered feasibility issues in observational research, though I haven't worked directly on trials.”  
Professor: “What kind of issues?”  
Student: “Missing information, sample-size limitations, and how reliably some variables can be measured.”  
Professor: “Right, those are important.”  
Student: “I'd be interested in learning how similar constraints are handled during trial design.”

The student identifies a genuine parallel and clearly distinguishes previous experience from future learning goals.

#### H3. Responding to an enthusiastic professor at length

Professor: “We've started working with another department. Their methodological background is quite different, so there have been disagreements about approaches. But I think the discussions are helping improve the study.”  
Student: “That sounds like a valuable collaboration, especially with the different perspectives.”  
Professor: “It can take time to get everyone on the same page.”  
Student: “I can imagine. How do you usually work through those differences?”  
Professor: “We discuss the assumptions and why people prefer one method.”  
Student: “That makes sense. So the disagreement is really about the reasoning behind the methods, rather than just picking an approach.”  
Professor: “Exactly!”  
Student: “That's the kind of research environment I find appealing. I enjoy discussing different ways of approaching a question.”

The student acknowledges, asks, paraphrases, gets confirmation, and makes a relevant personal connection.

### J. Listening with a purpose

As you listen, ask:

1. **Main point:** What matters most in what the person has said?
2. **Conversational intention:** Are they sharing, explaining, asking, correcting, or transitioning?
3. **Next useful move:** Should you acknowledge, paraphrase, ask, connect, or leave them room to continue?

**Interactional competence（互动能力）** is the capacity to build meaning collaboratively, respond to unexpected developments, and maintain a natural conversational rhythm.
