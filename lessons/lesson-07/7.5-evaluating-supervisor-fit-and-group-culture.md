# Lesson 07 · Expressing Expectations & Negotiating

## 7.5 Evaluating Supervisor Fit and Group Culture

**Learning objectives:** Evaluate whether a research group would suit your learning goals and working style; ask concrete questions about supervision and communication culture; request opportunities to talk with current students; interpret vague answers cautiously; distinguish research-topic fit from interpersonal and practical fit; and keep conclusions provisional until verified.

**Scope:** The purpose is **two-way evaluation**. You are assessing a prospective working relationship as well as presenting yourself as a candidate. We are learning how to seek reliable information and communicate an informed decision, without assuming a brief interview reveals everything about a person or group.

### A. “Good fit” is multidimensional

A project can be scientifically fascinating and still be a poor match for your training needs or working conditions.

| Kind of fit | What to examine | Question |
|---|---|---|
| Scientific | Topics, questions, methods | “What questions is the group prioritizing?” |
| Training | Opportunity to gain relevant skills | “What skills do students typically develop?” |
| Supervisory | Guidance, autonomy and feedback | “How is feedback usually provided?” |
| Interpersonal | Communication and response to disagreement | “How does the group handle different scientific opinions?” |
| Operational | Workload, data access, resources | “What would the student's responsibilities be?” |
| Development | Opportunities for presentations, writing and careers | “What paths have recent students taken?” |
| Structural | Degree rules, funding and admission | “Which arrangements are formally confirmed?” |

#### A1. Interviewing the supervisor is legitimate

You can say:

> “I'd also like to understand the working environment so I can judge whether the program and group would be a good fit for my training goals.”

This is constructive and transparent.

#### A2. The “fit” vocabulary

- **A good fit for me** — Appropriate to my interests and needs.
- **A good fit with my goals** — Correspondence with aims.
- **Align with my priorities** — Shared direction.
- **Work well with someone** — Compatible ways of collaborating.
- **Be comfortable with an arrangement** — Practical or interpersonal acceptability.
- **Have reservations about** — Hold specific concerns.

“Perfect fit” is usually premature during initial exploration.

### B. Asking about supervision culture rather than seeking slogans

#### B1. Convert broad claims into examples

Professor: “Our group is very supportive.”  
Student: “That's good to hear. Could you give me an example of how students receive support when a project becomes challenging?”

Professor: “We give students a lot of freedom.”  
Student: “How are major research decisions reviewed, particularly when a student is still getting familiar with the project?”

#### B2. What to ask about feedback and disagreement

- “How do you normally give constructive feedback on an analysis or draft?”
- “If a student disagrees with an analytical choice, how is that usually discussed?”
- “Would students be encouraged to raise methodological concerns in group meetings?”
- “How are disagreements about the scope of a project usually resolved?”
- “Who could a student approach if they needed advice beyond the immediate project team?”

These focus on **process**, not ungrounded character judgments.

#### B3. “Micromanage,” “hands-off,” “self-directed”

| Term | Nuance | Better neutral question |
|---|---|---|
| **Micromanage** | Excessively control details; critical | “How much day-to-day guidance is typical?” |
| **Hands-off** | Limited direct involvement; can be good or bad | “How frequently do students receive individual feedback?” |
| **Self-directed** | Independently set/complete tasks | “Which decisions are students expected to make independently?” |
| **Collaborative** | People work together; can be vague | “Can you give an example of how projects are shared?” |
| **Supportive** | Provides help; subjective | “What support is available when someone gets stuck?” |

Avoid labeling a professor “micromanaging” in an introductory meeting. Use observable questions to understand the practice.

### C. Learning how the research group operates

#### C1. Group structure

- “How many students and researchers are currently involved in the group?”
- “Do students mainly work on individual thesis projects or larger collaborative studies?”
- “How do people share analytical or project-management responsibilities?”
- “Are research meetings mostly used for progress updates, paper discussions or problem-solving?”
- “Would new students have opportunities to learn from postdocs or senior students?”

#### C2. Collaboration and credit

- “How are contributions to joint projects usually documented?”
- “What happens when a study becomes larger than the original thesis scope?”
- “How do you decide when a student's work is ready to be presented externally?”
- “Are students encouraged to contribute their own questions or interpretations?”

Detailed authorship policy belongs in 7.2; here the focus is how the team actually interacts.

#### C3. Work rhythm and flexibility

- “How are group meetings usually scheduled?”
- “Is work mainly organized around deadlines and milestones?”
- “How does the group handle periods when data collection or analysis is delayed?”
- “Are there times of year when the workload tends to be particularly heavy?”

These questions help identify whether the typical workflow matches your circumstances.

### D. Speaking with current students or alumni

Talking with students is often a useful way to learn how supervision works in practice. It does not guarantee an objective or comprehensive picture: individual experiences can differ, and students may feel constrained in what they can say.

#### D1. Asking the professor for an introduction

- “Would it be possible to speak with one of your current students about their experience in the group?”
- “If appropriate, could you put me in touch with a current research student?”
- “I'd appreciate the chance to hear how the program works from a student's perspective.”
- “Is there someone in the group who might be willing to answer a few general questions?”

**“Put me in touch with”** = connect two people so they can communicate. It doesn't require the professor to disclose someone's contact details without permission.

#### D2. What to ask students

- “What did your first few months in the group look like?”
- “How often do you receive feedback, and what form does it take?”
- “When you run into a problem, who do you usually speak to?”
- “How much flexibility do you have in shaping your project?”
- “How are project deadlines and workload discussed?”
- “What has surprised you about the program?”
- “What do you wish you'd known before joining?”
- “If you were choosing again, what questions would you ask?”

#### D3. Avoid loaded questions

**Loaded:** “Is your supervisor controlling and unpleasant?”  
**Better:** “How would you describe the supervisor's approach to feedback and independence?”

**Loaded:** “Are all the students unhappy?”  
**Better:** “What aspects of the working environment have been most challenging?”

A neutral question gives someone room to describe experiences in their own terms.

#### D4. Confidentiality and pressure

Do not ask students to reveal private complaints, unpublished data or other people's personal information. They may decline to answer a question; respect that. “No pressure at all if you'd rather not discuss that” is a useful expression, though it shouldn't be overused or made to sound scripted.

### E. Triangulating information rather than relying on one impression

**Triangulate（交叉核实）** = use more than one source or observation to evaluate a question.

Possible sources:

1. Official program documentation (degree rules, funding, milestones).
2. The prospective supervisor's explanation (intended arrangements).
3. Current students' experiences (everyday practice).
4. Recent alumni's experiences (progression and outcomes).
5. Public research outputs and project descriptions (observable work).

#### E1. Questions to compare consistently

| Question | Supervisor account | Student account | Official source |
|---|---|---|---|
| Frequency of meetings | Typical practice | Actual experience | Sometimes specified |
| Project autonomy | Intended expectations | How decisions happen | Rarely detailed |
| Degree milestones | Project timeline | Practical bottlenecks | Formal requirements |
| Funding | Potential support | Past arrangements | Authoritative conditions |
| Publication role | Expected opportunities | Contribution process | Relevant institutional/journal policy |

A difference between accounts may reflect changing circumstances or roles. Ask for clarification before assuming intentional deception.

#### E2. Useful verification language

- “I heard slightly different descriptions of how that works. Could I check whether the process varies by project?”
- “Is that the usual arrangement, or does it depend on the student's stage?”
- “Could you point me to the written program guidance on that?”
- “Would that be confirmed before the student accepts an offer?”

### F. Positive signs, concerns and uncertain evidence

These are **questions to investigate**, not a diagnostic test of someone's character.

#### F1. Potentially reassuring patterns

- Describes a clear process for asking questions and obtaining feedback.
- Can explain how responsibilities and authorship discussions are handled.
- Distinguishes formal promises from possibilities.
- Is willing to clarify milestones and appropriate expectations.
- Makes reasonable space for questions and alternative interpretations.
- Can point you to official program information.

#### F2. Questions worth investigating further

- Repeatedly vague answers about required work or supervision.
- Expectations that shift sharply without clarifying workload or trade-offs.
- Apparent pressure to commit before seeing program or funding details.
- Claims of guaranteed publication or admission without formal authority.
- Dismissal of reasonable questions about supervision or working conditions.
- Conflicting information about degree requirements, data access or responsibilities.

One awkward phrase or a rushed meeting is not enough for a reliable conclusion. Look for repeated behavior, context and confirmation.

#### F3. “Red flag”

*Red flag* means a warning sign. It is common informal language, including professional conversation, but usually best kept for personal notes or trusted discussions rather than saying to a potential supervisor: “That's a red flag.” In the meeting, describe the concrete issue:

> “I want to make sure I understand the expectations, because the workload still seems quite open-ended.”

### G. Expressing an assessment without overclaiming

#### G1. Favorable but provisional

- “The project sounds closely aligned with what I'd like to develop.”
- “The mix of independent work and group discussion appeals to me.”
- “Based on what we've discussed, this seems like a promising direction.”

#### G2. Mixed feelings

- “I'm interested in the topic, but I still have questions about the supervision arrangements.”
- “The project looks relevant, although I would need more clarity on the expected role.”
- “There are aspects that appeal to me, and a few I'd like to understand better.”

#### G3. Respectful recognition of a mismatch

> “I appreciate the opportunity to learn about the group. After considering the project structure and my training goals, I'm not sure it would be the right match for me.”

You are allowed to decide that an opportunity does not suit you.

#### G4. “Seems,” “sounds,” and “is”

- “It **sounds** supportive.” — Based on what you've heard.
- “It **seems** supportive.” — Tentative impression from information.
- “It **is** supportive.” — Strong factual characterization; harder to justify after a brief meeting.

Use epistemic caution（认识上的谨慎）when evidence is limited.

### H. Realistic and uncomfortable scenarios

#### Scenario 1 — The professor says “We're like a family”

Professor: “We're really like a family here.”  
Student: “That's interesting. What does that mean for how people work together—for example, how feedback and workload are handled?”

The phrase can mean many different things. Ask for concrete practices.

#### Scenario 2 — A professor has little time for individual meetings

Professor: “I rarely meet students one-on-one.”  
Student: “Thanks for explaining. How do students receive feedback on decisions that are too detailed for a group meeting?”

#### Scenario 3 — You hear a negative account from one student

Student A: “I found the feedback process difficult.”  
You: “Thank you for sharing that. If you're comfortable, could you explain what made it difficult in practice? I understand experiences may vary.”

Avoid turning a single account into a definitive public claim.

#### Scenario 4 — The professor discourages student contact

Professor: “There's no need to talk to current students.”  
Student: “I understand. Hearing from students would help me assess the training environment, so I'd still value a chance to speak with someone if that's possible.”

Their response can inform your own judgment without proving any hidden motive.

#### Scenario 5 — Research fit is excellent, supervision fit uncertain

Professor: “Our projects closely match your interests.”  
Student: “They certainly sound relevant. Could I also ask how students typically receive guidance during the early stages?”

#### Scenario 6 — Alumni outcomes are unclear

Professor: “Several students have gone on to research careers.”  
Student: “That's useful to know. Would you be able to share the kinds of roles recent graduates have taken, if that information is public or appropriate to share?”

#### Scenario 7 — Supervisor asks whether you have doubts

Professor: “Do you have any concerns about the group?”  
Student: “I'd like to understand the feedback process in more detail. I've learned that clear communication is especially important to me when a project is taking shape.”

#### Scenario 8 — Personal preferences are dismissed

Professor: “Students here just have to adapt. We don't discuss individual preferences.”  
Student: “I understand that there are established group expectations. I'd still need to know the core responsibilities and feedback arrangements before deciding whether I could meet them.”

#### Scenario 9 — Different students describe different experiences

You: “I've heard that some students meet weekly and others less often. Does the frequency depend on project stage or supervisor availability?”

#### Scenario 10 — The group seems unsuitable

Professor: “What would you like to do next?”  
Student: “Thank you for taking the time to explain the group. I need to consider whether this environment matches the training and supervision I'd be looking for, so I won't make a decision today.”

### I. Extended dialogue: evaluating the relationship as well as the research

**Professor:** “Our group has several projects that could fit your background.”  
**Student:** “That's encouraging. Could I ask a few questions about how the group works?”  
**Professor:** “Of course.”  
**Student:** “How much independence do students typically have in developing their analyses?”  
**Professor:** “Quite a lot. I prefer students to bring me a proposal rather than wait for instructions.”  
**Student:** “That could be a good learning experience. How are proposals usually reviewed before a student starts the analysis?”  
**Professor:** “We discuss them in group meetings, and sometimes with a postdoc.”  
**Student:** “I see. And if students have a different interpretation of a result, is there a process for discussing it?”  
**Professor:** “Yes, we often compare different explanations and approaches.”  
**Student:** “That's useful to know. Could you give me an example of how one of those discussions typically works?”  
**Professor:** “A student might bring two competing models, and the group examines the assumptions.”  
**Student:** “That sounds relevant to the way I'd like to develop. I'd also be interested in hearing from a current student about their experience. Would it be possible to put me in touch with someone who's willing to speak?”  
**Professor:** “I can ask whether one of our students is available.”  
**Student:** “Thank you, I'd appreciate that. I'd like to understand the day-to-day side of the program before reaching a final decision.”  
**Professor:** “That's sensible. Do you have any concerns so far?”  
**Student:** “The research interests seem well aligned. The main point I'd like to clarify is how individual feedback is handled when there's a major deadline or a complicated methodological question.”  
**Professor:** “We can talk about that in more detail after you review the current project outline.”  
**Student:** “That would be helpful. I'll read the outline and then follow up with any specific questions.”

#### I1. Language-function analysis

- **“Could I ask a few questions...?”** — Signals mutual evaluation, not interrogation.
- **“How are proposals reviewed?”** — Asks for process rather than personal slogans.
- **“Could you give me an example?”** — Makes an abstract claim testable.
- **“Put me in touch with...”** — Seeks a willing independent perspective.
- **“The main point I'd like to clarify...”** — Names a real remaining concern without accusing anyone.
- **“Before reaching a final decision”** — Avoids premature commitment.

#### I2. Branch: after hearing a mismatch

Professor: “Students generally receive no feedback until they hand in their completed thesis.”  
Student: “Thank you for clarifying. I think earlier opportunities for feedback would be important to my learning, so I'd want to think carefully about whether that arrangement would be suitable.”

### J. Practical question bank and principles

| Information needed | Question |
|---|---|
| Supervisor style | “How would you describe your approach to supervising research students?” |
| Feedback in practice | “Could you give me an example of how feedback is provided?” |
| Disagreement | “How are differences in scientific interpretation usually handled?” |
| Group interaction | “What role do group meetings play?” |
| Daily support | “Who do students normally go to when they get stuck?” |
| Workload culture | “How are expectations and milestones agreed?” |
| Student input | “Do students have opportunities to help shape research questions?” |
| Former students | “What kinds of paths have recent graduates taken?” |
| Current students | “Could you put me in touch with a current student who's willing to speak?” |
| Verify unclear point | “Does that vary across projects or students?” |
| State concern | “I still have some questions about...” |
| Defer decision | “I'd like to learn more before making a final decision.” |

**Core principles:**

1. Evaluate scientific fit, supervisory fit and practical feasibility separately.
2. Ask about **observable processes**, not merely whether people are “nice” or “supportive.”
3. Listen to both faculty and student perspectives while respecting privacy.
4. Compare accounts and check official procedures; no single meeting gives perfect information.
5. Use specific questions to investigate concerns before drawing conclusions.
6. It is appropriate to choose a different environment when the working arrangement does not suit your needs.
