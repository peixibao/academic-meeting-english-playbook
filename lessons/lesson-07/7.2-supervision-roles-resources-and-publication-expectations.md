# Lesson 07 · Expressing Expectations & Negotiating

## 7.2 Supervision, Roles, Resources, and Publication Expectations

**Learning objectives:** Discuss what supervision would look like in practice; clarify roles, responsibilities and decision-making; ask about data access and support; explore publication possibilities without assuming guarantees; respond to vague or conflicting descriptions; and identify points that require later confirmation.

**Scope:** This lesson extends 7.1 from broad training expectations to operational details. We are practicing **professional language and mutual clarification**, not assuming that any supervisor can promise admission, data access, authorship or funding. Institutional rules prevail, and formal commitments need appropriate confirmation.

### A. What does “supervision” actually include?

A useful conversation distinguishes at least six aspects:

1. **Scientific direction:** selecting questions, designs, analytical strategies and interpretations.
2. **Day-to-day guidance:** who answers practical questions and helps you troubleshoot.
3. **Feedback:** when and how drafts, code and results are reviewed.
4. **Accountability:** milestones, updates and who is responsible for each deliverable.
5. **Resources:** data, software, training, ethics approval and access to collaborators.
6. **Outputs:** thesis, presentation, report, poster, manuscript, or other deliverables.

Useful opening:

> “Could I ask how supervision would work on a typical research project in your group?”

#### A1. Supervisor, mentor, advisor and project lead

- **Supervisor / advisor:** Academic oversight; terminology and formal duties differ by institution.
- **Mentor:** Developmental guidance, possibly from someone other than the formal supervisor.
- **Co-supervisor / co-advisor:** Additional oversight; formal status depends on the program.
- **Project lead / principal investigator (PI):** Scientific and administrative leadership for a project; may not be the person who formally supervises the degree.
- **Postdoc / doctoral student:** May provide daily guidance without holding formal responsibility for your academic progress.

Ask **who does what** rather than inferring roles from job titles.

### B. Meetings, feedback and communication expectations

#### B1. Useful questions

- “How often do you typically meet with research students?”
- “Are most discussions one-on-one, or do students mainly receive feedback in group meetings?”
- “Who would be the main point of contact for day-to-day questions?”
- “How do you usually give feedback on analysis plans or manuscript drafts?”
- “Is there an agreed process for reviewing drafts?”
- “When a student gets stuck, what is the best way to raise it?”

#### B2. “Feedback turnaround” and timing

**Turnaround time** = the time between submitting something and getting a response or completed result.

- “What is the **usual turnaround time** for feedback on a draft?”
- “How far in advance should students send materials before a meeting?”
- “Would it be reasonable to agree on approximate feedback milestones?”

A meeting is not the place to demand **“You must return everything in 24 hours.”** You can identify important deadlines: “If the thesis deadline is approaching, how is feedback usually coordinated?”

#### B3. “Check in,” “touch base,” “follow up,” “get back to”

| Phrase | Typical meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **check in** | Provide a short update | “Could we check in after the first analysis?” |
| **touch base** | Have a brief update/discussion | “We could touch base next week.” |
| **follow up on** | Continue a prior issue | “I'd like to follow up on your feedback.” |
| **get back to someone** | Respond later | “I'll get back to you once I've checked.” |
| **walk through** | Explain step by step | “Could we walk through the analysis plan?” |
| **review** | Examine and comment | “Would you review the draft?” |

*Touch base* is conversational; *review the draft* describes a specific task. Don't blur a vague invitation to “touch base” into a promise of full supervision.

### C. Clarifying roles and responsibilities

#### C1. Useful verbs and role strength

| Phrase | What it suggests |
|---|---|
| “**contribute to** data cleaning” | Participation |
| “**be involved in** recruitment” | Participation, extent unspecified |
| “**take responsibility for** the analysis” | Accountability for the analysis |
| “**coordinate** data collection” | Organize people or stages |
| “**take the lead on** drafting the manuscript” | Leading a task, not automatic authorship order |
| “**assist with** literature screening” | Supporting role |
| “**oversee** the project” | Broader supervisory role |
| “**sign off on** a document” | Formally approve it in a particular process |

#### C2. Clarification requests

- “Which parts would the student be expected to handle independently?”
- “Who would make final decisions about the analysis plan?”
- “Would the student help develop the research question, or work from a predefined protocol?”
- “What would be the main deliverables during the degree?”
- “How would the responsibilities be divided between the student and other team members?”

Professor: “You'd lead the analysis.”  
Student: “Could I clarify what that would involve? Would I be developing the analysis plan, running agreed models, or both?”

#### C3. “On” vs. “for”

- “Work **on** a project” — Participate in the project.
- “Work **for** a professor” — May suggest employment or a reporting relationship; avoid implying you are an employee when discussing academic supervision.
- “Work **with** a professor” — Collaborative relation, often a natural neutral choice.

### D. Data, training and resources

#### D1. Data access and permissions

- “Would the project use an existing dataset?”
- “What approvals are required before a student could access the data?”
- “Are there restrictions on which variables students may use?”
- “Is there a standard process for data-use agreements?”
- “Would ethics approval already be in place, or would a new application be needed?”

**Important:** A professor saying “We have data” does not necessarily mean “You are already authorized to use the data.”

#### D2. Resources without presumption

- “What software or computing resources are available to students?”
- “Would there be access to statistical consultation if needed?”
- “Is there training for the data-management systems used by the group?”
- “Would there be opportunities to attend departmental seminars?”
- “If a project involves fieldwork, how is that normally organized and supported?”

#### D3. Fieldwork and bench work as distinct commitments

If a proposed project involves activities beyond your experience or preference, ask concretely:

> “Could I ask how much of the role would involve fieldwork, compared with data management and analysis?”

> “I have more experience with population-based research and data analysis. What training would be provided for the other components?”

A preference can be expressed without making assumptions about what a particular project requires.

### E. Publications, posters, theses and authorship

Research outputs are not interchangeable:

| Output | What it is | What it does **not** imply |
|---|---|---|
| **Thesis/dissertation** | Degree research document | Automatic journal acceptance |
| **Conference abstract/poster** | Conference contribution | Peer-reviewed full journal article |
| **Manuscript in preparation** | Draft being developed | Submission or acceptance |
| **Manuscript submitted/under review** | Journal process underway | Acceptance |
| **Published paper** | Article formally published | Specific individual's authorship without verification |
| **Preprint** | Public manuscript, often without journal peer review | Journal publication or acceptance |

#### E1. How to ask about publication prospects

- “Do master's thesis projects in your group sometimes lead to journal manuscripts?”
- “At what point would publication plans usually be discussed?”
- “Would there be opportunities to contribute to writing up the findings?”
- “How are publication responsibilities normally divided among team members?”
- “Would the student be expected to prepare a manuscript, or is the thesis the main required output?”

These are legitimate questions; their appropriateness often improves once you know the project and possible student role.

#### E2. How to ask about authorship

> “How do you usually discuss authorship expectations and individual contributions at the start of a project?”

> “If a student makes a substantial contribution to the analysis and manuscript, how would authorship discussions typically be handled?”

> “Would there be a way to clarify the expected contributions early and revisit them if the project changes?”

**Authorship standards depend on field, journal and institutional policy.** For biomedical journals, ICMJE's recommendations are a relevant professional reference: https://www.icmje.org/recommendations/browse/roles-and-responsibilities/defining-the-role-of-authors-and-contributors.html. A single task, such as running code, does not by itself guarantee first authorship or necessarily satisfy all criteria; discuss actual scholarly contributions and the complete applicable rules.

#### E3. “Publish” vs. “get published”

- “I'd like to **contribute to a manuscript**.” — Contribution is the goal.
- “I'd hope the project **could lead to a publication**.” — Possibility, appropriately tentative.
- “I'd like to **get a paper published**.” — Outcome-oriented, conversational.
- “I **will be first author**.” — Only say this if a properly confirmed arrangement warrants it, and remember authorship must reflect actual contributions and applicable policy.

#### E4. If the professor mentions a likely paper

Professor: “There's potential for a publication here.”  
Student: “That's encouraging. What stage is the project at, and what would the student's contribution to the manuscript involve?”

“Potential” is not “guaranteed.” Clarify the process and the student role.

### F. Milestones, changes of scope and accountability

#### F1. Useful questions

- “What would be the first concrete milestone?”
- “How would you usually handle changes to the research question?”
- “If the planned analysis proves infeasible, who would decide on an alternative?”
- “Would there be regular opportunities to revisit the timeline?”
- “What would success in the first three months look like?”

#### F2. “Deliverable,” “milestone,” “deadline,” “timeline”

- **Deliverable（交付成果）:** An output someone is expected to produce.
- **Milestone（里程碑）:** A significant checkpoint in progress.
- **Deadline（截止日期）:** The latest expected date.
- **Timeline（时间安排）:** Overall sequence and scheduling.

> “Could we agree on an initial timeline, with a few milestones for the first stage?”

This expresses a process preference, not an unverified fixed schedule.

#### F3. Changing scope

Professor: “We may add a second outcome later.”  
Student: “That could be interesting. If we expand the scope, how would we revisit the workload and thesis timeline?”

It is reasonable to discuss the effects of new tasks before accepting them.

### G. Grammar, register and careful commitments

#### G1. “Would” vs. “will”

- “**Would** I have access to the data?” — Conditional/hypothetical.
- “**Will** I have access to the data?” — More definite future assumption.
- “**Do** students generally have access?” — General policy.

#### G2. Ask about possibilities, processes and decisions separately

- Possibility: “**Could** the project lead to a publication?”
- Process: “**How** are publication decisions made?”
- Decision authority: “**Who** normally decides when a manuscript is ready to submit?”

These questions uncover different information.

#### G3. Phrases that deserve caution

| Overstrong | Better early-stage version |
|---|---|
| “Will you publish my paper?” | “How are publication opportunities usually approached?” |
| “I require first authorship.” | “How do you discuss authorship contributions and order?” |
| “You said I'd lead, so the whole project is mine.” | “Could we clarify which tasks I'd take the lead on?” |
| “Do you guarantee feedback every week?” | “How are feedback meetings typically scheduled?” |
| “I'm sure I can use the data.” | “What access and approvals would be required?” |

### H. Unexpected and potentially awkward situations

#### Scenario 1 — Daily contact is with a postdoc

Professor: “You would mainly work with our postdoc.”  
Student: “That could work well. How would responsibilities be divided between the postdoc and the formal supervisor?”

#### Scenario 2 — An urgent request conflicts with agreed milestones

Professor: “We may need help with another analysis at short notice.”  
Student: “I'd be open to discussing that. How would additional tasks be prioritized alongside the thesis milestones?”

#### Scenario 3 — “You'll be an author” is mentioned casually

Professor: “You'd probably be on the paper.”  
Student: “Thank you. Would it be possible to discuss the expected contributions and how authorship is determined once the project is defined?”

#### Scenario 4 — Feedback expectations are ambiguous

Professor: “I review drafts when I have time.”  
Student: “Understood. If a major deadline is approaching, is there a usual way students arrange review in advance?”

#### Scenario 5 — Dataset availability is uncertain

Professor: “We hope the data will be ready next term.”  
Student: “Would there be a contingency plan if access is delayed?”

#### Scenario 6 — A group project has several leads

Professor: “Two departments share the project.”  
Student: “Who would be responsible for the thesis decisions, and who would be the main contact for analysis questions?”

#### Scenario 7 — A publication opportunity is denied

Professor: “There may not be time to turn the thesis into a paper.”  
Student: “Thank you for explaining. Would the thesis still include opportunities to practice academic writing and present the findings?”

#### Scenario 8 — Supervisor asks about your own expectations of credit

Professor: “What are your expectations about authorship?”  
Student: “I'd want contributions and authorship to be discussed clearly and follow the relevant standards. I'd be interested in contributing substantially to a manuscript, if the project allows for that.”

### I. Extended dialogue: project responsibilities and outputs

**Professor:** “We have a dataset that may be suitable for a master's thesis.”  
**Student:** “That sounds promising. What would the student be responsible for in the first stage?”  
**Professor:** “Probably data cleaning, preliminary analysis and later the main models.”  
**Student:** “Would the student be involved in refining the research question and analysis plan as well?”  
**Professor:** “Yes, but I'd want to review those decisions.”  
**Student:** “That makes sense. Who would be the main point of contact while the analysis is underway?”  
**Professor:** “One of our postdocs handles day-to-day questions. We'd meet at key milestones.”  
**Student:** “Could I ask what those milestones usually are?”  
**Professor:** “An analysis plan, initial results, then a thesis draft.”  
**Student:** “I see. And would data-access approvals already be arranged for a new student?”  
**Professor:** “Not yet. Those would need to go through the research office.”  
**Student:** “Thanks for clarifying. It would be useful to know the likely timing, since that affects the project plan.”  
**Professor:** “Agreed. There might also be a publishable paper.”  
**Student:** “I'd be interested in that possibility. How do students normally become involved in manuscript preparation and authorship discussions?”  
**Professor:** “We discuss contributions once we know what the project can produce.”  
**Student:** “Understood. So the thesis would be the primary deliverable, with publication considered depending on the data, contributions and project progress. Have I got that right?”  
**Professor:** “Exactly.”  
**Student:** “That's helpful. I'd like to read more about the project and confirm the training and supervision arrangements before deciding whether it's the right fit.”

#### I1. Function analysis

- “What would the student be responsible for?” → role.
- “Who would be the main point of contact?” → guidance structure.
- “Would data-access approvals already be arranged?” → permission and dependencies.
- “How do students normally become involved?” → process, without demanding an outcome.
- “Have I got that right?” → check the distinction between firm deliverable and possibility.

### J. Reference bank and guiding principles

| Function | Phrase |
|---|---|
| Supervision style | “How do you typically supervise master's research students?” |
| Contact person | “Who would be my main point of contact?” |
| Feedback process | “How are drafts usually reviewed?” |
| Scope | “Could we clarify the scope of the student's role?” |
| Division of labor | “How would responsibilities be divided?” |
| Resources | “What approvals or resources would be needed?” |
| Timing | “What would the first major milestone be?” |
| Publication opportunity | “Could the project potentially lead to a manuscript?” |
| Authorship | “How are authorship expectations usually discussed?” |
| Contingency | “What would happen if access were delayed?” |
| Confirm | “So the main deliverable would be the thesis. Is that correct?” |

**Core principles:** Ask who, what, when and how; keep possible outputs separate from guarantees; link responsibilities to available training and realistic milestones; acknowledge collective scholarly work; respect access and confidentiality rules; record unresolved questions for follow-up.
