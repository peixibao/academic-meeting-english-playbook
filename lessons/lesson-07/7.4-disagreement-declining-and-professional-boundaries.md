# Lesson 07 · Expressing Expectations & Negotiating

## 7.4 Disagreement, Declining, and Professional Boundaries

**Learning objectives:** Express a genuine disagreement without hiding the substance; distinguish a scientific concern from a personal objection; ask for alternatives; refuse or defer a proposed responsibility; respond to sensitive questions; set boundaries firmly when necessary; and maintain an accurate record of what you have and have not agreed to.

**Scope note:** Lesson 03 covered general politeness, and Lesson 06 covered scientific questions. This section teaches the **interactive process of disagreement and negotiation** when a proposed task, expectation or interpretation may affect you. Legal rules on interview questions and employment/student rights vary by jurisdiction and context; these are language strategies, not legal determinations.

### A. What kinds of disagreement arise in academic meetings?

| Situation | Actual issue | Useful opening |
|---|---|---|
| Scientific disagreement | Model, design or interpretation | “I have a different interpretation of that result.” |
| Feasibility concern | Data, timeline, workload | “I'm concerned about whether the timeline is feasible.” |
| Preference mismatch | Topic or work type | “My preference would be for a project with a stronger analytical component.” |
| Role ambiguity | Expected tasks are unclear | “Could we clarify whether that would be part of the thesis role?” |
| Boundary | Request exceeds what you can agree to | “I wouldn't be able to commit to that arrangement.” |
| Sensitive question | Personal topic feels unrelated | “I'd prefer to focus on my relevant academic background.” |

Disagreement can lead to **clarification, revised plans, deferred decisions, or a firm refusal**. There is no requirement that every disagreement end in consensus.

#### A1. Separate three levels

1. **Concern:** “I wonder whether that approach would be feasible.”
2. **Different preference:** “I'd prefer to explore a different outcome.”
3. **Firm boundary:** “I wouldn't be able to take on that responsibility.”

Choose language that matches the actual strength of your position. Excessive softening can cause a genuine no to sound like a maybe.

### B. Polite disagreement that still communicates a view

#### B1. Professional alternatives

- “I see the reasoning behind that, although I have a concern about [X].”
- “I agree with the overall goal. I'm less certain about [SPECIFIC METHOD].”
- “I wonder whether [ALTERNATIVE] might be more appropriate.”
- “One limitation I'd want to consider is [ISSUE].”
- “I'm not entirely convinced that [CLAIM] follows from the data.”
- “I have a slightly different interpretation of the finding.”
- “Could we look at the assumptions behind that decision?”

These allow a clear scientific position without implying the other person is incompetent.

#### B2. “I see your point” vs. “I agree”

**I see your point** = I understand your reasoning. It does not necessarily mean I accept the conclusion.

**I agree with your point** = I endorse the point.

**That makes sense** often signals understanding or partial agreement, depending on context. If you mean “I understand but disagree,” make your position explicit:

> “I see your point about feasibility. I do have a concern about how the outcome is defined, though.”

#### B3. “I hear you”

“I hear you” conveys that you understand or acknowledge someone's concern. It can sound somewhat informal and, if delivered without substance, dismissive. In a first meeting, “I understand your concern” is a safe alternative.

#### B4. Phrases that can backfire

- “**With all due respect...**” — Often signals that a harsh disagreement follows; avoid as a default.
- “**Obviously, that's wrong.**” — Makes a personal judgment rather than explaining evidence.
- “**I beg to differ.**” — Grammatically fine but can sound pointed or theatrical in ordinary academic conversation.
- “**No offense, but...**” — Frequently forecasts offense; usually unnecessary.

### C. Giving a reason and proposing an alternative

A practical response pattern:

**Acknowledge → identify issue → explain implication → suggest next step.**

Professor: “Let's add six more variables to the model.”  
Student: “I see why those variables might be relevant. My concern is the small number of outcome events, which could make the estimates unstable. Could we first review the sample and the planned adjustment strategy?”

Note that a statistical concern is a **hypothesis about model adequacy**, not an assertion that an analysis must be wrong; the specifics depend on the data and model.

#### C1. Conditional disagreement

- “**If** the sample size is limited, we might need to simplify the model.”
- “**Given** the timeline, that may be difficult to complete.”
- “**Unless** additional data become available, I'd be cautious about that analysis.”
- “**Provided that** the data access is confirmed, the plan seems feasible.”

#### C2. “What if we...?” vs. “Could we...?”

- “**What if we** started with a simpler analysis?” — Exploratory suggestion.
- “**Could we** start with a simpler analysis?” — Polite proposal/request.
- “**I propose that we** start with...” — More assertive and formal.

#### C3. Trade-offs（权衡）

- “The trade-off would be greater detail at the cost of a longer timeline.”
- “That might improve precision, but it would also require more data.”
- “One option is [A]. Another is [B]. Which constraint matters most for this project?”

Avoid claiming a particular trade-off exists before checking the context.

### D. Saying no without creating ambiguity

#### D1. The ladder of refusal

| Strength | Expression | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| Tentative | “I'm not sure that would be the best fit for me.” | Reservation |
| Conditional | “I'd consider it if the scope were narrower.” | Possible with changes |
| Not ready | “I'd need more information before deciding.” | No present commitment |
| Clear refusal | “I wouldn't be able to take that on.” | Cannot agree |
| Explicit decline | “Thank you for the opportunity, but I'll have to decline.” | Decision made |

#### D2. Declining a proposed project

> “Thank you for explaining the project. After considering its requirements, I don't think it aligns closely enough with the kind of research training I'm seeking. I appreciate the opportunity to discuss it.”

You do not owe a fabricated alternative justification. A brief, genuine explanation is sufficient.

#### D3. Refusing a task you cannot do well

> “I wouldn't want to overpromise. I could help with [TASK I CAN DO], but I wouldn't be able to take responsibility for [TASK] within that timeline.”

That sets a clear limit and possibly offers a workable alternative.

#### D4. “Can't,” “won't,” and “wouldn't be able to”

- “I **can't** do that.” — Direct inability.
- “I **won't** do that.” — Firm decision; sometimes confrontational.
- “I **wouldn't be able to** do that.” — Polite conditional refusal.
- “That **wouldn't be feasible for me**.” — Emphasizes practical constraints.

Use directness when needed; politeness should not erase your decision.

### E. Deferring a decision and preserving options

#### E1. Useful phrases

- “I'd like a little time to think it through.”
- “Could I get back to you once I've reviewed the project outline?”
- “I'd prefer not to make a commitment before I've seen the full requirements.”
- “Would it be all right if I followed up after checking the program details?”
- “Could we revisit that question after [RELEVANT INFORMATION] is available?”

#### E2. “Think about” vs. “think through”

**Think about** = consider something generally. **Think through** = work through consequences carefully.

- “I need to **think about** the offer.”
- “I'd like to **think through** the workload and supervision arrangements.”

#### E3. “Get back to” vs. “come back to”

- “I'll **get back to you** on that.” — I'll respond later.
- “Can we **come back to that**?” — Can we revisit the topic in this or a later discussion?

#### E4. Deadline pressure

Professor: “Could you decide by tonight?”  
Student: “I understand that you're working to a tight timeline. I wouldn't be comfortable making a decision before reviewing the project details and funding conditions. Is there any flexibility on the deadline?”

A request for flexibility does not imply you will necessarily receive it.

### F. Clarifying changing expectations and preventing scope creep

**Scope creep（工作范围不断扩大）** describes gradual additions that change the original commitment.

Professor: “We'd also like you to coordinate recruitment at two sites.”  
Student: “That sounds like a substantial additional responsibility. Could we discuss how it would affect the original project milestones and which tasks would take priority?”

#### F1. Useful language

- “My understanding was that the original role focused on [X].”
- “Has the scope of the project changed?”
- “Which responsibilities would be essential, and which would be optional?”
- “If we add [TASK], what could be adjusted elsewhere?”
- “Could we confirm the revised expectations in writing afterward?”

#### F2. Correct a misunderstanding without accusation

- “I may not have explained my position clearly.”
- “I should clarify what I meant by [WORD].”
- “I was under the impression that [X]. Has that changed?”
- “I think we may be using [TERM] in different ways.”

Do not say “As I already told you...” unless a firmer clarification is necessary; it can suggest irritation.

### G. Sensitive or overly personal questions

The aim is to answer relevant academic questions while keeping appropriate control over personal information.

#### G1. Neutral redirection

- “I'd prefer to focus on my academic preparation and research experience.”
- “Could I ask how that relates to the requirements of the role?”
- “I'm happy to discuss my availability and whether I can meet the program's requirements.”
- “I'd rather not go into personal details, but I can explain my relevant experience.”

These are options; use judgment about the question, context, relationship and safety.

#### G2. If the professor asks about future personal plans

Professor: “Do you have any family plans that would affect your studies?”  
Student: “I'd prefer to keep my personal plans private. I can confirm that I'm evaluating the program's schedule and would want to meet the agreed academic requirements.”

No universal claim is made here about what questions are lawful; formal guidance depends on the jurisdiction and whether this is admission, employment or another process.

#### G3. Requests that raise an integrity concern

Professor: “Could you make the results look a little stronger?”  
Student: “Could you clarify what changes you have in mind? I wouldn't be comfortable changing results in a way that misrepresents the analysis. I'm happy to work on clearer presentation or properly justified sensitivity analyses.”

A request may be ambiguous. Clarify the intended action; protect the accuracy of research reporting.

#### G4. Requests for private or restricted materials

> “I'm not able to share those data outside the approved team, but I can explain the methods or provide publicly available information.”

Be accurate about actual approvals and confidentiality responsibilities.

### H. Difficult scenarios and flexible replies

#### Scenario 1 — Professor disagrees with your interpretation

Professor: “I don't think the result means what you're suggesting.”  
Student: “Thanks for pointing that out. Could you explain which assumption or part of the interpretation concerns you? My understanding was [SPECIFIC CLAIM], but I'm open to revising it.”

#### Scenario 2 — A proposed role has almost no research component

Professor: “You would mainly do administrative coordination.”  
Student: “Thanks for explaining. I'm looking for a role with substantive research training. Would there be opportunities to participate in analysis or interpretation, or is the role primarily administrative?”

#### Scenario 3 — You cannot accept the project's methods

Professor: “Most of the work would be bench experiments.”  
Student: “I appreciate you explaining that. My training and current interests are more closely aligned with population-based research. I don't think I'd be the strongest fit for a predominantly bench-based project.”

#### Scenario 4 — They assume you have agreed

Professor: “Great, I'll put you down for the project.”  
Student: “I'm glad we've had the chance to discuss it. I should clarify that I'm still considering the project and haven't committed yet. Could I follow up after reviewing the full details?”

#### Scenario 5 — You are repeatedly interrupted

Professor: “But you can handle all that, right?”  
Student: “Could I finish the point I was making? I have experience with [X], but I'd need additional training for [Y].”

#### Scenario 6 — A sensitive question is pressed

Professor: “I'd really like to know your personal situation.”  
Student: “I understand. I'd prefer not to discuss private circumstances. I'm happy to clarify any academic or scheduling requirements that would affect the project.”

#### Scenario 7 — Supervisor refuses a reasonable request

Professor: “We can't offer individual meetings every week.”  
Student: “Understood. Could we explore another way to obtain feedback at key project milestones?”

#### Scenario 8 — An unrealistic deadline is proposed

Professor: “Could you finish the full analysis by Friday?”  
Student: “I could complete the preliminary checks by Friday, but I wouldn't be able to validate and document the full analysis to a reliable standard by then. Would a staged delivery work?”

#### Scenario 9 — You need to end an unsuitable discussion respectfully

Professor: “So, shall we move ahead?”  
Student: “Thank you for discussing it with me. After hearing more about the role, I don't think it's the right fit for what I'm seeking, so I won't pursue this particular opportunity.”

### I. Extended dialogue: disagreement and a realistic boundary

**Professor:** “For the thesis, I'd like you to begin with our existing dataset and also organize participant recruitment for another study.”  
**Student:** “Thanks for explaining. Could I check whether both responsibilities would be part of the thesis requirements?”  
**Professor:** “The recruitment work would support the group, although it wouldn't directly contribute to your thesis.”  
**Student:** “I understand the value of supporting the group. My concern is whether taking on both responsibilities would leave enough time for the research and analysis.”  
**Professor:** “Our students usually help wherever the group needs them.”  
**Student:** “I see. What would be the expected time commitment, and could the tasks be prioritized against the thesis milestones?”  
**Professor:** “It might be quite demanding at times.”  
**Student:** “Thank you for being clear. I'm interested in the analysis project, but I'd need a better-defined workload before committing to the additional coordination.”  
**Professor:** “Would you refuse any recruitment work?”  
**Student:** “I'd be open to discussing occasional work that fits the project schedule. I wouldn't be able to promise open-ended recruitment responsibilities without knowing their scope.”  
**Professor:** “That's fair. We could identify a more specific task.”  
**Student:** “That would help. Could we outline the core thesis responsibilities first, then consider what additional work is feasible?”  
**Professor:** “Yes, let's do that.”  
**Student:** “Great. Once we've clarified the scope, I can make a more informed decision.”

#### I1. Analysis

The student distinguishes required thesis work from additional tasks, asks about hours and milestones, offers bounded flexibility and clearly refuses an open-ended obligation. No part of the dialogue guarantees that agreement will be reached.

#### I2. Branch: the supervisor will not change the arrangement

Professor: “The recruitment commitment would be non-negotiable.”  
Student: “Thank you for explaining. Given the scope and my research-training priorities, I don't think I could commit to that arrangement. I appreciate your time and the discussion.”

### J. Phrase bank and principles

| Function | English |
|---|---|
| Acknowledge | “I see the reasoning behind that.” |
| State a concern | “My concern is that...” |
| Give a different view | “I have a slightly different interpretation.” |
| Suggest an alternative | “Could we consider...?” |
| Express trade-off | “The trade-off would be...” |
| Ask for clarity | “What would that involve in practice?” |
| Clarify a misunderstanding | “I should clarify what I meant.” |
| Delay a decision | “I'd like to think that through before committing.” |
| Decline | “I wouldn't be able to take that on.” |
| Set a boundary | “I'd prefer to keep that private.” |
| Correct false assumption | “I haven't agreed to that arrangement yet.” |
| Request documentation | “Could we confirm the revised scope in writing?” |

**Core principles:** Clear disagreement can be respectful. Match the strength of the wording to your actual position. Explain evidence and consequences when useful. Keep essential conditions, preferences and commitments separate. If a proposed arrangement remains unsuitable, an explicit no is a legitimate outcome. Verify rights and institutional procedures where relevant.
