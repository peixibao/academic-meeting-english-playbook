# Lesson 07 · Expressing Expectations & Negotiating

## 7.1 Expressing Training Needs and Mutual Expectations

**Learning objectives:** Describe the research training you hope to gain; ask what a potential supervisor expects; distinguish preferences, needs, expectations and requirements; discuss independence and support without presenting yourself as either helpless or unrealistically self-sufficient; check whether apparent agreement really exists.

**Where this fits:** Lesson 03.4 introduced the basic language of expectations. Lesson 06 introduced scientific and program fit. Here we use those foundations to **negotiate a realistic working arrangement**. Exact degree rules, employment status and funding are institution-specific; verify them rather than treating examples as promises.

### A. What is being negotiated?

A prospective-supervisor meeting may involve several levels of expectation:

| Dimension | You may want to know | The supervisor may want to know |
|---|---|---|
| Training | Which skills can I develop? | Which skills do you already have? |
| Independence | Can I help shape questions? | Can you make progress without constant direction? |
| Guidance | How is feedback provided? | Can you use feedback productively? |
| Workload | What is realistically expected? | Can you meet agreed responsibilities? |
| Research scope | Is this an appropriate project? | Can the available data and time support it? |
| Development | What will I learn over the degree? | What milestones would show progress? |

**Useful opening:** “I'd be interested in understanding what the training would look like in practice.”

#### A1. Informal conversation vs. negotiation

You are not necessarily negotiating an offer. Early-stage language usually explores **possibilities**:

- “What does the first semester usually look like for a new research student?”
- “How much independence would students typically have?”
- “Would there be opportunities to develop [SKILL]?”

When an actual role or project is being considered, the language can become more concrete:

- “Before deciding, I'd like to clarify the expected scope of the project.”
- “Could we discuss how the training and project responsibilities would be balanced?”

### B. Hope, want, prefer, need, require and expect

These words carry different levels of commitment or force.

| Expression | Typical implication | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **I hope to...** | Desired outcome | “I hope to become more independent in analysis.” |
| **I'd like to...** | Clear, courteous goal | “I'd like to gain more experience with study design.” |
| **I'm looking for...** | A feature you are actively seeking | “I'm looking for substantial involvement in a research project.” |
| **I'd prefer...** | Preference, potentially flexible | “I'd prefer a project involving population-based data.” |
| **I'd need...** | A condition important to proceeding | “I'd need to understand the data-access arrangements before committing.” |
| **I would require...** | Strong or formal condition | “I would require confirmation that the project can be completed remotely.” |
| **I expect...** | Anticipation, or what you believe should happen | “I expect to graduate next year”; “I would expect regular feedback.” |

**Pragmatics（语用学）:** “I expect the supervisor to respond within a day” may sound like a demand. “How is feedback normally handled?” is a better opening when you do not know the group's practices.

#### B1. Need vs. want

“I'd like opportunities to present my work” expresses a preference. “I would need access to the necessary dataset” may describe a practical prerequisite. Choose the stronger wording only when you mean it.

#### B2. “Ideally” and “if possible”

- “Ideally, I'd like to be involved in developing the research question.”
- “If possible, I'd also appreciate opportunities to observe group meetings.”
- “My preference would be to build on my existing analytical experience.”

“Ideally” leaves room for alternatives. It does **not** automatically make an unrealistic request reasonable; context still matters.

### C. Describing training goals with evidence

A useful four-part response is **experience → current limit → desired training → possible application**.

> “I've had some hands-on experience analyzing observational data. I'd like to develop a stronger understanding of study design and learn how research questions are refined. Ideally, I'd be able to apply those skills through a defined project.”

This is a **model answer**, not a claim about your actual record. Substitute only experience you can substantiate.

#### C1. Training verbs and their objects

| Verb phrase | Example | Nuance |
|---|---|---|
| **gain experience in + noun/-ing** | “gain experience in drafting protocols” | Practical exposure |
| **develop skills in + noun/-ing** | “develop skills in interpreting models” | Skill development |
| **strengthen my understanding of** | “strengthen my understanding of causal reasoning” | Conceptual depth |
| **become more comfortable with** | “become more comfortable with programming workflows” | Confidence from practice |
| **get more exposure to** | “get more exposure to longitudinal research” | See and participate; not necessarily mastery |
| **build on** | “build on my existing analytical experience” | Extend an existing base |
| **take ownership of** | “take ownership of a defined analysis” | Accountability for a bounded task |

Compare **learn a method** (understand/use it), **apply a method** (use it on data), and **develop a new method** (create methodological research). Do not imply the third if you mean the first two.

#### C2. Asking what training is genuinely available

- “What training do new students typically receive before starting the project?”
- “Is training mostly provided through coursework, supervision, or project work?”
- “Would there be opportunities to attend seminars or methodological workshops?”
- “How do students usually develop the skills they need for a project?”
- “Could you tell me what skills students tend to gain by the end of the degree?”

### D. Independence and support: a continuum

A supervisor may ask, **“How independently do you like to work?”** A credible answer separates tasks you can handle from decisions that require guidance.

> “I'm comfortable working independently on clearly defined analytical tasks. For broader decisions—such as refining the study question or choosing between substantially different designs—I value discussion and feedback.”

#### D1. Degrees of autonomy

- **Work independently:** perform tasks without continuous guidance.
- **Work autonomously:** exercise broader discretion; may imply higher-level decision-making.
- **Work with minimal supervision:** potentially little oversight; use cautiously.
- **Take initiative:** identify and advance useful tasks without always being prompted.
- **Check in:** briefly update someone on progress.
- **Run something by you:** informally get another person's opinion before proceeding.

“I'd like to **run the analysis plan by you** before finalizing it” = discuss it for feedback; casual-professional.

#### D2. When the supervisor values independence

Professor: “Our students need to be pretty self-directed.”  
Student: “That appeals to me. Could you tell me what self-directed work looks like in your group, especially at the beginning of a project?”

A useful response shows interest **and** seeks a concrete definition.

#### D3. When you want more initial guidance

> “I learn well by taking responsibility for a task, but I think clear initial expectations and periodic feedback would help me become independent more quickly.”

This does not require pretending you need constant instruction.

### E. Asking about expectations, workload and milestones

#### E1. The basic questions

- “What would you typically expect from a first-year research student?”
- “What would the main priorities be during the first few months?”
- “How is progress usually assessed?”
- “Would there be agreed milestones for the project?”
- “How much of the student's time would be devoted to the thesis project?”
- “Are there any regular group meetings or other activities students are expected to attend?”

These ask about **usual practice**, not a guaranteed schedule.

#### E2. Clarifying ambiguous adjectives

Professor: “We expect students to be very proactive.”  
Student: “That makes sense. Could you give me an example of what proactive involvement would look like during the first few months?”

Professor: “The project moves quickly.”  
Student: “What are the major milestones, and how much flexibility is there if an analysis takes longer than expected?”

Words such as *proactive*, *independent*, *available*, *fast-paced* and *flexible* are invitations to ask about **observable behavior**.

#### E3. Effort, time and deliverables

- “Would there be a fixed weekly schedule, or is work organized around milestones?”
- “What kinds of deliverables would be expected at each stage?”
- “If the scope changes, how would we normally revisit the timeline?”

Avoid inventing a program's expected weekly hours; check its official requirements.

### F. Expressing alignment without making a commitment

#### F1. Levels of response

| Response | Commitment level |
|---|---|
| “That sounds interesting.” | Interest only |
| “I'd be interested in exploring that further.” | Willing to discuss |
| “That seems broadly aligned with what I'm looking for.” | Provisional assessment |
| “I'd need a clearer idea of the project scope before deciding.” | Explicit reservation |
| “That arrangement would work for me.” | Acceptance of the arrangement described; use when informed |
| “I'm happy to commit to that.” | Stronger commitment; use only when intended |

#### F2. “Align with,” “match,” “fit,” “suit”

- “The project **aligns with** my interests.” — Shared direction.
- “The workload **would suit** my schedule.” — Practical compatibility.
- “The training **matches** what I'm looking for.” — Close correspondence.
- “It **sounds like a good fit**.” — Overall impression, potentially tentative.

“I'd like to learn more before drawing a conclusion about fit” is entirely reasonable in an exploratory meeting.

### G. Grammar and natural phrasing

#### G1. “Would” for hypothetical arrangements

- “**Would** students have access to the dataset?” — Ask about a possible arrangement.
- “**Do** students have access to the dataset?” — Ask about general/current policy.
- “**Will** I have access to the dataset?” — Suggests a specific expected future arrangement.

In a first conversation, **would** and **typically** often avoid premature assumptions.

#### G2. “Be expected to”

“Would students **be expected to attend** weekly meetings?” = passive description of a requirement. “Would you expect me to attend?” addresses the supervisor personally. Both can be appropriate.

#### G3. Embedded questions（间接疑问句）

- “Could you explain **how feedback is usually provided**?” ✅
- “Could you explain **how is feedback usually provided**?” ❌
- “I was wondering **whether students usually develop their own questions**.” ✅

#### G4. “Responsible for” vs. “involved in”

- “Would I be **responsible for** recruitment?” — Ownership/accountability.
- “Would I be **involved in** recruitment?” — Some participation; degree unclear.
- “Would I **take the lead on** recruitment?” — Leadership over that task.

### H. Unexpected situations and branching responses

#### Scenario 1 — They ask what you need from supervision

Professor: “What would you need from a supervisor?”  
Student: “I value clear feedback, particularly when defining a project and making major analytical decisions. Once the task is agreed, I'm comfortable working independently and checking in as needed.”  
Professor: “What counts as clear feedback to you?”  
Student: “Specific comments on the reasoning or next steps are especially helpful. I'd be happy to adapt to the group's usual process.”

#### Scenario 2 — They expect much greater independence

Professor: “I don't usually give students step-by-step instructions.”  
Student: “I understand. How are research questions and major decisions usually discussed, particularly when a student is new to the project?”

#### Scenario 3 — The available project is narrower than expected

Professor: “The student would mainly clean an existing dataset.”  
Student: “That could be a useful starting point. Would there also be an opportunity to contribute to the analysis or interpretation later in the project?”

#### Scenario 4 — The professor assumes methodological expertise

Professor: “So you're already proficient in causal inference?”  
Student: “I've encountered some of the concepts, but I wouldn't claim advanced proficiency. It's an area where I'd welcome further training.”

#### Scenario 5 — An expectation is vague

Professor: “We expect a high level of commitment.”  
Student: “Could I ask what that means in practice—for example, in terms of project milestones or scheduled activities?”

#### Scenario 6 — You want to compare two options

Professor: “One project is mostly secondary analysis; another includes fieldwork.”  
Student: “I'd be interested in learning more about both. What would a student's role and training look like in each?”

#### Scenario 7 — They want an immediate agreement

Professor: “Would you be happy to take on that project?”  
Student: “It's definitely worth exploring. Before I commit, could I learn a little more about the exact responsibilities and available supervision?”

#### Scenario 8 — You hear a preference mistaken for a condition

Professor: “So you wouldn't do any data collection?”  
Student: “I should clarify: my experience and current preference lean toward data analysis. I'd like to understand the proposed data-collection tasks before making that decision.”

### I. Extended two-way prospective-supervisor conversation

**Professor:** “What are you hoping to gain from graduate research training?”  
**Student:** “I'd like to gain more experience developing a research question and carrying a project through analysis and interpretation. I've had some exposure to observational research, and I'd like to build on that.”  
**Professor:** “Are you looking for close supervision or a lot of independence?”  
**Student:** “A combination would work well for me. I can be independent with defined tasks, but I value feedback on major decisions.”  
**Professor:** “We expect students to find their own solutions most of the time.”  
**Student:** “I can see the value in that. What does the support structure look like if a student reaches a methodological problem they can't resolve alone?”  
**Professor:** “They would normally discuss it at our biweekly group meeting.”  
**Student:** “That sounds helpful. Would there also be opportunities for individual feedback?”  
**Professor:** “Occasionally, depending on the project stage.”  
**Student:** “I see. So the regular mechanism is the group meeting, with individual discussions arranged when needed. Is that right?”  
**Professor:** “Exactly. How do you feel about that arrangement?”  
**Student:** “It sounds workable in principle. I'd like to understand the expected timeline and the kind of project a new student would take on before making a final judgment.”  
**Professor:** “That's fair. Let's talk about the current projects.”  
**Student:** “Sure. I'd be interested in hearing what the first few months would involve.”

#### I1. What makes the exchange effective?

The student articulates specific goals, gives an accurate account of their experience, asks for concrete examples rather than accepting vague language, **paraphrases expectations**, and avoids promising to accept a project during a preliminary conversation.

#### I2. A branch where the expectations do not align

Professor: “My students usually work alone and receive detailed feedback only at the end.”  
Student: “Thank you for explaining. I think more frequent feedback during the early stages would be important for my development, so I'd want to consider carefully whether that arrangement would be the right fit.”

You can acknowledge a mismatch without accusing the professor of a poor approach.

### J. Functional phrase bank and core principles

| Function | Useful English | Note |
|---|---|---|
| State a goal | “I'd like to develop more independence in...” | Purpose |
| State a preference | “My preference would be...” | Flexible |
| Describe a condition | “I'd need to clarify...” | Decision-relevant |
| Ask about training | “What training would be available for...?” | Do not assume |
| Ask about expectations | “What would you typically expect from...?” | Professional |
| Ask for an example | “What would that look like in practice?” | Converts vague claims into specifics |
| Ask about support | “How do students usually get feedback on...?” | Factual |
| Express alignment | “That seems broadly aligned with...” | Provisional |
| Reserve judgment | “I'd like to understand the scope before deciding.” | Clear boundary |
| Confirm | “So my understanding is that... Is that right?” | Check interpretation |

**Core principles:**

1. Explain **what** you want to learn and **why**, using accurate personal evidence.
2. Treat independence and supervision as adjustable dimensions, not opposing identities.
3. Ask about observable practices: meetings, milestones, feedback and responsibilities.
4. Distinguish preference from essential condition, and interest from commitment.
5. Confirm key expectations in the conversation; Lesson 08 will cover written follow-up.
6. Do not assume that a hypothetical answer creates an admission or funding offer.
