# Lesson 07 · Expressing Expectations & Negotiating

## 7.3 Funding, Eligibility, and Admissions Process

**Learning objectives:** Ask about available funding without sounding presumptuous; distinguish tuition, scholarships, stipends, assistantships and project support; determine who has the authority to confirm admission; clarify entry requirements and contingencies; and respond appropriately to uncertain, partial or negative information.

**Important accuracy note:** Funding, visa/work rules, tuition classifications, admissions eligibility, research-degree availability and supervisor authority vary across **universities, programs, countries and funding schemes**. The conversations here teach the language of inquiry. They make **no factual claims about a particular institution's current policies**. Consult the program's official published rules and the responsible admissions/funding office for binding information.

### A. Why funding and admission are distinct questions

There are at least four decisions that may be made by different people:

1. **Scientific fit:** Is there a relevant project and potential supervisory interest?
2. **Supervisor capacity:** Is the supervisor able and permitted to take another student?
3. **Formal eligibility/admission:** Does the applicant meet the program requirements, and who makes the formal decision?
4. **Funding:** Can the applicant secure a scholarship, studentship, stipend or other support, and on what terms?

A positive answer to one question does not automatically settle the others.

**Useful distinction:**

> “It's encouraging to hear that my background may be a good fit. Could I also ask how formal admission and funding decisions are made?”

#### A1. Informal encouragement vs. formal offer

Professor: “I think you could be a good fit for the group.”  
This indicates interest, **not necessarily admission**.

Professor: “I'd be happy to support an application.”  
This indicates potential support, **not necessarily an admission or scholarship award**.

Admissions office: “We are pleased to offer you admission, subject to...”  
This may be a **conditional formal offer**; read the actual terms carefully.

### B. Funding vocabulary: learn what each term specifies

| Term | Working meaning | What to confirm |
|---|---|---|
| **tuition fees** | Charges for enrollment | Amount, classification and schedule |
| **scholarship** | Award providing financial support, often competitive | Eligibility, duration, covered costs |
| **studentship** | Common term in some systems for funded study/research opportunity | Whether fees, stipend or both are included |
| **stipend** | Regular living-support payment | Amount, frequency, duration, tax treatment if relevant |
| **assistantship** | Role involving teaching/research work, often with compensation/support | Duties, hours, funding and student status |
| **RA / research assistantship** | Research employment/assistant role or funded student appointment, depending on system | Contract type, duties and program compatibility |
| **TA / teaching assistantship** | Teaching-related work/support | Duties, required hours and eligibility |
| **fee waiver / tuition waiver** | Some/all tuition fees are waived | Exactly which fees and for how long |
| **research expenses** | Project costs: software, field travel, recruitment, etc. | Whether the student bears any cost |
| **living expenses** | Housing, food, transportation, etc. | Expected costs and support |
| **self-funded** | Student finances their studies without a specified award | Whether permitted and feasible |

Do not assume **“funded” = full tuition + living stipend + health insurance + travel**. Ask what the package covers.

#### B1. “Funded,” “fully funded,” and “funding available”

- “The project **has funding**.” — A project budget exists; may or may not cover a student's fees.
- “There **may be funding available**.” — Possibility, unconfirmed.
- “The position is **fully funded**.” — Sounds comprehensive, but ask for written details of coverage and duration.
- “You could **apply for a scholarship**.” — Eligibility/application possibility, not an award.

#### B2. “Cost of attendance” and “total cost”

Ask separate questions about **tuition**, **required university fees**, **living costs**, and **research-related costs**. Estimated costs are not necessarily actual bills or guaranteed expenses.

### C. Asking about funding naturally

#### C1. Strong first-meeting questions

- “Are there any funding opportunities associated with this degree?”
- “Does the group have any funded studentships available for the coming intake?”
- “Are students usually considered for department-wide scholarships?”
- “Would research master's students be eligible for the same funding schemes as doctoral students?”
- “Is funding decided by the supervisor, the department, or a separate competition?”

The last two questions require institution-specific answers, never assumptions.

#### C2. More specific follow-ups

- “Does the funding cover tuition fees, a living stipend, or both?”
- “What is the normal duration of the support?”
- “Would funding be renewable, and on what conditions?”
- “Would any teaching or research-assistant duties be involved?”
- “Is the funding linked to a particular project or supervisor?”
- “Are there separate deadlines for admission and scholarship applications?”
- “Are international applicants eligible for that funding scheme?”

#### C3. Asking without appearing entitled

> “I also want to understand the financial side of the program so I can assess whether it would be feasible. Could you tell me what funding routes students normally explore?”

There is no need to apologize for asking about affordability. A well-timed, clearly framed question is professional.

### D. Admission eligibility and authority

#### D1. Useful terms

- **Eligibility** — Whether you meet the stated requirements.
- **Admission / acceptance** — Decision allowing entry to a program.
- **Selection** — Evaluation and choice among candidates.
- **Shortlist** — Group of applicants chosen for further consideration.
- **Conditional offer** — An offer dependent on specified conditions.
- **Unconditional offer** — An offer without those stated outstanding admission conditions; other administrative requirements may remain.
- **Nomination** — A person or unit puts you forward for consideration.
- **Intake / cohort** — Group of students entering in a period.
- **Available position / capacity** — Potential room to supervise; not always a formally advertised job or funded opening.

#### D2. Ask about who makes the decision

- “Does a student need to secure a supervisor before applying?”
- “Is the supervisor's support required for admission?”
- “Does the department make the final selection decision?”
- “Would the application also be reviewed by a graduate admissions committee?”
- “If you thought there might be a fit, what would the formal next step be?”

Avoid assuming that a potential supervisor can personally issue an offer.

#### D3. Clarify degree routes rather than conflating them

- “Does this department offer an MPhil or another research-focused master's route?”
- “Would that program accept applicants directly from a bachelor's degree?”
- “Is there a separate application process for a PhD?”
- “Are transfer or progression arrangements officially available, or would a separate application be required?”

**MPhil, MRes, MSc by Research and PhD** do not have a globally uniform structure. Ask about a *specific program*, rather than interpreting the degree label alone.

### E. Discussing grades, language requirements and application readiness

#### E1. Questions about formal requirements

- “Could you point me to the official entry requirements for this program?”
- “Is there a minimum academic average or degree classification?”
- “Are there separate language requirements for admission and scholarship consideration?”
- “Are additional prerequisites required for applicants from related disciplines?”
- “Does the department consider applicants whose undergraduate curriculum is interdisciplinary?”

Use official documents to confirm; a faculty member's informal reassurance may not bind admissions staff.

#### E2. Asking about an apparent shortfall

Professor: “Your academic record may be below the usual threshold.”  
Student: “Thank you for pointing that out. Could I ask whether that is a formal minimum requirement or a typical profile? I'd like to check the official eligibility criteria before deciding how to proceed.”

A **hard minimum** and **typical competitiveness** are distinct. Do not ask the professor to guarantee an exception they cannot authorize.

#### E3. Talking about pending documents

- “My final transcript won't be available until [DATE]. Would a current transcript be acceptable at the application stage?”
- “I'm planning to take the language test again. Which scores and deadlines would apply?”
- “Would the degree certificate need to be submitted before enrollment?”
- “Is there an official contact who can confirm whether my current documents are sufficient?”

These examples use placeholders; do not supply fictional dates or scores in an actual conversation.

### F. Conditional funding and what to do when nothing is guaranteed

#### F1. Common conditional statements

| What you hear | What it may mean | Sensible response |
|---|---|---|
| “There might be a scholarship.” | Possibility | “When would decisions be announced?” |
| “I can nominate you.” | Willingness to recommend | “What would the nomination process involve?” |
| “We have funding for the project.” | Budget exists | “Would that funding include student support?” |
| “You could enroll without funding.” | Self-funded route may be possible | “What would the total tuition and expected costs be?” |
| “We won't know until spring.” | Decision is pending | “Is there an application I should submit in the meantime?” |

#### F2. Polite follow-up without pressure

> “I understand that funding may depend on a separate competition. Could you let me know which deadlines I should watch and whom I should contact for official details?”

#### F3. If there is no funding

> “Thanks for being clear about that. I'll need to consider the financial feasibility carefully. Is there an official page or office that lists other funding options for this degree?”

This response avoids falsely committing to self-funding.

### G. Language precision and common traps

#### G1. “Be eligible for” vs. “be entitled to”

- “I may **be eligible for** the scholarship.” — Meet conditions for consideration.
- “I am **entitled to** the payment.” — Have an established right to receive it; much stronger.

Eligibility does not imply selection.

#### G2. “Apply to” vs. “apply for”

- “Apply **to** the University of X.” — Institution.
- “Apply **for** an MPhil program / scholarship / position.” — Opportunity.
- “Apply **to** a program.” — Also idiomatic when the program is the admissions destination.

#### G3. “Offer,” “admission,” “nomination,” “funding”

- “I received an **offer of admission**.” — Admissions decision.
- “The supervisor **encouraged me to apply**.” — Interest, not an offer.
- “I was **nominated for a scholarship**.” — Nomination, not award.
- “I was **awarded a scholarship**.” — Actual award.

#### G4. “Would you be able to...” vs. “Could you guarantee...”

Asking whether someone can explain a process is different from asking them to guarantee an outcome:

> “Would you be able to explain how funding decisions are usually made?”

is safer and more informative early on than

> “Can you guarantee I'll be funded?”

A guarantee question may become appropriate only when negotiating a formal offer, and then it should be tied to written terms.

#### G5. What if you hear “We'll see”?

“We'll see” is deliberately noncommittal. You can ask for the **next procedural step** without forcing a yes/no:

> “Of course. Is there anything you'd recommend I submit or check while the decision is pending?”

### H. Unexpected funding and admission situations

#### Scenario 1 — Supervisor sounds enthusiastic

Professor: “I'd be happy to have someone with your interests in the group.”  
Student: “Thank you. Could I ask what the formal application route would be, and whether there's an available place for the coming intake?”

#### Scenario 2 — Funding is uncertain

Professor: “We might have a studentship.”  
Student: “I see. When is that expected to be confirmed, and would applicants need to submit a separate funding application?”

#### Scenario 3 — A department makes the final decision

Professor: “I don't make the admission decision myself.”  
Student: “Understood. Would your support be part of the application, or would it be reviewed independently by the department?”

#### Scenario 4 — Funding is offered for a different degree

Professor: “Our doctoral students may be funded, but I don't know about the master's route.”  
Student: “Thanks for clarifying. Do you know which office could confirm the funding options specifically for the research master's?”

#### Scenario 5 — An applicant is invited to self-fund

Professor: “You could consider coming as a self-funded student.”  
Student: “I appreciate you mentioning that option. I'd need to review the full costs and program conditions before deciding whether it would be feasible.”

#### Scenario 6 — Requirements seem inconsistent

Professor: “I believe the language requirement is [SCORE].”  
Student: “Thank you. I'll confirm it against the current official requirements, since I know they can differ by program and entry year.”

#### Scenario 7 — The scholarship includes duties

Professor: “The funding would involve assisting with teaching.”  
Student: “Could I ask how many hours would typically be expected and how those duties fit around thesis milestones?”

#### Scenario 8 — Admission is conditional

Admissions representative: “The offer would be conditional on final graduation results.”  
Student: “Could you confirm which documents and minimum results are required, and by what deadlines?”

#### Scenario 9 — You need an answer but the professor is not responsible

Professor: “I'm not sure about fee classifications for international students.”  
Student: “No problem. Could you recommend the right office to contact for a definitive answer?”

### I. Extended dialogue: interest, capacity, funding and formal admission

**Professor:** “Your interests seem relevant to one of our projects.”  
**Student:** “I'm glad to hear that. Would there be scope for a research master's student to get involved?”  
**Professor:** “Possibly. I have some capacity, but the funding situation is uncertain.”  
**Student:** “Thank you for explaining. Would any available funding come through your project, or through a separate university competition?”  
**Professor:** “Probably the university competition.”  
**Student:** “Would international applicants be eligible, and would the scholarship application have a separate deadline?”  
**Professor:** “I'm not entirely sure about the eligibility rules.”  
**Student:** “Understood. I'll check with the graduate admissions or scholarships office. Does the department require supervisor support before applicants apply?”  
**Professor:** “A potential supervisor should normally be identified, but admissions makes the formal decision.”  
**Student:** “That's helpful. If you think the fit is worth exploring further, would you recommend that I submit an application now or wait until the project details are clearer?”  
**Professor:** “I'd suggest reading the project outline and then applying if it still interests you.”  
**Student:** “That sounds sensible. Before I make any financial decisions, I'll also confirm tuition and scholarship conditions through the official channels.”  
**Professor:** “Good idea.”  
**Student:** “Thank you. Just to check, the next step is for me to read the outline and verify the application requirements; funding remains uncertain at this stage.”  
**Professor:** “That's correct.”

#### I1. The four separate threads

The student identifies potential **research fit**, possible **supervisor capacity**, the **formal admissions process**, and unresolved **funding eligibility**. The dialogue avoids treating encouragement as acceptance.

### J. Quick-reference questions and principles

| Goal | English |
|---|---|
| Start funding topic | “Could I ask how research students are typically funded?” |
| Clarify coverage | “Would that cover tuition, living expenses, or both?” |
| Clarify duration | “For how long would the funding be available?” |
| Ask about teaching duties | “Would any teaching responsibilities be involved?” |
| Ask about eligibility | “Would international applicants be eligible?” |
| Ask who decides | “Who makes the final admission decision?” |
| Ask about capacity | “Are you considering taking on new students for this intake?” |
| Ask about deadlines | “Is there a separate scholarship deadline?” |
| Clarify a condition | “Would the offer be conditional on anything?” |
| Avoid premature commitment | “I'd need to assess the financial feasibility before deciding.” |
| Confirm uncertainty | “So the funding remains unconfirmed at this stage?” |

**Core principles:** Funding is a reasonable topic. Ask what is covered, who decides, what conditions apply and when decisions happen. Keep research interest, supervision, admission and money logically separate. Verify current institutional rules and obtain formal written details before relying on them.
