# Research Motivation: Breadth, Interdisciplinarity, and Future Training

**Reusable expression bank**, suitable for prospective-supervisor meetings. Keep personal reflections and private correspondence out of the public repository.

## A. Describe the breadth of training

- “My undergraduate training spans basic medical sciences, clinical medicine, and public health.”
- “My degree combines biomedical foundations, clinical coursework, and public health training.”
- “That breadth sparked my interest in the intersection of clinical medicine and public health.”
- “I'm interested in how clinical questions can be investigated through epidemiological methods.”

**Span** = extend across; **spark an interest** = initiate interest; **intersection** = area where fields connect.

## B. Explain the postgraduate direction

- “I'd like to build deeper methodological expertise while maintaining an interdisciplinary perspective.”
- “I want to develop methodological depth without sacrificing interdisciplinary breadth.”
- “I'm interested in a program where research questions can cross disciplinary boundaries.”
- “I'm looking for an environment that supports both independent thinking and rigorous research training.”

The issue is the **fit of a specific training model**, not the assumption that any country or every institution has only one model.

## C. Discuss the concern about overly narrow specialization

| Expression | Nuance |
|---|---|
| “Become narrowly specialized” | Work in a limited area |
| “Get pigeonholed into a narrow field” | Feel restricted to a predefined role or niche |
| “Work within disciplinary silos” | Fields remain separate |
| “Lose sight of the bigger picture” | Miss broader connections |
| “Get caught up in one narrow research question” | Over-focus on one detail |
| “Bridge clinical medicine and public health” | Connect two fields |

Example: “I'm concerned about becoming so specialized that I lose sight of broader clinical and public health questions.”

Population-based research can itself be interdisciplinary; **narrow specialization** and **population-based research** are different ideas.

## D. Suggested response to “Why study abroad?”

“Well, there are both academic and personal reasons. My previous training has been quite broad, which is what drew me to research at the intersection of clinical medicine and public health. I'm now looking for more in-depth methodological training without giving up that interdisciplinary perspective. I'd also like to experience a different academic environment and explore opportunities internationally.”

When the discussion becomes more personal, the speaker can expand honestly at their own discretion.

## E. Adapt to specific questions

**Why a thesis-based MSc?**  
“I've gained experience with observational studies, and I'd now like more systematic training in research methodology through supervised, independent work.”

**Why a PhD eventually?**  
“I'm interested in developing the skills to formulate independent research questions, design rigorous studies, and interpret evidence responsibly.”

**Why this research group?**  
“I'm interested in understanding how your group connects clinical questions with methodological work, and whether that might provide a good environment for an MSc project.”

**Question to ask the professor:**  
“How much flexibility do MSc students typically have to pursue interdisciplinary questions connecting clinical medicine, epidemiology, and research methodology?”

## F. Natural collocations

- “My training **spans** several disciplines.”
- “That **sparked my interest** in...”
- “That's where my interests **lie**.”
- “I would like to **build on** that foundation.”
- “I want to **bridge** clinical questions and population-level evidence.”
- “I hope to **maintain breadth while developing depth**.”

**Authenticity note:** Choose examples that reflect your actual coursework, projects, and aspirations. Do not imply a level of clinical qualification or independent research responsibility you do not hold.
